Lesson 01Functions, units, and engineering magnitudes

This lesson makes every later calculation in the programme checkable. It does three things: it treats a unit as an algebraic object that travels through an equation exactly as a number does, it reads a function as a written record of how a machine behaves, and it insists that you estimate a magnitude before you trust an arithmetic result. The habit it builds — substitute units before numbers — removes a large share of engineering calculation errors before they can be made.

Key points

  • A physical quantity is a number and a unit; the unit obeys algebra, so it can be cancelled, multiplied and raised to powers.
  • Dimensional homogeneity: both sides of a physical equation must carry identical dimensions. An equation that fails this is wrong before any number is substituted.
  • The arguments of \( \sin \), \( \ln \) and \( e^x \) must be dimensionless — there is no such thing as the logarithm of a metre.
  • A function records machine behaviour: a pump curve is head as a function of flow, written \( H(Q) \).
  • A domain is a physical statement, not a formality. Beyond a pump's runout flow the curve is forbidden, not merely undefined.
  • Convert every input to base SI before any arithmetic, and convert back only at the end.
  • Estimate first. If a suitcase-sized transfer pump computes to 300 kW, the arithmetic is wrong and you know it without re-checking.

Units are algebra, not decoration

Every physical quantity is a number together with a unit, and the unit is not a label attached afterwards. It is an algebraic object that multiplies, divides and cancels exactly as a number does. Writing 45 cubic metres per hour as \( 45\ \mathrm{m^3/h} \) means literally 45 multiplied by cubic metres divided by hours, and every later step may treat it that way.

That gives the single most useful check in engineering calculation. The principle is called dimensional homogeneity, written \( [\mathrm{LHS}] = [\mathrm{RHS}] \) and read as ‘the dimensions of the left-hand side equal the dimensions of the right-hand side’. Both sides of any physical equation must carry identical dimensions. If they do not, the equation is wrong, and you know it before substituting a single number.

Take hydraulic power, the power delivered to a fluid, written \( P = \rho g Q H \) and read as ‘P equals rho g Q H’. Here the density, written rho \( (\rho) \), is mass per unit volume; the gravitational acceleration is \( g \); the volumetric flow rate is \( Q \); and the head is \( H \). Substitute dimensions rather than values, using M for mass, L for length and T for time:

\[ \frac{M}{L^3}\cdot\frac{L}{T^2}\cdot\frac{L^3}{T}\cdot L = \frac{M L^2}{T^3} \]

which is the dimension of a watt. The equation survives the check, so it is at least dimensionally possible. This is a necessary condition and not a sufficient one: a wrong constant in front of a correct grouping passes it. What it reliably catches is a dropped term, a mis-transcribed exponent, or a variable substituted into the wrong slot.

One consequence is worth stating on its own. The argument of a transcendental function must be dimensionless. There is no such thing as \( \ln \) of a metre. Whenever such a function appears, whatever sits inside it is a ratio, and identifying that ratio usually explains the physics.

A function is a machine's behaviour, written down

In service you rarely meet a function as an abstract rule. You meet it as a curve on a datasheet: the head a pump produces at each flow, the torque a motor produces at each speed, the temperature of a bearing against time after start-up. The function notation \( H(Q) \), read ‘H of Q’, simply names that relationship — the head that corresponds to a given flow.

Two things follow, and both matter more in practice than the algebra does. The first is that the domain is a physical statement. A pump curve is drawn between shut-off and runout, and outside that interval the relationship is not merely undefined in the mathematical sense — it is forbidden in the engineering sense. Reading a value off an extrapolated curve is how equipment gets specified into cavitation.

The second is that the shape carries the engineering. A curve that falls steeply with flow behaves differently in a system than one that is nearly flat, and the difference decides whether two pumps can be run in parallel, how a control valve behaves, and how sensitive the duty point is to a fouled strainer. You will meet the formal machinery for describing shape — slope, curvature, maxima — in later lessons. For now the discipline is to read a curve as a statement about a machine rather than as a picture.

Note the notation clash you will meet everywhere: \( P \) is used for power and \( p \) for pressure. They are different quantities with different dimensions, and the only defence is to state which you mean the first time you use it in any calculation.

Magnitude first, arithmetic second

An answer that is wrong by a factor of a thousand is far commoner than one that is wrong by ten per cent, because the usual cause is not faulty arithmetic but a unit left unconverted. A flow rate given in cubic metres per hour and used as though it were cubic metres per second is wrong by exactly 3600, and the arithmetic that follows it is flawless.

The protection is an estimate formed before the calculation. Small process pumps are kilowatt machines; a transfer pump you could carry is not a 300 kW machine, and any route to that number is wrong. Holding a rough expectation converts a silent error into an obvious one.

The working method, used from here on.

  • One. Write the equation and check its dimensions before any number goes in.
  • Two. Convert every input to base SI — metres, kilograms, seconds, pascals, watts. Do this in one place, visibly.
  • Three. Estimate the answer to within a factor of ten, and write the estimate down.
  • Four. Do the arithmetic, carrying units through it.
  • Five. Compare against the estimate, then convert to whatever unit the reader expects — kilowatts, bar, metres of water.

Step five matters as much as step two. A result is communicated to someone holding a gauge in bar or a datasheet in cubic metres per hour, and an answer left in base SI transfers the conversion risk to them.

Hydraulic power, and the trap in the flow unit

Problem. A pump delivers \( Q = 45\ \mathrm{m^3/h} \) of water against a head of \( H = 32\ \mathrm{m} \). Take the density as \( \rho = 998\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} \) and \( g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \). Find the hydraulic power delivered to the water.

Approach. Use \( P = \rho g Q H \). The whole difficulty is in the units, not the algebra: the equation returns watts only when every input is in base SI, and flow on a datasheet is almost always quoted in cubic metres per hour. Convert the flow first, in its own visible step, because doing it silently inside the arithmetic is exactly how the factor of 3600 goes missing. Estimate before calculating: a few tens of cubic metres per hour against a few tens of metres of head is a small pump, so expect single-figure kilowatts.

Solution.

Step 1 — convert the flow to base SI.
\( Q = 45/3600 = 0.0125\ \mathrm{m^3/s} \).

Step 2 — group the constants.
\( \rho g = 998 \times 9.81 = 9790\ \mathrm{kg/(m^2 s^2)} \).

Step 3 — multiply through.
\( P = 9790 \times 0.0125 \times 32 = 3916\ \mathrm{W} \).

Step 4 — compare with the estimate and report.
3.92 kW is single-figure kilowatts, as expected.

Answer. \( P = 3.92\ \mathrm{kW} \).

The trap. Leaving \( Q \) at 45 gives 14 098 kW — a 14-megawatt transfer pump. The arithmetic is perfect and the answer is wrong by exactly 3600. The estimate in the Approach is what catches it.

MTH 101 · Lesson 01 · Functions, units, and engineering magnitudes · worked example 2

Pressure head, in SI and in customary units

Problem. (a) A gauge reads 1 bar. Express this as a head of water. (b) A datasheet from a supplier quotes 30 psi. Express that as a head of water in both metres and feet. Use \( \rho = 998\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} \), \( g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \), and \( 1\ \mathrm{psi} = 6894.76\ \mathrm{Pa} \).

Approach. Pressure head is \( h = p/(\rho g) \), read ‘h equals p divided by rho g’, where the pressure is written lower-case \( p \) to keep it distinct from power. Convert each pressure to pascals first; the equation returns metres only from pascals. Part (b) is included because supplier data does not arrive in SI, and converting at the boundary — on the way in, and again on the way out — is the habit that keeps the middle of a calculation clean.

Solution.

(a) Step 1. \( 1\ \mathrm{bar} = 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa} \).
Step 2. \( h = 10^5/(998 \times 9.81) = 10^5/9790 = 10.21\ \mathrm{m} \).

(b) Step 1. \( p = 30 \times 6894.76 = 206\,843\ \mathrm{Pa} \).
Step 2. \( h = 206\,843/9790 = 21.13\ \mathrm{m} \).
Step 3. \( 21.13/0.3048 = 69.3\ \mathrm{ft} \).

Answer. (a) 10.21 m of water. (b) 21.13 m, or 69.3 ft.

Worth remembering. One bar is very nearly 10.2 m of water, so a pressure in bar can be turned into an approximate head by multiplying by 10. That is a useful field estimate and a good check on part (b): 30 psi is about 2.07 bar, so roughly 21 m — which is what the full calculation returned.
