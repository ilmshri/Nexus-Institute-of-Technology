Mechanisms and mobility

Links, joints, and kinematic chains; Grübler's mobility count. Four-bar and slider-crank as the industrial workhorses. Inversions and what each one becomes.

المحاضرة

§1 · Links, joints, and the chain they make

Machinery is not made of forces first. It is made of rigid bodies connected so that only certain motions survive, and the forces follow from the motion that is left. A link is one of those rigid bodies; a joint, or kinematic pair, is a connection that removes some of the freedom between two links. Connect links in a closed loop and you have a kinematic chain; fix one link to the earth and the chain becomes a mechanism.

A free rigid body in a plane has three degrees of freedom — two translations and a rotation. Joints are graded by how much of that they take away. A pin (revolute) and a slider (prismatic) each leave exactly one relative freedom, so each removes two; they are called lower pairs because they touch over a surface. A cam pressed against a follower or a gear tooth pressed against another touches along a line, leaves two freedoms — rolling and sliding along the contact — and so removes only one. Those are higher pairs. The distinction is not cosmetic: it is the whole difference between the two terms in the count that follows.

§2 · Mobility, counted before anything is drawn

Add up what the links bring and subtract what the joints take. With \( N \) links one is grounded, so \( 3(N-1) \) freedoms enter; each lower pair removes two and each higher pair removes one.

Key relation — Kutzbach mobility criterion

\[ M = 3(N-1) - 2j_1 - j_2 \]

\( N \) counts links including the fixed one, \( j_1 \) counts lower pairs (pins and sliders) and \( j_2 \) counts higher pairs (cam and gear contacts). \( M \) is the number of inputs the mechanism needs before its geometry is fully determined.

For the four-bar in the figure, \( N=4 \) and \( j_1=4 \), so \( M = 3(3) - 8 = 1 \). One motor, one position — which is why the four-bar is everywhere. A slider-crank counts identically: four links again, three pins and one slider, \( M=1 \). A cam driving a translating follower has three links, two lower pairs and one higher pair: \( M = 3(2) - 4 - 1 = 1 \).

Read the other answers as verdicts, not failures. \( M = 0 \) is a structure — it does not move, which is exactly what a bracket should do. \( M < 0 \) is a statically indeterminate structure, over-constrained. \( M = 2 \) needs two inputs, like a robot arm with two motors. The count costs nothing and is done before geometry, which makes it the cheapest design check in the subject.

It has one honest limitation. Kutzbach counts joints, not where they are, so a chain with special proportions can move when the count says it cannot. A parallelogram linkage with a redundant fifth link duplicating an existing one counts as \( M = 0 \) yet runs perfectly, because the extra link is exactly parallel to the one it shadows — locomotive coupling rods work this way. A count of zero on a mechanism that visibly moves means the geometry is special, and special geometry is fragile: build that same linkage with real tolerances and it binds.

§3 · Grashof: which link is allowed a full turn

Mobility says a four-bar needs one input. It does not say that input can be a motor. For a link to be driven by a continuously rotating shaft it must be able to pass through every angle, and a four-bar only permits that for certain proportions.

Key relation — Grashof condition

\[ s + \ell \le p + q \]

\( s \) is the shortest link, \( \ell \) the longest, and \( p,q \) the other two. If the inequality holds, the shortest link can rotate fully relative to all the others; if it fails, no link can, and every link merely rocks.

Satisfying Grashof tells you a full rotation exists somewhere in the chain. Which motion you get depends on where the shortest link sits. Ground the link adjacent to the shortest and you get a crank-rocker: the short link spins, the far link oscillates — the wiper linkage, the oscillating conveyor, the classic rotation-to-oscillation converter. Ground the shortest link itself and both side links rotate fully: a double-crank, or drag-link, used where two shafts must both turn but at a deliberately uneven rate. Ground the link opposite the shortest and both side links rock while the coupler makes the full turn: a double-rocker. Fail the inequality and every inversion is a triple-rocker — useful for guided motion, useless for a motor.

The figure's linkage gives \( 40+120 = 160 \le 100+90 = 190 \). It is Grashof, the shortest link is the crank, and the crank is adjacent to ground, so it is a crank-rocker and a motor can drive it.

§4 · Inversions, and the angle that decides whether it is any good

Fixing a different link of the same chain produces an inversion. The relative motion between links is untouched — only the observer's anchor moves — yet the machine that results can be unrecognisable. The slider-crank makes the point better than any other chain. Ground the block and you have a reciprocating engine or compressor. Ground the crank and the cylinder swings: the oscillating-cylinder engine, and the Whitworth quick-return that gives a shaper its fast idle stroke. Ground the connecting rod and you get the hand pump. Four machines, one chain, one set of relative motions.

What separates a working linkage from a nominally correct one is the transmission angle \( \mu \), the angle between the coupler and the driven link. The force in the coupler is a push along its own line; only the component perpendicular to the driven link produces torque, and that component goes as \( \sin\mu \). At \( \mu = 90^\circ \) the transfer is perfect. As \( \mu \) approaches \( 0^\circ \) or \( 180^\circ \) the coupler force — the push the coupler carries along its own line, which Lesson 7 puts a number on — turns into bearing load and heat, the mechanism becomes sensitive to friction, and it can stall. Practice keeps \( \mu \ge 40^\circ \) through the whole cycle.

Apply it to the figure. At \( \theta_2 = 60^\circ \), \( \mu = 46.4^\circ \) — acceptable. But \( \mu \) is worst when the crank points straight at the far pivot, and there \( \mu = 29.0^\circ \). This linkage is Grashof, has \( M=1 \), and is still a poor design: once per revolution it works through an angle no designer would sign off. Shortening the crank from 40 to 25 mm lifts the worst case to 38.6°. Mobility and Grashof are gates; the transmission angle is the grade.

الأسس

What this lesson assumes

  • Rigid-body kinematics (Dynamics L1) — a planar rigid body has three degrees of freedom, the fact the whole mobility count is built on.
  • Free-body reasoning (Statics L2) — a two-force member carries load along its own line, which is why the transmission angle matters.
  • Vector geometry (Mathematics I) — the cosine rule, used here to find the transmission angle from link lengths alone.
  • Constraint counting (Statics L11) — the same subtract-what-is-removed argument that decides static determinacy decides mobility.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Mobility of four chains

Problem. Count the mobility of each planar chain and say what it is. (a) A four-bar: 4 links, 4 pins. (b) A cam driving a translating roller follower: 3 links, 2 lower pairs, 1 higher pair. (c) A Watt six-bar: 6 links, 7 pins. (d) A proposed bracing linkage: 5 links, 6 pins.

Solution. Use \( M = 3(N-1) - 2j_1 - j_2 \) throughout.

(a) \( M = 3(4-1) - 2(4) - 0 = 9 - 8 = 1 \). One input, one configuration — a mechanism.

(b) \( M = 3(3-1) - 2(2) - 1 = 6 - 4 - 1 = 1 \). The higher pair removes only one freedom, which is exactly what leaves the follower free to be positioned by the cam.

(c) \( M = 3(6-1) - 2(7) = 15 - 14 = 1 \). Six-bars are built to keep \( M=1 \) while giving the designer more coupler curves than a four-bar can.

(d) \( M = 3(5-1) - 2(6) = 12 - 12 = 0 \). Not a mechanism — a structure.

Answer: (a) 1, (b) 1, (c) 1, (d) 0. Sanity check: only (d) differs, and it should — it was proposed as bracing. Had the answer come out \( -1 \) the bracing would still stand, but it would be over-constrained and would fight thermal expansion. Note also that (b) would have counted 0 if the cam contact had been treated as a lower pair; the one-freedom difference between line contact and surface contact is the entire result.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Grashof classification in US customary units

Problem. Three four-bars are specified as (ground, crank, coupler, rocker) in inches: (a) (8, 2, 7, 5); (b) (3, 8, 7, 6); (c) (7, 6, 2.5, 8). For each, test the Grashof condition and name the mechanism obtained with the stated link grounded.

Solution. The condition \( s+\ell \le p+q \) is a comparison of lengths, so it is dimensionless and inches serve as well as millimetres — no conversion is needed.

(a) Sorted: 2, 5, 7, 8. \( s+\ell = 2+8 = 10 \le 5+7 = 12 \) ✓. The shortest link (2 in) is the crank, which is adjacent to the fixed ground link → crank-rocker.

(b) Sorted: 3, 6, 7, 8. \( s+\ell = 3+8 = 11 \le 6+7 = 13 \) ✓. The shortest link (3 in) is the ground itself → double-crank (drag-link): both side links turn fully.

(c) Sorted: 2.5, 6, 7, 8. \( s+\ell = 2.5+8 = 10.5 \le 6+7 = 13 \) ✓. The shortest link (2.5 in) is the coupler → double-rocker: both side links rock and the coupler makes the full revolution.

Answer: all three are Grashof; (a) crank-rocker, (b) drag-link, (c) double-rocker. Sanity check: the three cases share one chain type and differ only in which link is grounded — they are inversions, and they demonstrate that passing Grashof tells you a full rotation exists but not who performs it. A fourth set, (10, 4, 6, 5), gives \( 4+10 = 14 > 6+5 = 11 \): non-Grashof, so every inversion is a triple-rocker and none of them may be motor-driven.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Transmission angle over a full revolution

Problem. For the linkage in the figure — ground 100 mm, crank 40 mm, coupler 120 mm, rocker 90 mm — find the transmission angle at \( \theta_2 = 60^\circ \), then at the two crank positions that give its extremes, and judge the design. Use \( \cos\mu = (r_3^2+r_4^2-d^2)/(2r_3r_4) \), where \( d \) is the distance from the moving crank pin A to the fixed pivot \( O_4 \).

Solution. (1) At \( \theta_2 = 60^\circ \), A sits at \( (40\cos 60^\circ,\ 40\sin 60^\circ) = (20.00,\ 34.64) \) mm, so \( d = \sqrt{(100-20)^2 + 34.64^2} = \sqrt{6400+1200} = 87.18\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(2) \( \cos\mu = \frac{120^2+90^2-87.18^2}{2(120)(90)} = \frac{14400+8100-7600}{21600} = 0.6898 \), so \( \mu = 46.4^\circ \).

(3) \( \mu \) is monotonic in \( d \), and \( d \) is extreme when the crank lies along the ground line. At \( \theta_2 = 0^\circ \), \( d = 100-40 = 60\ \mathrm{mm} \): \( \cos\mu = (14400+8100-3600)/21600 = 0.8750 \Rightarrow \mu = 29.0^\circ \).

(4) At \( \theta_2 = 180^\circ \), \( d = 100+40 = 140\ \mathrm{mm} \): \( \cos\mu = (14400+8100-19600)/21600 = 0.1343 \Rightarrow \mu = 82.3^\circ \).

Answer: \( \mu = 46.4^\circ \) at 60°, ranging from 29.0° to 82.3° over the revolution. The minimum is well below the 40° guideline, so the design is rejected. Sanity check: the fix follows from the same arithmetic — the extremes come from \( d = r_1 \mp r_2 \), so shrinking the crank narrows the swing of \( d \). Recomputing with a 25 mm crank gives \( d = 75 \) to 125 mm and \( \mu_{\min} = 38.6^\circ \), nearly acceptable, at the cost of the rocker stroke the crank was sized to produce. That trade — stroke against transmission angle — is the whole of four-bar design.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

A free rigid body in plane motion has how many degrees of freedom?

Two translations and one rotation. That is why the mobility count opens with \( 3(N-1) \): every link but the grounded one brings three freedoms into the chain before any joint takes them away. Six is the answer in three dimensions, and using it by mistake in a planar count is the most common way to get a nonsense mobility.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

A cam pressed against a follower is a higher pair because the contact

Higher and lower refer to the geometry of contact, not the load. A cam and its follower touch along a line and can both roll and slide there, so two relative freedoms survive and only one is removed — the \( j_2 \) term. A pin or slider touches over a surface, leaves one freedom, and removes two. Grading the pair wrongly changes the mobility by exactly one, which is usually the difference between a mechanism and a structure.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A planar chain is counted and gives \( M = 0 \). It is

Zero mobility means no motion is available: the chain is a structure, and if it was meant to be a bracket that is the right answer. Over-constraint shows up as \( M < 0 \), where the chain would still not move but would also fight its own tolerances and thermal growth. The one caution is that Kutzbach counts joints and not their positions, so a chain with special proportions — parallel links, or a redundant link shadowing another — can move despite counting zero.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

A four-bar has links 30, 90, 70 and 60 mm. Is it Grashof?

Sort the lengths: \( s=30 \), \( \ell=90 \), and the intermediates are 60 and 70. \( s+\ell = 120 \le 60+70 = 130 \), so the condition holds. Grounding does not enter the test at all — it decides which inversion you get (crank-rocker, drag-link or double-rocker) once the test has already been passed.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Grounding the shortest link of a Grashof four-bar produces a

With the shortest link fixed, both links attached to it can complete full revolutions, giving the drag-link used where two shafts must both turn but at a deliberately uneven rate through the cycle. Grounding a link adjacent to the shortest gives the crank-rocker; grounding the link opposite gives the double-rocker, where the coupler makes the full turn. A triple-rocker is what every inversion becomes when the Grashof test fails.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Two mechanisms are inversions of the same kinematic chain. What is identical between them?

An inversion changes which link is held to the earth, so the relative motion between any two links is untouched — that is the definition. Everything measured against ground does change: absolute paths, velocities and inertia forces are all different, which is why a slider-crank becomes an engine, a quick-return shaper or a hand pump depending on what is bolted down.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

The force a coupler delivers to the driven link goes as

The coupler is a two-force member, so it pushes along its own line. Resolve that push at the driven link: the component perpendicular to the link, which is the only one that makes torque, is proportional to \( \sin\mu \). At \( \mu = 90^\circ \) all of it is useful; at \( \mu = 30^\circ \) half of it is, and the rest becomes bearing load. That is why practice sets a floor of about 40° on the worst \( \mu \) in the cycle.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A four-bar passes Grashof and has \( M = 1 \), but its transmission angle falls to 22° once per revolution. The correct verdict is

Mobility and Grashof are gates, not grades: they say a full rotation exists and one input controls it, and say nothing about how well force is passed. At 22° only \( \sin 22^\circ = 0.37 \) of the coupler force does useful work while the rest loads the bearings, so friction and load variation can stall the mechanism there. Doubling the motor torque drives the joint loads up with it rather than fixing the geometry; the real fix is to change the proportions, usually by shortening the crank relative to the ground link.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the bracket that was moved 40 mm. An oscillating screen drive used a crank-rocker: ground 100 mm between the two fixed pivots, 40 mm crank, 120 mm coupler, 90 mm rocker — \( 40+120 = 160 \le 100+90 = 190 \), Grashof, motor-driven for years. During a plant modification a new pipe run fouled the motor pedestal, and the pedestal was moved 40 mm toward the rocker pivot. Nothing else was touched: same motor, same crank, same coupler, same rocker, same fasteners. The drive then stalled on start-up in some positions and ran in others, and the motor tripped on overload.

The ground link is a link. Shortening it from 100 to 60 mm changed the set to (60, 40, 120, 90): the shortest is still 40 and the longest still 120, but the other two now sum to \( 60+90 = 150 \), and \( 160 > 150 \). The linkage had become non-Grashof, and a non-Grashof four-bar has no fully rotating link at all. Checking the geometry angle by angle confirms it: the chain assembles at 307 of 360 crank angles and cannot be assembled at all within \( 26.4^\circ \) either side of the crank pointing at the far pivot. The motor was being asked to drive the crank into a position the linkage geometrically cannot occupy, so whether it started depended on where it had stopped.

Two practices follow. A pivot location is a design dimension with a tolerance, not a mounting convenience — the ground link deserves the same drawing control as the parts that visibly move. And any change to a linkage, including one that moves no moving part, is re-tested through Grashof and through the worst transmission angle before it is fitted; both tests are arithmetic on four numbers and take a minute.
