KDM 252 · YEAR 2 · SEMESTER 2

Kinematics & Dynamics of Machinery

Mechanisms in motion: linkages, cams, gear geometry and trains, flywheels, and machine balancing — how rotation becomes useful motion, and where the shaking forces that vibration analysts chase are born.

Career outlook — where this course pays

Kinematics & Dynamics of Machinery is the course that decides whether a machine runs quietly or destroys its own bearings, and three sectors hire directly on it:

  • Balancing and vibration services — field engineers at balancing-machine manufacturers such as Schenck RoTec live in Lesson 10's arithmetic: the two-plane conditions, the influence coefficients that replace known masses with measured response, and the judgement that a correction improving one bearing while worsening the other is a couple nobody cancelled.
  • Packaging and filling machinery — design engineers at machine builders such as Tetra Pak work Lesson 4 continuously, because a filling line is a cam machine: the motion law sets the follower's acceleration, the spring holds the higher pair together, and the rate the line can be pushed to is limited by the jump condition rather than by the motor.
  • Industrial gearboxes — application engineers at gear specialists such as David Brown Santasalo apply Lessons 5 and 6 as one subject: tooth geometry sets the contact ratio and the interference limit, the train equation sets the ratio (with the epicyclic +1 that catches the unwary), and both together predict the mesh frequencies a condition-monitoring report will name.

Interview angle: the standard probe is a planetary ratio asked casually — "sun 20, ring 60, ring held, what's the reduction?" — where 3:1 is the wrong answer and 4:1 is the right one, and the follow-up is which member you would hold to get a different ratio from the same hardware. The second favourite is a vibration spectrum with a line at neither shaft speed: the expected move is to compute the mesh frequencies before touching the machine.
