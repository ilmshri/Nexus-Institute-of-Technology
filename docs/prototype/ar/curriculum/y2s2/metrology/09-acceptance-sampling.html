Acceptance sampling

Sampling plans, OC curves, and the risks each party carries. AQL semantics without the folklore. Where sampling makes sense and where 100% inspection is owed.

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:

§1 · The plan, and the curve that is the plan

A lot arrives. Inspecting all of it may be impossible, uneconomic, or destructive, so a single sampling plan takes \( n \) parts at random and accepts the lot if the number of defectives \( d \) is at most the acceptance number \( c \). Two integers, and the whole behaviour of the scheme follows from them.

Key relation — the operating-characteristic curve

\[ P_a(p)=\sum_{d=0}^{c}\binom{n}{d}p^{\,d}(1-p)^{\,n-d} \]

The probability of accepting a lot whose true fraction defective is \( p \). Plotted against \( p \) this is the OC curve, and it is a complete description of the plan — every other property quoted about a plan is a point read off it.

For \( n=125 \), \( c=3 \): a lot at 1% defective is accepted 96.3% of the time, at 2% it is accepted 75.9%, at 3% 48.1%, and at 5% 12.4%. Notice what the curve is not. It is not a step. There is no fraction defective below which acceptance is certain and above which rejection is certain, because the plan sees only a sample and a sample is a random draw. Every plan that inspects fewer than all the parts has a sloping curve, and every sloping curve means both parties carry a risk.

§2 · The two risks, which are the two errors of a test

Those risks have names, and they are the familiar pair from hypothesis testing wearing work clothes.

Key definitions — the two risks

\[ \alpha=1-P_a(\mathrm{AQL})\quad\text{(producer's risk)},\qquad \beta=P_a(\mathrm{LTPD})\quad\text{(consumer's risk)} \]

\( \alpha \) is the chance a good lot is rejected — a type I error, and the supplier pays for it. \( \beta \) is the chance a bad lot is accepted — a type II error, and the customer pays for it. Conventionally \( \alpha=0.05 \) and \( \beta=0.10 \).

Reading the reference plan's curve at those two probabilities gives its two defining qualities. It accepts 95% of lots at \( p=1.10\% \), so 1.10% is the acceptable quality level for this plan; and it accepts 10% of lots at \( p=5.27\% \), so 5.27% is the lot tolerance percent defective. The ratio of the two, \( 5.27/1.10=4.79 \), is the plan's discrimination ratio — how much worse a lot has to be before the plan reliably notices.

Only one thing improves that ratio, and it is not the acceptance number. Compare four plans, each chosen to hold roughly the same AQL: \( n=50,c=1 \) discriminates at 10.56; \( n=125,c=3 \) at 4.79; \( n=200,c=5 \) at 3.49; \( n=500,c=12 \) at 2.29. Sample size buys discrimination, and nothing else does.

Which disposes of the most durable piece of folklore in the subject: that a sample should be some percentage of the lot. It should not, because the OC curve barely depends on lot size at all. Taking 125 parts from a lot of 1,000 that is 3% defective accepts it with probability 0.4713; taking the same 125 from a lot of 100,000 at the same 3% accepts with probability 0.4813. The lot grew a hundredfold and the plan's behaviour moved by one point in a hundred. A plan is defined by \( n \) and \( c \); "10% of the lot" is a rule that inspects far too many small lots and far too few large ones.

§3 · What an AQL does not mean

The AQL is the most misread number in quality, in two distinct ways, and both are worth stating as flat corrections.

It is not a target, and it is not permission. The AQL is a property of the plan — the quality it happens to accept 95% of the time. It is not an agreement that the supplier may run at that level. A supplier who does run at exactly the AQL will have one lot in twenty rejected, and across twenty consecutive lots the chance of at least one rejection is \( 1-0.95^{20}=64\% \). Sitting on the AQL is not a comfortable place to be; it is the edge of the plan's tolerance for you.

An accepted lot is not a good lot. Acceptance is a decision about the lot, not a certificate about its parts. A lot accepted at \( p=3\% \) still contains 3% defectives, all of which proceed into assembly. Sampling never removes defectives from an accepted lot; it only decides whether the lot goes forward.

What sampling can bound is the long-run average, provided rejected lots are screened rather than returned unchanged.

Key relation — average outgoing quality

\[ \mathrm{AOQ}(p)\approx p\,P_a(p),\qquad \mathrm{AOQL}=\max_p\ \mathrm{AOQ}(p) \]

Accepted lots pass at \( p \); rejected lots are 100%-inspected and their defectives replaced, so they pass at nearly zero. The product has a maximum — incoming quality that is very good produces little outgoing defect, and incoming quality that is very bad is almost always rejected and rectified.

For the reference plan the maximum is \( \mathrm{AOQL}=1.55\% \), occurring at an incoming level of 2.34%. That is a genuine guarantee with a genuine condition: however bad the supplier's process becomes, long-run outgoing quality cannot exceed 1.55% defective — if every rejected lot is really screened and rectified. Where rejected lots are returned to the supplier and shipped back untouched, the AOQL is not a bound on anything.

§4 · When to sample, and when 100% inspection is owed

The economic answer is sharper than most people expect, and it says that sampling is usually the wrong choice.

Key relation — the all-or-none rule

\[ p^{*}=\frac{k_1}{k_2}:\qquad pp^{*}\Rightarrow\text{inspect all} \]

\( k_1 \) is the cost of inspecting one part and \( k_2 \) the cost of one defective passing into production. For a stable process at known \( p \), the minimum-cost policy is one of the two extremes; partial sampling costs more than the better of them.

With inspection at 0.40 per part and an escape costing 55, the break-even is \( p^{*}=0.40/55=0.73\% \). A process running at 0.2% defective costs \( 0.002\times55=0.11 \) per part if nothing is inspected, against 0.40 per part to screen: inspect nothing. The same process drifted to 2% costs \( 1.10 \) per part unscreened: inspect everything. There is no value of \( p \) at which checking one part in eight is the cheapest policy.

So why does acceptance sampling exist at all? Because the rule's premise — a stable process at known \( p \) — is exactly what a receiving inspector does not have. Lots arrive from a supplier whose process state is invisible, may be stable or not, and may differ lot to lot. Sampling is the rational hedge under that ignorance, and it is also a contractual instrument: it gives both sides a defined, auditable rule. Its right home is incoming inspection from an unverified source, and its natural replacement is evidence — a supplier who can produce control charts and capability studies has told you \( p \), and once \( p \) is known the all-or-none rule applies.

Three situations override the arithmetic entirely. Where the test is destructive, sampling is not a choice but the only possibility, and the plan's discrimination has to be paid for in scrapped parts. Where the consequence of an escape is a safety event, no escape rate is acceptable and the answer is 100% inspection with redundancy, or a design that removes the failure mode. And where the process is not in control, \( p \) is not a number, so no policy derived from it is valid until stability is restored.

One honest qualification belongs with all of this. "100% inspection" is a name, not a guarantee: attribute inspection typically detects 80–95% of the defectives present, so a 2% incoming rate screened at 90% efficiency still passes 0.2%. A second pass takes it to 0.02% and a third to 0.002%, which is why critical characteristics get redundant checks rather than one careful one. The instrument, the inspector and the plan are all part of the same measuring system, and none of them is perfect.

الأسس

What this lesson assumes

  • The binomial distribution (Engineering Statistics L3) — the OC curve is a binomial tail evaluated at every \( p \).
  • Probability rules and independence (Engineering Statistics L2) — the \( 1-0.95^{20} \) argument, and the escape rate through repeated inspection.
  • Hypothesis testing, type I and type II errors (Engineering Statistics L7) — producer's and consumer's risk are exactly \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \).
  • Attribute inspection (Lesson 5) — a sampling plan counts pass/fail verdicts, with all the information loss that implies.
  • Fraction nonconforming and stability (Lessons 7 and 8) — the all-or-none rule assumes a process in control at a known \( p \).

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Read a plan off its own curve

Problem. A single sampling plan takes \( n=125 \) and accepts on \( c=3 \). Give the probability of accepting lots at 1%, 3% and 5% defective. The plan's AQL (the quality accepted 95% of the time) is 1.10% and its LTPD (accepted 10% of the time) is 5.27%; state the producer's and consumer's risks and the discrimination ratio. Then find the probability that a supplier running at exactly the AQL has at least one of twenty consecutive lots rejected.

Solution. (1) \( P_a(p)=\sum_{d=0}^{3}\binom{125}{d}p^d(1-p)^{125-d} \).

(2) At \( p=0.01 \): the four terms are 0.2847, 0.3595, 0.2251 and 0.0932, giving \( P_a=0.963 \).

(3) At \( p=0.03 \): \( P_a=0.481 \). At \( p=0.05 \): \( P_a=0.124 \).

(4) Producer's risk \( \alpha=1-P_a(\mathrm{AQL})=1-0.95=0.05 \): one good lot in twenty is rejected. Consumer's risk \( \beta=P_a(\mathrm{LTPD})=0.10 \): one lot in ten at 5.27% defective is accepted and shipped.

(5) Discrimination ratio \( =5.27/1.10=4.79 \). A lot must be nearly five times worse than the AQL before the plan rejects it reliably.

(6) Lots are independent, so \( P=1-0.95^{20}=1-0.3585=0.642 \).

Answer: \( P_a \) = 0.963, 0.481, 0.124 at 1%, 3% and 5%; \( \alpha=0.05 \), \( \beta=0.10 \), discrimination ratio 4.79; a supplier at the AQL has a 64% chance of at least one rejection in twenty lots. Sanity check: the curve falls from 0.96 to 0.12 as \( p \) goes from 1% to 5%, which is gradual, not a step — and that gradualness is the two risks. Note what the 64% means in practice: the AQL is not a safe operating point for a supplier, and treating it as a target guarantees regular arguments over rejected lots that were exactly as agreed. The place to sit is well below the AQL, which is the same conclusion the capability threshold of Lesson 8 reaches from the other direction.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Two pieces of folklore, tested

Problem. (a) A quality manual requires the sample to be "10% of the lot". Taking \( n=125 \), \( c=3 \) from lots that are 3% defective, the exact acceptance probabilities are 0.4713 for a lot of 1,000 and 0.4813 for a lot of 100,000. Comment on what this implies for the 10% rule. (b) The same plan is used with rejected lots 100%-inspected and rectified. Given that \( \mathrm{AOQ}(p)\approx pP_a(p) \) peaks at 1.55% when \( p=2.34\% \), state what is and is not guaranteed, and evaluate \( \mathrm{AOQ} \) at \( p=1\% \) and \( p=6\% \) using \( P_a=0.9626 \) and \( P_a=0.0539 \).

Solution. (1) The lot grew by a factor of 100 and \( P_a \) moved from 0.4713 to 0.4813 — one part in a hundred. The plan's behaviour is governed by \( n \) and \( c \), not by the fraction of the lot they represent.

(2) So the 10% rule inspects 100 parts from a lot of 1,000 — roughly the right sample — and 10,000 from a lot of 100,000, which buys discrimination nobody asked for at eighty times the cost. Run the other way, on a lot of 200 it takes 20 parts, a plan far too weak to discriminate at all. The rule is wrong in both directions.

(3) \( \mathrm{AOQ}(0.01)=0.01\times0.9626=0.96\% \). Most lots pass, carrying their defectives with them.

(4) \( \mathrm{AOQ}(0.06)=0.06\times0.0539=0.32\% \). Incoming quality is far worse, but almost every lot is rejected and rectified, so little defect gets through.

(5) Between those extremes lies the maximum, 1.55% at \( p=2.34\% \). What is guaranteed is that long-run average outgoing quality cannot exceed 1.55% defective whatever the supplier does. What is not guaranteed is anything about a particular lot: an accepted lot at 3% still contains 3% defectives. And the guarantee fails entirely if rejected lots are returned unscreened rather than rectified.

Answer: (a) lot size is nearly irrelevant, so the 10% rule over-inspects large lots and under-inspects small ones; (b) AOQ is 0.96% at \( p=1\% \) and 0.32% at \( p=6\% \), with a guaranteed long-run ceiling of 1.55% that depends entirely on rejected lots actually being screened. Sanity check: AOQ falling at both ends is the right shape — good incoming quality produces few defectives and bad incoming quality gets rejected — so a maximum in between must exist. Note the two conditions that are easy to lose: the ceiling is a long-run average and not a property of any lot, and it evaporates the moment rejected lots go back to the supplier and return untouched.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Sample, screen, or leave it alone (with a per-gross cost)

Problem. Inspecting one part costs 0.40 and one defective escaping into production costs 55, in the same units. For a stable process, find the break-even fraction defective and give the minimum-cost policy at \( p=0.2\% \) and at \( p=2\% \). Then repeat the break-even calculation for a fastener line where inspection is quoted at 48 per gross. Finally, if 100% inspection detects 90% of the defectives present, find the outgoing rate from a 2% incoming rate after one, two and three passes.

Solution. (1) Cost per part of inspecting nothing is \( pk_2 \); of inspecting everything, \( k_1 \). They are equal at \( p^{*}=k_1/k_2=0.40/55=0.00727 \), that is 0.73%.

(2) At \( p=0.002 \): unscreened costs \( 0.002\times55=0.11 \) per part, screening costs 0.40. Inspect nothing.

(3) At \( p=0.02 \): unscreened costs \( 0.02\times55=1.10 \) per part against 0.40. Inspect everything. No intermediate sampling fraction beats the better extreme, because both costs are linear in the fraction inspected.

(4) A gross is 144 parts, so \( k_1=48/144=0.3333 \) per part and \( p^{*}=0.3333/55=0.00606 \), that is 0.61% — 6,061 parts per million.

(5) Each pass leaves 10% of what it was given: \( 0.02(0.10)=0.2\% \), then \( 0.02\%\), then \( 0.002\% \).

Answer: \( p^{*}=0.73\% \); inspect nothing at 0.2% and everything at 2%; at 48 per gross the break-even is 0.61%; screening at 90% efficiency gives 0.2%, 0.02% and 0.002% after one, two and three passes. Sanity check: the rule assumes a stable process at a known \( p \), which is exactly what a receiving inspector lacks — and that, rather than economics, is why acceptance sampling exists. Note that the per-gross figure needed only a count conversion, and the answer moved from 0.73% to 0.61% because inspection got cheaper, which is the direction it should move. Note finally that "100% inspection" is a name: one pass at 90% still ships 2,000 defectives per million, which is why critical characteristics are checked redundantly rather than carefully.

The operating-characteristic curve of a sampling plan plots

\( P_a(p) \) against \( p \). It is the complete description of the plan — the AQL, the LTPD, both risks and the discrimination ratio are all points read off it. Two integers, \( n \) and \( c \), determine the whole curve.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

Producer's risk \( \alpha \) is

It is a type I error in the language of hypothesis testing: rejecting a lot that was in fact acceptable. The supplier pays for it. Consumer's risk \( \beta \) is the type II error — accepting a bad lot — and the customer pays for that. Both exist because the OC curve slopes, and it slopes because a sample is a random draw.

Both costs are linear in the fraction inspected, so the optimum is at one end or the other — partial sampling never beats the better extreme. "100% inspection" of an attribute characteristic typically

Inspector and gauge are part of the measuring system and neither is perfect. At 90% efficiency a 2% incoming rate leaves 0.2% after one pass, 0.02% after two and 0.002% after three. That is why critical characteristics are checked redundantly rather than once carefully, and why "100% inspected" on a certificate is a description of effort, not of outcome.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the AQL that was read as an allowance. A purchase specification for machined fittings called for inspection at "AQL 1.0". The supplier's quality plan set the process aim so that about 1% of parts were nonconforming, on the stated reasoning that 1% was what the contract allowed. Lots were rejected repeatedly, each rejection was disputed, and both sides believed the other was in breach.

Both readings of the number were wrong in the same way. An AQL is a property of the sampling plan — the quality it accepts about 95% of the time — not a permitted defect rate. A supplier sitting exactly on it faces \( 1-0.95^{20}=64\% \) odds of at least one rejection in twenty lots, so the rejections were the plan working as designed, not evidence of a worse process than agreed. The customer's position was no better founded: accepted lots at 1% defective had been carrying 1% defectives into assembly the whole time, because acceptance is a decision about a lot and never a treatment of it.

Two practices follow. Write the specification in terms both sides can check — the plan \( (n,c) \), the resulting AQL and LTPD, and the risks each party is accepting — rather than a bare AQL figure that invites the allowance reading. And prefer evidence to sampling where it can be had: a supplier who supplies control charts and capability studies has stated the process fraction defective directly, at which point the receiving decision stops being a gamble on a sample and becomes the straightforward cost comparison of the all-or-none rule.
