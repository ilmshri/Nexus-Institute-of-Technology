Form, fits, and GD&T verification

Measuring flatness, roundness, and runout — the rotating-equipment repair set. Verifying position with datums. CMM measurement in outline.

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Measure runout on a shaft: setup, instrument, and what the number means.
  • What does a datum system add to a position check?

§1 · Size is one number; form is not

A two-point measurement — a micrometer across a diameter — returns a size. It says almost nothing about form, which is how the real surface departs from its ideal geometry. The classic demonstration is a three-lobed shaft: measured across any diameter it reads exactly the same everywhere, passes a size check perfectly, and will not rotate properly in a bearing, because its diameter is constant while its radius is not.

Form controls exist because of that gap. Flatness and straightness constrain a surface or a line to lie between two parallel planes or lines; roundness constrains a circular section between two concentric circles; cylindricity does both at once along a length. None of them references a datum — a form tolerance is about the feature's own shape, which is why it can be checked without knowing how the part is held.

Runout and position are different animals: they are related controls, and they need datums. That distinction — form controls are self-contained, orientation and location controls are not — is the organising idea of the whole GD&T system, and it also predicts which measurements are hard.

§2 · Roundness, and the artefact that dominates it

Measure radius against angle around a section and the result is a polar trace. Its peak-to-valley value looks like an answer, and usually is not, because a part mounted even slightly off-centre produces a large sinusoidal signal at one cycle per revolution — the first harmonic — that has nothing to do with the part's shape.

Key relation — separating centring from form

\[ r(\theta)\approx a_0+b_1\cos\theta+c_1\sin\theta+\underbrace{\ldots}_{\text{form}}, \qquad e=\sqrt{b_1^2+c_1^2} \]

The first harmonic is the eccentricity of the setup. Genuine out-of-roundness lives in the second harmonic and above — ovality at two cycles, three-lobing at three.

Take a trace of eight points, in micrometres of deviation: 12, 6, −4, −10, −8, 0, 7, 11. Peak to valley is 22 µm. Fitting the first harmonic gives \( b_1 = 9.77 \), \( c_1 = -5.40 \), so \( e = 11.17\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) at 331°. Subtract it and the residual peak-to-valley is 2.82 µm. Eighty-seven per cent of the apparent form error was the part sitting off-centre in the fixture.

This is why a roundness instrument centres and levels the part before it measures, and why the number it reports is not simply the range of its trace. It is also why the harmonic content is worth reading rather than only the total: two-cycle ovality suggests chuck clamping, three-lobe suggests centreless grinding, and high harmonics suggest chatter. The total alone hides the diagnosis.

§3 · Runout: the cheap measurement that means several things at once

Put a dial indicator on a rotating shaft and read the total indicator reading, the full sweep of the needle over one revolution. It is the fastest useful check in a workshop and the most commonly over-interpreted.

Key relation — runout is a composite

\[ \text{TIR}=2e+(\text{form error})+(\text{datum error}) \]

A perfectly round part running off-centre by \( e \) gives \( \mathrm{TIR}=2e \). Runout never separates its causes — it sums them.

A shaft shows TIR = 0.080 mm. A separate roundness check, properly centred, gives 0.006 mm of form error. The eccentricity is therefore \( (0.080-0.006)/2 = 0.037\ \mathrm{mm} \) — and that is the number with consequences: a 12 kg rotor with 0.037 mm of eccentricity at 3000 rev/min carries an unbalance force of \( m e\omega^2 = 12(3.7\times10^{-5})(314.2)^2 = 43.8\ \mathrm{N} \), once per revolution, straight into the bearings. Kinematics & Dynamics of Machinery calls that the shaking force; metrology is where its magnitude is established.

The practical rule is that runout is an excellent screen and a poor diagnosis. A high TIR tells you to look; it does not tell you whether to re-machine the journal, re-cut the centres, or re-mount the part.

§4 · Position, datums, and the bonus most inspectors forget

Location is controlled by position tolerance, and it works in a way that catches people trained on plus/minus dimensions. The tolerance defines a cylindrical zone of stated diameter, centred on the theoretically exact position, within which the feature's axis must lie. Because the zone is round rather than square, it is 57% larger in area than the \( \pm \) box that circumscribes the same limits — and it is the correct shape, since a radial error does not care about direction.

Key relations — position and the MMC bonus

\[ \varnothing_{\text{error}}=2\sqrt{\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2},\qquad \text{bonus}=|\,\text{actual size}-\text{MMC}\,| \]

At maximum material condition the tolerance grows by the amount the feature departs from MMC, because a hole made larger than its smallest permitted size leaves more room for the mating part.

A hole is specified \( \varnothing10.00 \) to \( \varnothing10.10 \) with position \( \varnothing0.2 \) at MMC. It is produced at \( \varnothing10.06 \), with its axis 0.08 mm out in \( x \) and 0.09 mm in \( y \). The positional error is \( 2\sqrt{0.08^2+0.09^2} = 0.241\ \mathrm{mm} \). Against the stated 0.2 it fails. But MMC for a hole is its smallest size, 10.00, so the bonus is \( 10.06-10.00 = 0.06 \) and the available tolerance is \( 0.26\ \mathrm{mm} \). The part passes.

Rejecting it would scrap a good part, and this is not a rare situation — it is the normal one, since features are rarely produced exactly at MMC. The bonus is not a concession or a leniency; it is a statement that the assembly still fits, which is what the tolerance was protecting in the first place.

A coordinate measuring machine is the instrument that makes this practical at scale. It probes points, fits substitute geometry to them by least squares, establishes the datum reference frame from the datum features in order, and evaluates the tolerance in that frame. Two cautions belong with it: the fitted geometry depends on where the points were taken, so a sparse or badly distributed sample can report a form error that is really a sampling artefact; and the machine's own uncertainty belongs in the budget of Lesson 3 like any other instrument's.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

How much of this roundness trace is real?

Problem. A roundness trace of eight equally spaced points gives radial deviations, in micrometres, of 12, 6, −4, −10, −8, 0, 7, 11. Find the peak-to-valley value as measured, fit the first harmonic \( a_0+b_1\cos\theta+c_1\sin\theta \), find the eccentricity it represents, and find the residual peak-to-valley out-of-roundness.

Solution. (1) As measured, peak to valley is \( 12-(-10)=22\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(2) \( a_0 = \frac{1}{8}\sum r_i = \frac{14}{8} = 1.75\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(3) \( b_1 = \frac{2}{8}\sum r_i\cos\theta_i \). With \( \cos\theta = 1, 0.707, 0, -0.707, -1, -0.707, 0, 0.707 \), the sum is 39.09, so \( b_1 = 9.77\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(4) \( c_1 = \frac{2}{8}\sum r_i\sin\theta_i \). With \( \sin\theta = 0, 0.707, 1, 0.707, 0, -0.707, -1, -0.707 \), the sum is −21.61, so \( c_1 = -5.40\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(5) \( e=\sqrt{9.77^2+5.40^2} = \sqrt{95.51+29.18} = 11.17\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), directed at \( \operatorname{atan2}(-5.40,\,9.77) = 331^\circ \).

(6) Subtracting the fitted harmonic point by point leaves residuals of 0.48, 1.16, −0.35, −1.02, 0.02, 1.34, −0.15, −1.48 µm, so the residual peak to valley is \( 1.34-(-1.48) = 2.82\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

Answer: 22 µm as measured; eccentricity 11.17 µm at 331°; genuine out-of-roundness 2.82 µm. Sanity check: the eccentricity removed \( (22-2.82)/22 = 87\% \) of the apparent form error, which is why a roundness instrument centres and levels a part before measuring rather than simply reporting the range of its trace. Note the direction is worth keeping: 331° points at where the part sits proud in the fixture, which is actionable, while the 22 µm alone would have sent the part back to the grinder for a fault it does not have.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Runout, decomposed and then costed

Problem. A shaft journal shows a total indicator reading of 0.080 mm. A separate, properly centred roundness check on the same section gives 0.006 mm of form error. Find the eccentricity. Then, treating the shaft as part of a 12 kg rotor running at 3000 rev/min, find the unbalance force that eccentricity produces.

Solution. (1) For a round part running off-centre, \( \mathrm{TIR} = 2e \); the measured TIR also contains the form error, so \( 2e = 0.080-0.006 = 0.074 \) and \( e = 0.037\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(2) \( \omega = 2\pi(3000)/60 = 314.16\ \mathrm{rad/s} \).

(3) \( F = m e\omega^2 = 12(3.7\times10^{-5})(314.16)^2 = 12(3.7\times10^{-5})(98\,696) = 43.8\ \mathrm{N} \).

Answer: \( e = 0.037 \) mm, producing a 43.8 N rotating force at 3000 rev/min. Sanity check: the decomposition matters because the two causes have different cures — 0.006 mm of form error is a machining problem and 0.037 mm of eccentricity is a mounting or centres problem, and TIR alone cannot tell them apart. The force is the reason anyone cares: 43.8 N once per revolution, fifty times a second, straight into the bearings, from a 37 micrometre error that a size check would never have seen. Note also that the force scales as \( \omega^2 \), so the same shaft at 6000 rev/min would deliver 175 N.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Position with the MMC bonus (with a US customary check)

Problem. A hole is specified \( \varnothing10.00 \) to \( \varnothing10.10 \) mm with a position tolerance of \( \varnothing0.2 \) at MMC. It is produced at \( \varnothing10.06 \), with its axis displaced 0.08 mm in \( x \) and 0.09 mm in \( y \) from true position. Find the positional error, the bonus tolerance, and the verdict — both as specified and as it would be if the tolerance were applied regardless of feature size. Give the positional error in inches.

Solution. (1) The zone is cylindrical, so the error is a diameter: \( \varnothing_{\text{error}} = 2\sqrt{0.08^2+0.09^2} = 2\sqrt{0.0064+0.0081} = 2(0.1204) = 0.241\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(2) MMC for a hole is its smallest permitted size, 10.00 mm. The feature was made at 10.06, so bonus \( = 10.06-10.00 = 0.06\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(3) Available tolerance \( = 0.2+0.06 = 0.26\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(4) \( 0.241 < 0.26 \) → the part passes as specified. Applied regardless of feature size, the limit would be 0.2 and \( 0.241 > 0.2 \) → it would fail.

(5) \( 0.241\ \mathrm{mm} \div 25.4 = 0.00948\ \mathrm{in} \).

Answer: error \( \varnothing0.241 \) mm (0.00948 in), bonus 0.06 mm, allowed 0.26 mm — passes at MMC, would fail regardless of feature size. Sanity check: the two verdicts differ on the same measured part, which is exactly why the modifier on the drawing has to be read before the calculation is done. The bonus is not leniency: a hole larger than MMC leaves more clearance for the mating fastener, so the assembly still fits and the tolerance has done its job. Note also the doubling — forgetting that a position error is stated as a zone diameter and comparing the 0.120 radial value against 0.2 would have passed the part for the wrong reason.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

A three-lobed shaft is measured across several diameters with a micrometer. The readings are all identical. This shows that

An odd-lobed form has constant diameter and varying radius, so every two-point measurement agrees while the part is not round. It is the standard demonstration that size and form are different quantities, and the reason roundness is measured against a rotating axis rather than across a diameter. The part may well be within size tolerance and still not run in a bearing.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

The first harmonic of a roundness trace represents

One cycle per revolution is what an off-centre mounting produces, so the first harmonic is a setup artefact rather than a property of the part. Genuine form lives at two cycles (ovality, often from clamping), three (lobing, often from centreless grinding) and above (chatter). In the worked trace the first harmonic accounted for 87% of the apparent 22 µm.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A roundness trace shows 22 µm peak to valley; after removing the fitted first harmonic the residual is 2.8 µm. The part's out-of-roundness is

2.8 µm — the rest was the part sitting 11.2 µm off-centre in the fixture. Reporting the raw 22 would reject a good part for a fault belonging to the setup, which is why a roundness instrument centres and levels before measuring and why the reported value is not simply the range of the trace.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

For a perfectly round shaft mounted with eccentricity \( e \), the TIR is

The indicator sees the high side and then the low side, so the needle sweeps \( 2e \) over a revolution. This is why TIR must be halved before it can be compared with an eccentricity or fed into an unbalance calculation — and why a 0.080 mm TIR is a 0.040 mm mounting error, not 0.080.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Runout is best described as

TIR sums whatever moves the indicator: the part's own form, its eccentricity about the datum axis, and any error in the datum itself. That makes it a fast and genuinely useful screen and a poor diagnosis — a high reading says look, not what to fix. Separating the causes needs a second, centred measurement, as the worked example does.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A position tolerance defines a zone that is

A cylinder, because a radial error has no preferred direction. That is also why the error is computed as a diameter, \( 2\sqrt{\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2} \) — forgetting the factor of two is the commonest arithmetic slip in position evaluation and passes parts that should fail. A round zone is also 57% larger in area than the square box that circumscribes the same plus/minus limits.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A hole specified \( \varnothing10.00\text{--}10.10 \) with position \( \varnothing0.2 \) at MMC is produced at \( \varnothing10.06 \). The available position tolerance is

MMC for a hole is its smallest size, 10.00, so departing to 10.06 earns a bonus of 0.06 and the tolerance becomes 0.26. The bonus is not a concession: a larger hole leaves more clearance for the mating fastener, so the assembly the tolerance was protecting still works. Ignoring it scraps good parts routinely, because features are rarely produced exactly at MMC.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A CMM reports a form error that turns out to be a sampling artefact. The most likely cause is

A CMM evaluates substitute geometry least-squares-fitted to the points it took, so the reported form depends on where those points were. A sparse or clustered sample can miss a lobe entirely or invent one. It is the same lesson as the roundness harmonic: the number a machine reports is a property of the measurement as well as of the part, and the sampling strategy belongs in the method.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the journals that were reground for nothing. A pump shaft was checked during overhaul with a dial indicator between centres. The journal showed 0.080 mm TIR against a 0.030 mm limit, and both journals were reground. The shaft was reassembled, ran, and showed the same vibration as before.

The measurement was correct and the conclusion drawn from it was not. TIR is a composite: \( \mathrm{TIR} = 2e + \text{form} + \text{datum error} \). A centred roundness check on the same section found only 0.006 mm of genuine form error, so the eccentricity was \( (0.080-0.006)/2 = 0.037\ \mathrm{mm} \) — and eccentricity measured between centres is a statement about the centres, which had been damaged, as much as about the journal. Regrinding the journal removed metal from the one part of the assembly that was nearly right. The 0.037 mm that remained is worth 43.8 N of rotating force on a 12 kg rotor at 3000 rev/min, which is what the vibration survey had been reporting all along.

Two practices follow. Runout is a screen, and a screen that fails should trigger a measurement that can separate causes — a centred roundness check costs minutes and distinguishes a machining fault from a mounting one. And when a repair does not change the symptom, the first suspect is the diagnosis rather than the workmanship: the same TIR before and after regrinding was evidence that the metal removed had not been the problem.
