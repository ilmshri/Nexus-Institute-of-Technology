المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • %GRR at 35%: what does that verdict mean for your SPC plans?
  • Repeatability versus reproducibility: whose variation is each?

§1 · The measuring system has a variance of its own

Lesson 8 made the point in passing and then moved on: what a study observes is not the process. Every reading is the part plus the measurement, and because those two are independent their variances add.

Key relation — the observed variance

\[ \sigma^2_{\text{obs}}=\sigma^2_{\text{part}}+\sigma^2_{\text{gauge}},\qquad \sigma^2_{\text{gauge}}=\sigma^2_{\text{EV}}+\sigma^2_{\text{AV}} \]

Repeatability (equipment variation, EV) is the scatter of one operator measuring one part with one gauge, repeatedly — the instrument and the method talking. Reproducibility (appraiser variation, AV) is the scatter between operators measuring the same parts — the people, or more often the procedure they were given.

Everything else in this lesson is bookkeeping on those two terms. The reason it matters is that every number in the previous three lessons was computed from measured data: the control limits of Lesson 7, the \( \hat\sigma \) behind Lesson 8's indices, and the accept/reject decision on every part. If the measuring system's share of the variance is unknown, so is the meaning of all of them.

The distinction between the two components is diagnostic, not academic. High repeatability variation points at the gauge, the fixture, the location the part is held in, or a method that leaves the reading to judgement. High reproducibility variation points at the instructions — two operators doing genuinely different things, both convinced they are following the procedure. They have different cures, and a single combined figure tells you which to buy nothing about.

§2 · The crossed study, using the estimator you already have

The standard design is crossed: every operator measures every part, several times. Ten parts, three operators, three trials is 90 readings and is the usual size. The parts must span the real process range — a study run on ten parts hand-picked from the middle of the distribution will report a gauge far worse than it is, because it has shrunk the part variation it is compared against.

The arithmetic needs no new machinery. Ranges convert to standard deviations exactly as in Lesson 7.

Key relations — the average-and-range method

\[ \sigma_{\text{EV}}=\frac{\bar R}{d_2},\qquad \sigma_{\text{AV}}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{R_o}{d_2}\right)^{2}-\frac{\sigma^2_{\text{EV}}}{n\,r}},\qquad \sigma_{\text{PV}}=\frac{R_p}{d_2} \]

\( \bar R \) is the mean of the within-cell ranges, \( R_o \) the range of the operator averages, \( R_p \) the range of the part averages, with \( d_2 \) taken at the relevant count — 1.693 for three, 3.078 for ten. The subtraction in \( \sigma_{\text{AV}} \) is essential: an operator's average already carries repeatability error, \( \sigma^2_{\text{EV}}/nr \) of it, and leaving it in would charge the operators for the gauge.

A study on a bore gives \( \bar R=5.4\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), \( R_o=4.5\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) and \( R_p=34\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), with \( n=10 \), \( r=3 \), against a 100 µm tolerance. Then \( \sigma_{\text{EV}}=5.4/1.693=3.19\ \mu\mathrm{m} \); \( (4.5/1.693)^2=7.065 \) less \( 3.19^2/30=0.339 \) gives \( \sigma_{\text{AV}}=2.59\ \mu\mathrm{m} \); so \( \mathrm{GRR}=\sqrt{3.19^2+2.59^2}=4.11\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). The parts give \( \mathrm{PV}=34/3.078=11.05\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), and \( \mathrm{TV}=\sqrt{4.11^2+11.05^2}=11.79\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

One honest note on the constants. Using \( d_2 \) throughout is the usual teaching form and is right for \( \bar R \), which averages thirty cell ranges. A single range of three operator averages is a one-subgroup estimate, for which standards tabulate a slightly different small-sample constant; the difference is a few per cent and changes no verdict here. The modern alternative is to run the study as an analysis of variance, which estimates the same components directly and additionally detects operator-by-part interaction that the range method cannot see.

§3 · The verdict, and what 35% actually costs

Two ratios are reported, and they answer different questions.

Key relations — the two verdicts and the resolution

\[ \%\mathrm{GRR}_{\text{process}}=100\frac{\mathrm{GRR}}{\mathrm{TV}},\qquad \%\mathrm{GRR}_{\text{tolerance}}=100\frac{6\,\mathrm{GRR}}{\mathrm{USL}-\mathrm{LSL}},\qquad \mathrm{ndc}=\left\lfloor\sqrt2\,\frac{\mathrm{PV}}{\mathrm{GRR}}\right\rfloor \]

The first asks whether the gauge can see the process — the question that matters for control charts and capability. The second asks whether it can see the tolerance — the question that matters for accepting parts. Under 10% is accepted, over 30% is not, and 10–30% is judged on what the gauge is for. \( \mathrm{ndc} \) is the number of distinct categories the gauge can resolve across the part spread.

For this study \( \%\mathrm{GRR}_{\text{process}}=100(4.11/11.79)=34.9\% \), and \( \%\mathrm{GRR}_{\text{tolerance}}=100(6\times4.11)/100=24.7\% \). The two verdicts differ, and both are correct: this gauge is unacceptable for process control and marginal for conformance decisions. A single number would have hidden that.

Now the part that surprises people, and it is worth doing carefully because the intuition is wrong. Thirty-five per cent sounds like a third of the data is noise. In variance it is \( 4.11^2/11.79^2=12.2\% \), and the effect on the process standard deviation is smaller still: removing the gauge entirely would take \( \sigma \) from 11.79 to 11.05, a fall of 6.7%, and lift \( C_p \) from 1.41 to 1.51. Quadrature is forgiving that way — which is exactly why \( \%\mathrm{GRR} \) is a poor guide to how much the gauge is distorting a capability index.

What 35% destroys is resolution. \( \mathrm{ndc}=\lfloor1.41\times11.05/4.11\rfloor=3 \): the gauge sorts this process into three categories, which is barely more than pass, middle and fail. The constant is \( \sqrt2 \), and the meaning is the number of non-overlapping intervals of gauge uncertainty that fit inside the part spread. Five is the usual minimum for a variables control chart; below that the chart is plotting the gauge. The concrete cost on an \( \bar X \) chart is that the limits, computed from inflated data, are too wide: a genuine one-standard-deviation shift in the process is detected with probability 0.183 instead of 0.222, so the average run length to catch it goes from 4.5 subgroups to 5.5 — 22% slower, permanently, from an instrument nobody is looking at.

§4 · What to do about it

Start by reading which component is large, because the two have different prices.

Here \( \sigma^2_{\text{EV}}=10.17 \) and \( \sigma^2_{\text{AV}}=6.73 \), so 60% of the gauge's variance is repeatability — equipment and method, not people. Training the operators would address the smaller share. What addresses the larger share is a better fixture, a more definite location for the part, a finer instrument, or simply averaging: the mean of \( r \) readings has repeatability variance \( \sigma^2_{\text{EV}}/r \). Averaging three readings takes \( \sigma_{\text{EV}} \) from 3.19 to 1.84, \( \mathrm{GRR} \) to 3.18, \( \%\mathrm{GRR} \) to 27.7% and \( \mathrm{ndc} \) to 4 — a real improvement bought with time rather than capital. Had \( \sigma_{\text{AV}} \) dominated instead, none of that would have helped and the answer would have been the work instruction.

Resolution belongs in the same audit. A rounded reading contributes a variance of \( (\text{resolution})^2/12 \), so a 1 µm instrument adds 0.29 µm and is negligible here, while a 5 µm instrument would add 1.44 µm — a fifth of the repeatability, from the display alone. The old rule of thumb that the discrimination should be a tenth of the process spread or the tolerance is this calculation in disguise.

Meanwhile, parts still have to be accepted or rejected with the gauge you have, and the honest way to do that is the guard band of Lesson 3 — the same rule, with the study's \( \sigma_{\text{GRR}} \) in place of a certificate's expanded uncertainty, because the repeatability and reproducibility measured here are the working-condition version of the same quantity.

Key relation — guard banding

\[ \text{accept only if } |y|\le \frac{T}{2}-z_{\gamma}\,\sigma_{\text{GRR}} \]

Pulling the acceptance limit inside the specification by \( z_\gamma \) gauge standard deviations makes a false acceptance at that confidence impossible. It buys that confidence with tolerance, and the price is set entirely by the gauge.

At 95% one-sided confidence, \( z=1.645 \), so the band is \( 1.645\times4.11=6.76\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) at each end — 13.5% of the 100 µm tolerance surrendered to the measuring system. With a 1.0 µm gauge the same confidence would cost 3.3%. That is the number to put in front of whoever is deciding whether a better instrument is worth buying, because it is denominated in the thing the shop actually values.

Two ordering rules close the subject. The study comes before the control charts and the capability study, not after them, because both are computed from measured data and a study run afterwards can only invalidate work already done. And the parts used must span the real process range: a study on ten similar parts understates \( \mathrm{PV} \), inflates \( \%\mathrm{GRR} \), and condemns a gauge that was adequate — the mirror image of the error it is meant to catch.

What this lesson assumes

  • \( \sigma^2_{\text{obs}}=\sigma^2_{\text{proc}}+\sigma^2_{\text{meas}} \) (Lesson 8, and Engineering Statistics L4) — the identity this whole lesson decomposes.
  • \( \hat\sigma=\bar R/d_2 \) and the \( d_2 \) table (Lesson 7) — the same estimator, applied to cells, operators and parts.
  • Capability indices and control-chart sensitivity (Lessons 7 and 8) — the two things a poor measuring system quietly degrades.
  • Type A uncertainty evaluation (Lesson 3) — a gauge study is a type A evaluation of two named components.
  • Resolution and accuracy (Lesson 1) — discrimination as a variance source, \( (\text{resolution})^2/12 \).
  • Guard banding against an uncertainty (Lesson 3) — introduced there against a certificate's expanded uncertainty; this lesson supplies the working-condition figure that belongs in it.
  • Normal tail areas and one-sided confidence (Engineering Statistics L4 and L6) — the \( z_\gamma=1.645 \) behind a guard band.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Work a crossed study end to end

Problem. A crossed study uses 10 parts, 3 operators and 3 trials. The mean of the within-cell ranges is \( \bar R=5.4\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), the range of the three operator averages is \( R_o=4.5\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), and the range of the ten part averages is \( R_p=34\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). Take \( d_2=1.693 \) for three and 3.078 for ten. Find EV, AV, GRR, PV and TV, then \( \%\mathrm{GRR} \) against both the process and a 100 µm tolerance, and the number of distinct categories. Give the verdict.

Solution. (1) \( \sigma_{\text{EV}}=\bar R/d_2=5.4/1.693=3.19\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(2) The operator spread is \( R_o/d_2=4.5/1.693=2.658 \), but an operator's average already carries repeatability error worth \( \sigma^2_{\text{EV}}/nr=10.17/30=0.339 \). So \( \sigma^2_{\text{AV}}=2.658^2-0.339=7.065-0.339=6.726 \) and \( \sigma_{\text{AV}}=2.59\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(3) \( \mathrm{GRR}=\sqrt{3.19^2+2.59^2}=\sqrt{10.17+6.73}=\sqrt{16.90} =4.11\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(4) \( \mathrm{PV}=34/3.078=11.05\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), so \( \mathrm{TV}=\sqrt{16.90+122.02}=\sqrt{138.92}=11.79\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(5) \( \%\mathrm{GRR}_{\text{process}}=100(4.11/11.79)=34.9\% \).

(6) \( \%\mathrm{GRR}_{\text{tolerance}}=100(6\times4.11)/100=24.7\% \).

(7) \( \mathrm{ndc}=\lfloor1.41\times11.05/4.11\rfloor=\lfloor3.79\rfloor=3 \).

(8) Over 30% against the process: unacceptable for process control. Between 10 and 30% against the tolerance: marginal for accept/reject, to be judged on the cost of an error.

Answer: EV 3.19, AV 2.59, GRR 4.11, PV 11.05, TV 11.79 µm; \( \%\mathrm{GRR} \) 34.9% to the process and 24.7% to the tolerance; ndc = 3. Unacceptable for charting, marginal for conformance. Sanity check: the two verdicts differ because they answer different questions, and reporting only one of them is how a gauge gets approved for the wrong job. Note the subtraction in step 2: it is worth 0.339 of variance here, small, but omitting it charges the operators for the equipment and misdirects the fix. Note finally that ndc = 3 is the binding result — a control chart drawn with a gauge that resolves three categories is largely plotting the instrument.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

What 35% costs, and what it does not

Problem. For the study above (GRR 4.11, PV 11.05, TV 11.79 µm, tolerance 100 µm), find the gauge's share of the variance, the percentage by which the observed standard deviation is inflated, and \( C_p \) computed with and without the gauge. Then find the probability that an \( \bar X \) chart with \( n=5 \) detects a one-standard-deviation process shift using this gauge, against a perfect one, and the average run lengths.

Solution. (1) Variance share: \( \mathrm{GRR}^2/\mathrm{TV}^2=16.90/138.92=12.2\% \) — not 34.9%, because the ratio of standard deviations is not the ratio of variances.

(2) Inflation of the standard deviation: \( \mathrm{TV}/\mathrm{PV}=11.79/11.05=1.067 \), so 6.7%.

(3) \( C_p \) with the gauge: \( 100/(6\times11.79)=1.41 \). Without it: \( 100/(6\times11.05)=1.51 \). The index is 6.3% low.

(4) A shift of one true process standard deviation is \( 11.05/11.79=0.937 \) observed standard deviations, so on the chart it is \( 0.937\sqrt5=2.096 \) standard errors instead of \( \sqrt5=2.236 \).

(5) Detection: \( 1-\Phi(3-2.096)=1-\Phi(0.904)=0.183 \), against \( 1-\Phi(0.764)=0.222 \) with a perfect gauge.

(6) Average run lengths: \( 1/0.183=5.47 \) subgroups against \( 1/0.222=4.50 \) — 22% slower.

Answer: the gauge is 12.2% of the variance and inflates \( \sigma \) by 6.7%, costing 6.3% of \( C_p \); detection of a \( 1\sigma \) shift falls from 0.222 to 0.183 and the run length rises from 4.50 to 5.47 subgroups. Sanity check: a figure that reads as "a third of my data is noise" turns out to cost 6% of a capability index, which is the quadrature at work and worth internalising before arguing for a new instrument on the strength of \( \%\mathrm{GRR} \) alone. The real damage is elsewhere: ndc = 3 and a chart that is permanently 22% slower to notice a shift. Note that the penalty is invisible in the data — the chart looks normal, the limits look reasonable, and they are simply too wide.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Guard bands and fixes, in two unit systems

Problem. With \( \mathrm{GRR}=4.11\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) and a 100 µm tolerance, find the guard band needed for 95% one-sided confidence that an accepted part really conforms, and express it as a percentage of the tolerance; repeat for a gauge at \( \mathrm{GRR}=1.0\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). Then, given \( \sigma_{\text{EV}}=3.19 \) and \( \sigma_{\text{AV}}=2.59\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), say which fix is worth buying and find \( \%\mathrm{GRR} \) if each measurement becomes the mean of three readings (PV stays 11.05). Finally repeat the guard-band calculation for a gauge at \( \mathrm{GRR}=0.00016 \) in against a 0.0040 in tolerance.

Solution. (1) For 95% one-sided confidence, \( z=1.645 \), so the band is \( 1.645\times4.11=6.76\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) at each end.

(2) Both ends: \( 13.52\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) of a 100 µm tolerance, that is 13.5% surrendered to the measuring system.

(3) At \( \mathrm{GRR}=1.0 \): \( 2\times1.645=3.29\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), or 3.3%. The difference, 10 percentage points of tolerance, is what the better instrument buys.

(4) \( \sigma^2_{\text{EV}}=10.17 \) against \( \sigma^2_{\text{AV}}=6.73 \), so 60% of the gauge's variance is repeatability — equipment, fixture and method, not the operators. Training addresses the smaller share.

(5) Averaging three readings divides the repeatability variance by three: \( \sigma_{\text{EV}}=3.19/\sqrt3=1.84 \), so \( \mathrm{GRR}=\sqrt{3.39+6.73}=3.18 \), \( \mathrm{TV}=\sqrt{10.12+122.02}=11.49 \), and \( \%\mathrm{GRR}=100(3.18/11.49)=27.7\% \), with ndc rising to 4.

(6) In inches: \( 1.645\times0.00016=0.000263 \) in each side, \( 0.000527 \) in total, which is \( 13.2\% \) of 0.0040 in. (The gauge is \( 0.00016\times25400=4.06\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), so the same answer in different clothes.)

Answer: 6.76 µm each side, 13.5% of tolerance, falling to 3.3% with a 1.0 µm gauge; repeatability dominates, so averaging three readings is the cheap fix and takes \( \%\mathrm{GRR} \) to 27.7% with ndc 4; the inch case gives 0.000263 in each side, 13.2%. Sanity check: the guard band is the right currency for this argument — a shop that shrugs at "34.9% GRR" will not shrug at losing 13.5% of its tolerance, and the two statements are the same fact. Note that averaging helps only because EV dominates here; if the operators had been the larger term, three readings each would have bought almost nothing and the answer would have been to rewrite the method. Note also that the inch and micrometre cases agree once converted, as they must — the guard band is a length, not an index.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

Repeatability and reproducibility are, respectively, the variation

Repeatability is one operator, one part, one gauge, repeated — the equipment and the method talking. Reproducibility is the scatter between operators on the same parts, which in practice usually means the work instruction rather than the people. The two have different cures, which is why a combined figure alone tells you nothing about what to fix.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

A study returns \( \%\mathrm{GRR}=35\% \) against the process variation. For an SPC deployment this means

Above 30% is not acceptable for process control. The binding consequence is resolution: \( \mathrm{ndc}=\lfloor1.41\times11.05/4.11\rfloor=3 \), where five is the usual minimum for a variables chart, so the chart is largely plotting the instrument. Widening the limits is what the inflated data already did, and it is the problem rather than the fix.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

With GRR 4.11 and TV 11.79 µm, the gauge's share of the variance is

\( 4.11^2/11.79^2=16.90/138.92=12.2\% \). The 34.9% is the ratio of standard deviations, and 6.7% is how much the observed \( \sigma \) is inflated. Because the terms combine in quadrature, a \( \%\mathrm{GRR} \) that sounds alarming distorts a capability index only modestly — here \( C_p \) reads 1.41 instead of 1.51.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

The number of distinct categories is \( \lfloor\sqrt2\,\mathrm{PV}/\mathrm{GRR}\rfloor \). Its practical significance is that

ndc counts the non-overlapping intervals of gauge uncertainty that fit inside the part spread — how many genuinely different values the gauge can tell apart. Five is the usual minimum for a variables chart; at 3 the gauge is closer to an attribute device. It complements \( \%\mathrm{GRR} \) rather than replacing it, and it is usually the more damning of the two.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A gauge with \( \mathrm{GRR}=4.11\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) is used to accept parts against a 100 µm tolerance, with a guard band giving 95% one-sided confidence. The band consumes

\( 1.645\times4.11=6.76\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) at each end, so 13.5 µm of 100. With a 1.0 µm gauge the same confidence costs 3.3%. Stating the gauge's cost as lost tolerance is usually more persuasive than \( \%\mathrm{GRR} \), because tolerance is the thing a shop is short of.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

An instrument displaying to 5 µm contributes a standard deviation of about

Rounding to a resolution \( q \) contributes a variance \( q^2/12 \), so \( 5/\sqrt{12}=1.44\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) — a fifth of the repeatability in the worked study, from the display alone. At 1 µm it is 0.29 µm and negligible. Being unbiased does not make it free; it is a variance, not an offset.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A gauge study is run on ten parts hand-picked from the middle of the process distribution. The reported \( \%\mathrm{GRR} \) will be

\( \%\mathrm{GRR} \) compares the gauge with the part variation, so narrowing the parts inflates the ratio and condemns an adequate gauge. The study's parts must span the real process range. The mirror error — deliberately including extreme parts — flatters the gauge for the same reason.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the charts that were plotting the instrument. A machining cell put \( \bar X \)–\( R \) charts on a bore diameter and ran them for a year. The charts were noisy, signalled often, and every investigation ended without a finding; operators came to treat the signals as background and stopped recording them.

A gauge study run afterwards explained the year. With \( \bar R=5.4 \), \( R_o=4.5 \) and \( R_p=34\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), the measuring system's own standard deviation was 4.11 µm against a part variation of 11.05, giving \( \%\mathrm{GRR}=34.9\% \) and \( \mathrm{ndc}=3 \) — three distinguishable categories across the whole process range. The within-subgroup ranges the limits were computed from were largely the gauge, so the limits were too wide to catch real shifts and the point-to-point scatter that looked like an unstable process was the instrument. Sixty per cent of the gauge's variance was repeatability, so the cure was the fixture and the method rather than the operators who had been repeatedly asked to be more careful.

Two practices follow. Run the study before the charts, not after: everything computed from measured data inherits the measuring system's variance, so a study run afterwards can only tell you which of the past year's conclusions to discard. And when a chart signals repeatedly with no assignable cause ever found, treat the measuring system as a suspect equal to the process — a year of unexplained signals is itself evidence, and it costs one study to check.
