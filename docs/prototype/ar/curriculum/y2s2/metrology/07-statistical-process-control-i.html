المحاضرة

§1 · Two kinds of variation, and why the response differs

Every process varies. The whole of statistical process control rests on one distinction, due to Shewhart: some of that variation belongs to the process and some does not.

Common-cause variation is the sum of many small influences that are always present — tool wear between regrinds, coolant temperature, material hardness within its own specification, the operator's loading. No single one is identifiable, and together they produce a stable, predictable distribution. Special-cause variation comes from something assignable that is not part of the system: a tool has chipped, a batch of material is different, a fixture has loosened, a new operator is running the machine.

The two demand opposite responses, and this is the practical point. A special cause should be found and removed — it is an event, it has a story, and the story is usually short. A common cause should be left alone at the machine and attacked at the system level, because there is no single thing to fix and adjusting for it makes the output worse. Not slightly worse: measurably worse.

Key result — the cost of tampering

\[ \sigma^2_{\text{adjusted}}=2\sigma^2,\qquad \sigma_{\text{adjusted}}=\sqrt2\,\sigma\approx1.414\,\sigma \]

Adjusting the machine by \( -(x_n-T) \) after every part gives \( x_{n+1}=T-e_n+e_{n+1} \), the difference of two independent errors. The variance doubles and the standard deviation rises by 41%.

The derivation is worth following because the result is so counter-intuitive to anyone standing at a machine. Let the uncontrolled output be \( x_n=T+a_n+e_n \), where \( a_n \) is the current offset and \( e_n \) is an independent random error with standard deviation \( \sigma \). The conscientious operator sets \( a_{n+1}=a_n-(x_n-T)=-e_n \). The next part is then \( x_{n+1}=T-e_n+e_{n+1} \), which has variance \( \sigma^2+\sigma^2 \). Every correction was sensible, every one was made in good faith, and the process got 41% worse. This is tampering, and it is the commonest way a shop makes a stable process unstable.

So the first question about any deviation is not "how do I correct it?" but "is this the process talking, or something new?" A control chart exists to answer exactly that question and nothing else.

§2 · Building an \( \bar{X} \)–\( R \) chart

Take small samples of consecutive parts — a rational subgroup, usually four or five — at intervals. Consecutive matters: the subgroup must be chosen so that only common causes can act within it, leaving any special cause to show up between subgroups. A subgroup made of one part from each of five machines destroys the method before it starts.

From each subgroup take the mean \( \bar{X} \) and the range \( R \). Average those over a baseline of twenty or so subgroups to get \( \bar{\bar{X}} \) and \( \bar{R} \), and the limits follow.

Key relations — the chart limits

\[ \hat\sigma=\frac{\bar R}{d_2},\qquad \bar{\bar X}\pm A_2\bar R\ \text{ with }\ A_2=\frac{3}{d_2\sqrt n},\qquad \text{UCL}_R=D_4\bar R,\ \ \text{LCL}_R=D_3\bar R \]

At \( n=5 \): \( d_2=2.326 \), so \( A_2=3/(2.326\sqrt5)=0.577 \), and \( D_4=2.114 \), \( D_3=0 \). The limits are three standard errors of the mean, nothing more exotic — \( A_2 \) simply packages \( 3/(d_2\sqrt n) \) so the shop never has to compute \( \hat\sigma \).

The chart in the diagram uses ten subgroups of five bores, measured in micrometres above 25.000 mm. The first eight are the baseline: \( \bar{\bar X}=10.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) and \( \bar R=4.25\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), so \( A_2\bar R=0.577(4.25)=2.45 \) and the limits are 7.75 and 12.65 µm. The range chart's upper limit is \( 2.114(4.25)=8.98\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

Read the range chart first, always. It reports the process spread, and the \( \bar{X} \) limits are computed from \( \bar R \) — so if the spread is unstable, the \( \bar{X} \) limits were built on sand and mean nothing. Here every range from 3 to 6 µm sits well inside 8.98, so the spread is stable and the \( \bar{X} \) chart can be trusted. Subgroup 9 then reads 13.1, above the upper control limit, while its range is an unremarkable 5. A signal on the mean with no signal on the range says the process level shifted and its spread did not — which points at a setting, an offset, a new batch, not at a worn tool or a loose fixture.

The estimate that falls out of the same arithmetic is \( \hat\sigma=\bar R/d_2=4.25/2.326=1.83\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), so the process's own spread is \( 6\hat\sigma=10.96\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) against a 21 µm tolerance — it occupies about half of what it was given. Comparing those two numbers properly is the next lesson's subject.

§3 · Control limits are not specification limits

This is the confusion that ruins more charts than any other, and it is worth stating as bluntly as possible.

Key distinction

Control limits are computed from the process and describe what it does. They apply to a subgroup statistic, and on an \( \bar{X} \) chart they sit at \( \pm3\sigma/\sqrt n \).

Specification limits are set by the designer and describe what is wanted. They apply to an individual part. They are never computed, never plotted on an \( \bar{X} \) chart, and never used to judge whether a process is stable.

The arithmetic shows why mixing them is not a matter of taste. With \( n=5 \) the control limits sit at \( \pm3\sigma/\sqrt5=\pm1.34\sigma \) — less than half the \( \pm3\sigma \) spread of the individual parts. On the worked chart that is \( \pm2.45\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) about the centre line for the means, against \( \pm5.48\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) for single bores. The specification limits are at −10.2 and +10.8 µm from the same centre. Draw them on the \( \bar{X} \) chart, as the diagram does deliberately, and they are so far out that no realistic shift would ever reach them: the chart would signal nothing until the process had been producing scrap for a long time.

Two statements follow that sound like paradoxes and are not. A process can be in perfect statistical control and produce nothing but scrap — control means predictable, not good, and a stable process centred outside the tolerance is stably wrong. And a process can sit comfortably inside the tolerance while being wildly out of control — there is room to wander, and it is wandering, and the day it wanders past the edge nothing on the chart will have warned anyone.

Those are the two independent questions a shop has to keep separate: is it stable?, which the control chart answers, and is it good enough?, which the tolerance answers. A chart with specification limits drawn on it has silently merged them and answers neither.

§4 · Reading the chart, and the two errors on offer

The basic signal is a point beyond a control limit. With limits at three standard errors and a stable normal process, the chance a point falls outside by luck alone is \( 2(1-\Phi(3))=0.0027 \), so the average run length between false alarms is \( 1/0.0027=370 \) subgroups. That is the number the three-sigma convention was chosen to produce: rare enough that a signal is worth acting on.

Rare per point is not rare per chart. Over 25 in-control points the chance of at least one false alarm is \( 1-(1-0.0027)^{25}=6.5\% \), and a shop running several charts sees false alarms routinely. Supplementary runs rules — eight consecutive points on one side of the centre line, two of three beyond two standard errors — catch small sustained shifts that the beyond-limit rule misses, at the price of more false alarms still: eight in a row on one side has probability \( 2(0.5)^8=0.0078 \) by chance alone.

Key relation — how fast a shift is caught

\[ P_{\text{detect}}=1-\Phi\!\left(3-k\sqrt n\right)+\Phi\!\left(-3-k\sqrt n\right), \qquad \mathrm{ARL}=\frac{1}{P_{\text{detect}}} \]

for a shift of \( k \) process standard deviations. The subgroup size enters as \( \sqrt n \), which is the whole reason subgroups exist.

With \( n=5 \), a shift of one standard deviation is detected with probability 0.222 per subgroup, an average run length of 4.5 subgroups. A shift of two is detected with probability 0.930 — an average run length of 1.08, essentially the next point. Small shifts are slow to find and large ones are immediate, and the leverage is in \( \sqrt n \): moving from \( n=1 \) to \( n=5 \) is what turns a one-sigma shift from nearly invisible into a four-subgroup wait.

Behind all of it are two errors with different costs. Treating common cause as special is tampering, and §1 priced it at a 41% rise in the standard deviation. Treating special cause as common is missing a real event while it is still cheap to fix, and leaving it in the process until it becomes the new normal. The chart is not a scoreboard; it is an instrument for telling those two mistakes apart, and its only output is a decision about which kind of variation just occurred.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Build the chart, in millimetres and then in inches

Problem. Eight baseline subgroups of five bores give \( \bar{\bar X}=10.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) above 25.000 mm and \( \bar R=4.25\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). Derive \( A_2 \) from \( d_2=2.326 \) rather than quoting it, and give the \( \bar X \) and \( R \) chart limits and the estimated process standard deviation. Then state whether a ninth subgroup with \( \bar X=13.1 \) and \( R=5 \) is a signal, and what kind. Finally, repeat the \( \bar X \) limits for a shaft process with \( \bar{\bar X}=1.24980 \) in and \( \bar R=0.00042 \) in.

Solution. (1) The control limits are three standard errors of the mean, and \( \hat\sigma=\bar R/d_2 \), so \( 3\hat\sigma/\sqrt n = 3\bar R/(d_2\sqrt n) \). Hence \( A_2 = 3/(d_2\sqrt n) = 3/(2.326\sqrt5) = 3/5.2011 = 0.577 \).

(2) \( A_2\bar R = 0.577(4.25) = 2.452\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), so \( \mathrm{UCL}=10.2+2.452=12.65 \) and \( \mathrm{LCL}=10.2-2.452=7.75\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(3) \( \mathrm{UCL}_R = D_4\bar R = 2.114(4.25) = 8.98\ \mu\mathrm{m} \); \( D_3=0 \) at \( n=5 \), so \( \mathrm{LCL}_R = 0 \).

(4) \( \hat\sigma = 4.25/2.326 = 1.83\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), so the process spread is \( 6\hat\sigma = 10.96\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). As a check, \( 3\hat\sigma/\sqrt5 = 2.451 \), which matches \( A_2\bar R = 2.452 \) to the rounding in \( A_2 \).

(5) Subgroup 9: \( \bar X = 13.1 > 12.65 \), so the \( \bar X \) chart signals. Its range, 5 µm, is well inside 8.98, so the \( R \) chart does not. A signal on the mean alone means the process level shifted while its spread was unchanged.

(6) In inches: \( \hat\sigma = 0.00042/2.326 = 0.000181 \) in; \( A_2\bar R = 0.577(0.00042) = 0.000242 \) in; limits \( 1.24980\pm0.000242 \), that is 1.249558 and 1.250042 in.

Answer: \( A_2=0.577 \); \( \bar X \) limits 7.75 / 12.65 µm, \( R \) limits 0 / 8.98 µm, \( \hat\sigma=1.83\ \mu\mathrm{m} \); subgroup 9 is a level shift, not a spread change; the inch chart runs 1.249558 to 1.250042 in. Sanity check: \( A_2 \) is not a magic table entry — it is \( 3/(d_2\sqrt n) \), and recovering 0.577 from 2.326 and \( \sqrt5 \) confirms the limits really are three standard errors. Note the order of reading: the \( \bar X \) limits are built from \( \bar R \), so an unstable range chart would make them meaningless, which is why the range chart is read first. Note also that the two unit systems needed no separate theory — every constant is dimensionless.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

What the conscientious operator costs

Problem. A stable process produces \( x_n = T + a_n + e_n \), where \( T \) is the target, \( a_n \) the current machine offset and \( e_n \) an independent error with standard deviation \( \sigma \). After each part the operator resets the offset by \( a_{n+1} = a_n - (x_n - T) \). Find the variance of the output under this policy, and the percentage change in the standard deviation. State what the operator should have done instead, and how a control chart would have told them.

Solution. (1) Substituting the observation into the adjustment rule, \( a_{n+1} = a_n - (a_n + e_n) = -e_n \). The offset becomes exactly the negative of the last error.

(2) The next part is therefore \( x_{n+1} = T + a_{n+1} + e_{n+1} = T - e_n + e_{n+1} \).

(3) The two errors are independent, so their variances add: \( \operatorname{Var}(x_{n+1}) = \sigma^2 + \sigma^2 = 2\sigma^2 \).

(4) The standard deviation becomes \( \sqrt2\,\sigma = 1.414\sigma \), a rise of 41.4%.

(5) The correct action is to leave the machine alone: the deviations were common-cause, and a process in control is already doing the best it can without changing the system. A control chart would have shown every one of those readings inside the limits, which is precisely the instruction "do not adjust".

Answer: the variance doubles to \( 2\sigma^2 \) and the standard deviation rises 41.4%; the operator should have made no adjustment, and an in-control chart is what says so. Sanity check: nothing in the derivation depends on \( \sigma \), the target or the machine, so tampering costs the same 41% on any stable process — it is a property of the feedback rule, not of the equipment. Note that every individual correction was reasonable and made in good faith, which is why the result has to be derived rather than argued: intuition at the machine says each adjustment reduced an error, and the arithmetic says the corrections were adding a second independent error to every part.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

How long before the chart notices?

Problem. An \( \bar X \) chart uses subgroups of five and three-sigma limits. Find the false-alarm probability per point and the in-control average run length, and the probability of at least one false alarm in 25 in-control points. Then find the probability of detecting a shift of one process standard deviation on any single subgroup, and of two, with the corresponding average run lengths. Comment on what changing \( n \) would do.

Solution. (1) In control, a point falls outside three standard errors with probability \( 2(1-\Phi(3)) = 2(0.00135) = 0.0027 \).

(2) \( \mathrm{ARL}_0 = 1/0.0027 = 370 \) subgroups between false alarms.

(3) Over 25 points, \( P = 1-(1-0.0027)^{25} = 1-0.9347 = 0.065 \), about one chart in fifteen.

(4) A shift of \( k \) process standard deviations moves the mean by \( k\sqrt n \) standard errors, so \( P = 1-\Phi(3-k\sqrt n)+\Phi(-3-k\sqrt n) \). For \( k=1 \), \( n=5 \): \( 1-\Phi(3-2.236) = 1-\Phi(0.764) = 0.222 \), and the lower tail adds nothing. ARL \( = 1/0.222 = 4.5 \) subgroups.

(5) For \( k=2 \): \( 1-\Phi(3-4.472) = 1-\Phi(-1.472) = 0.930 \), so ARL \( = 1.08 \) — essentially the next subgroup.

(6) \( n \) enters only as \( \sqrt n \). Doubling the subgroup to 10 makes \( k\sqrt n = 3.162 \) for a one-sigma shift, raising detection to \( 1-\Phi(-0.162)=0.564 \) and dropping the ARL to 1.8 — at twice the measuring cost per subgroup.

Answer: 0.0027 per point, \( \mathrm{ARL}_0 = 370 \), 6.5% over 25 points; detection 0.222 (ARL 4.5) for a \( 1\sigma \) shift and 0.930 (ARL 1.08) for \( 2\sigma \); sensitivity scales as \( \sqrt n \). Sanity check: the in-control ARL of 370 is what the three-sigma convention was chosen to deliver — a false alarm rare enough to be worth investigating. The 6.5% over 25 points is the same number read the other way and explains why a shop running several charts sees false alarms regularly; it is not evidence the charts are wrong. Note the asymmetry that matters in practice: big shifts are caught immediately and small ones are slow, which is exactly why runs rules exist and why they buy their extra sensitivity with extra false alarms.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

An operator adjusts the machine by the full deviation after every part. Compared with leaving it alone, the output standard deviation

The adjustment makes the offset \( -e_n \), so the next part carries \( -e_n+e_{n+1} \): two independent errors instead of one, variance \( 2\sigma^2 \), standard deviation \( \sqrt2\,\sigma \) — 41% worse. The result depends only on the feedback rule, not on the machine or the size of \( \sigma \).

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

On an \( \bar X \)–\( R \) chart pair, the range chart should be read first because

\( \bar{\bar X}\pm A_2\bar R \) takes its width entirely from \( \bar R \). If the spread is unstable, \( \bar R \) is not estimating anything and the \( \bar X \) limits were built on sand. The mean chart, not the range chart, is the faster detector of a level shift.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

The constant \( A_2 = 0.577 \) at \( n = 5 \) is

\( A_2 \) packages three standard errors of the mean expressed through the range: \( 3\hat\sigma/\sqrt n = 3\bar R/(d_2\sqrt n) \). At \( n=5 \), \( 3/(2.326\sqrt5)=0.577 \). It exists so the shop never has to compute \( \hat\sigma \), not because the limits are anything other than three standard errors.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

A subgroup mean falls outside the \( \bar X \) limits while the range chart stays in control. This points to

The two charts separate level from spread. A mean-only signal says the process moved without becoming more variable — a setting, an offset, a new batch of material — rather than a worn tool or a loose fixture, which would widen the ranges. Specification limits play no part in either chart.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Control limits and specification limits differ in that

One describes what the process does, the other what is wanted, and they apply to different quantities — a subgroup mean against a single part. On an \( \bar X \) chart at \( n=5 \) the control limits sit at \( \pm1.34\sigma \) while individual parts spread over \( \pm3\sigma \), so specification limits drawn on the same chart sit far outside and the chart signals nothing.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A process is in perfect statistical control. It follows that

Control means stable and predictable, not good. A process centred outside its tolerance can be in flawless control and produce nothing but scrap — stably wrong. The converse also holds: a process can sit inside the tolerance while wandering badly, with no warning on the day it wanders out.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

With three-sigma limits, the average run length between false alarms on an in-control chart is about

\( 2(1-\Phi(3)) = 0.0027 \) per point, so \( \mathrm{ARL}_0 = 1/0.0027 = 370 \). That is the number the three-sigma convention was chosen to produce. Read the other way, 25 points carry a 6.5% chance of at least one false alarm, which is why a shop with several charts sees them regularly.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

At \( n = 5 \), an \( \bar X \) chart detects a shift of one process standard deviation with probability about 0.22 per subgroup. Increasing the subgroup to \( n = 10 \) helps because

A shift of \( k \) process standard deviations is \( k\sqrt n \) standard errors, so \( n=10 \) turns a one-sigma shift from 2.24 to 3.16 standard errors and detection rises from 0.222 to 0.564. The false-alarm rate is unchanged at 0.0027 — three-sigma limits are three-sigma limits — and the cost is measuring twice as many parts per subgroup.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the chart that was drawn with the wrong limits. A machining cell introduced control charts on a bore operation. The lines drawn on the chart were the drawing's own limits, 25.000 and 25.021 mm, because that was what everyone recognised. For eleven months no point ever came close to either line, the charts were filed, and the exercise was judged a success. Then a batch was rejected at assembly for bores that measured 25.024.

The chart had been incapable of signalling. Plotted on it were subgroup means of five, whose standard error is \( \sigma/\sqrt5 \); the correct control limits were \( \bar{\bar X}\pm A_2\bar R \), which for this process was \( \pm2.45\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) about a centre line of 10.2 µm — a band 4.9 µm wide inside a 21 µm specification. Every subgroup mean sat inside the specification because a mean of five is four times less variable than a part; that is arithmetic, not performance. Re-plotting the filed data against real control limits showed the level had shifted above the upper control limit five months earlier and stayed there.

Two practices follow. Control limits are computed from the process and nothing else — if the lines on a chart came off a drawing, the chart is decoration. And the fact that a chart never signals is not evidence of a good process; it is first evidence that the chart may not be able to signal. The check costs one calculation: limits that are not roughly \( \pm3\bar R/(d_2\sqrt n) \) about the process's own centre line are not control limits.
