Shaft design

Combined bending and torsion under fatigue; deflection and critical-speed checks. Shoulders, keyways, and their concentration costs. A pump shaft sized and detailed — the pathway's signature component.

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Which loads combine on a pump shaft, and which failure mode usually governs?
  • Why do shoulders and keyways decide a shaft's fatigue life?

01 · Why shafts get their own method

A shaft is where this course's first four lessons arrive together. It carries bending and torsion at once, so Lesson 2's combined-stress work applies; it turns, so Lesson 3's fatigue correction applies; and its torque is usually steady while its bending is not, so Lesson 4's mean-stress treatment applies to the two loads differently. That last point is the one worth slowing down for.

Consider a fibre on the surface of a rotating shaft under a fixed transverse load. Half a revolution later that same fibre is on the other side of the neutral axis. The bending stress it sees is therefore fully reversed: \( \sigma_m = 0 \) and \( \sigma_a = 32M/\pi d^3 \). The torque, meanwhile, does not care where the fibre is — a steadily driven pump applies the same torque continuously, so the shear stress is steady: \( \tau_a = 0 \) and \( \tau_m = 16T/\pi d^3 \). One shaft, one speed, two completely different fatigue characters.

02 · Assembling the design equation

Combine each pair into a von Mises equivalent — amplitude from the bending, mean from the torsion — and substitute into Goodman. Since \( \sigma'_a = \sigma_a \) for pure reversed bending and \( \sigma'_m = \sqrt3\,\tau_m \) for pure steady torsion, Goodman becomes an equation in \( d^3 \) that inverts cleanly.

Key relation — DE-Goodman shaft diameter

\[ d^3 = \frac{16n}{\pi}\left[\frac{2K_f M_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sqrt3\,K_{fs} T_m}{S_{ut}}\right] \]

\( M_a \) the alternating bending moment, \( T_m \) the steady torque, \( K_f \) and \( K_{fs} \) the fatigue concentration factors in bending and torsion at the section being sized. Every term is a moment over a strength, so the bracket has units of volume and the cube root returns a length.

Two habits make this equation safe. First, size at the critical section, not the section of maximum moment — a shoulder or keyway a little away from the peak often governs, because \( K_f \) there can outweigh the smaller moment. Second, note that \( d \) responds to the cube root of everything: doubling the factor of safety enlarges the shaft by only 26%. Fatigue margin on a shaft is cheap in diameter and expensive in nothing else, which is why shafts are rarely the component worth trimming.

03 · The checks strength does not cover

A shaft sized for stress can still be unfit. Deflection is the usual first offender: gears mesh badly and bearings run at an angle if the shaft bends too far, so slope at each bearing is normally held to around 0.001 rad and gear-mesh deflection to a small fraction of a module. These are stiffness limits, and stiffness depends on \( d^4 \) and on \( E \) — which means a stronger steel does not help at all, since every steel has essentially the same modulus.

The second is critical speed. A shaft with mass on it is a spring-mass system that will resonate at \( \omega_n = \sqrt{k/m} \). Run at that speed and deflection grows until something limits it, usually the shaft. The first critical speed must sit well clear of the operating range — a common rule keeps the operating speed below about three-quarters of the first critical.

Key relation — first critical speed

\[ \omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}=\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}}, \qquad k=\frac{48EI}{L^3} \ \text{(central mass, simple supports)} \]

The two forms are the same statement: \( \delta = mg/k \) is the static deflection under the mass, so a shaft that barely sags has a high critical speed. A useful field check needs nothing but a dial indicator.

04 · Detailing, where the fatigue life is actually decided

The diameter is the easy part. What survives or does not is the detail. A shoulder is a stress raiser whose \( K_f \) depends almost entirely on the fillet radius, so the drawing note specifying that radius is a structural requirement — a sharp corner where a generous radius was intended can move \( K_f \) from 1.5 to well past 2. A keyway is worse, typically 2.0 or more in bending, and it sits exactly where torque is transferred. Press fits raise the concentration at the end of the hub. Surface finish enters through \( k_a \), which is why grinding a fillet is such a good return, as Lesson 3's worked case showed.

The practical consequence: when a shaft calculation comes out marginal, the cheapest fixes are usually not more diameter. Increase the fillet radius, grind the critical section, move the keyway away from the peak moment, or use a splined or interference-fit torque path instead. Each buys fatigue strength without buying steel.

Closing. Reversed bending and steady torque enter the fatigue criterion through different terms; that asymmetry is the shaft equation. Then check deflection and critical speed, which strength never covers, and detail the fillets and keyways as if the life depends on them, because it does.

الأسس

What this lesson assumes

  • Bending and torsional stress formulae (Strength of Materials L2–L4) — \( \sigma=32M/\pi d^3 \) and \( \tau=16T/\pi d^3 \) for solid round sections.
  • Von Mises equivalent stress (Lesson 2) — how a bending–torsion state collapses to one number for comparison against a strength.
  • Corrected endurance limit (Lesson 3) — \( S_e \), the amplitude-side denominator.
  • Goodman criterion (Lesson 4) — the mean-stress treatment the shaft equation is derived from.
  • Natural frequency of a spring–mass system (Physics I / Dynamics) — \( \omega_n=\sqrt{k/m} \), reused here as critical speed.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Sizing the pump shaft

Problem. A pump shaft transmits 15 kW at 1450 rpm and carries a peak bending moment of 250 N·m at the impeller hub. The steel is the course's usual \( S_{ut}=600\ \mathrm{MPa} \) with a corrected \( S_e=174\ \mathrm{MPa} \). At the critical shoulder \( K_f=1.8 \) and \( K_{fs}=1.5 \). Design for \( n=2.0 \). Find: the transmitted torque and the required diameter.

Solution. (1) \( \omega = 1450\times2\pi/60 = 151.84\ \mathrm{rad/s} \), so \( T = P/\omega = 15{,}000/151.84 = 98.8\ \mathrm{N\,m} \). (2) Amplitude term: \( 2K_fM_a/S_e = 2(1.8)(250)/174\times10^6 = 5.172\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3} \). (3) Mean term: \( \sqrt3 K_{fs}T_m/S_{ut} = 1.732(1.5)(98.8)/600\times10^6 = 4.278\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^3} \). (4) \( d^3 = (16\times2/\pi)(5.172\times10^{-6}+4.278\times10^{-7}) = 10.186\times5.600\times10^{-6} = 5.704\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^3} \). (5) \( d = 38.5\ \mathrm{mm} \) — specify the next standard size, 40 mm.

Answer: \( T=98.8\ \mathrm{N\,m} \), \( d=38.5\ \mathrm{mm} \), specified as 40 mm. Sanity check: the bending term is twelve times the torsion term, so this shaft is bending-governed and the torque is almost incidental to the size — worth knowing before anyone proposes trimming the shaft to save on a torque re-rate. Back-substituting the specified 40 mm gives \( \sigma_a=71.6 \) and \( \sqrt3\tau_m=20.4\ \mathrm{MPa} \), a Goodman margin of 2.24 — the rounding up to a standard size bought 12% of extra margin for nothing.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

What a keyway costs

Problem. The same shaft, but a keyway at the critical section raises the bending fatigue concentration from \( K_f=1.8 \) to \( K_f=2.2 \). Everything else is unchanged. Find: the new required diameter, and express the penalty as a percentage of diameter and of cross-sectional area.

Solution. (1) New amplitude term: \( 2(2.2)(250)/174\times10^6 = 6.322\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3} \). (2) \( d^3 = 10.186\,(6.322\times10^{-6}+4.278\times10^{-7}) = 6.876\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^3} \), so \( d = 41.0\ \mathrm{mm} \). (3) Diameter penalty: \( 41.0/38.5-1 = 6.4\% \). Area penalty: \( 1.064^2-1 = 13.3\% \).

Answer: \( d=41.0\ \mathrm{mm} \) — a 6.4% diameter and 13.3% area penalty. Sanity check: a 22% rise in \( K_f \) produced only a 6.4% rise in diameter, which is the cube root at work again. Read it the other way, though, and it is a warning: the same cube root means a detailing error that quietly doubles \( K_f \) is not absorbed by a shaft sized with a margin of two. It is exactly the kind of change that consumes a margin nobody notices being spent.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Is it clear of resonance?

Problem. The 40 mm shaft spans 500 mm between bearings and carries a 20 kg impeller at midspan. Take \( E=207\ \mathrm{GPa} \) and treat the supports as simple, so \( k=48EI/L^3 \). Find: the shaft stiffness, the first critical speed in rpm, and the margin over the 1450 rpm operating speed.

Solution. (1) \( I=\pi d^4/64 = \pi(0.040)^4/64 = 1.2566\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^4} \). (2) \( k = 48(207\times10^9)(1.2566\times10^{-7})/(0.500)^3 = 9.989\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{N/m} \). (3) \( \omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}=\sqrt{9.989\times10^6/20}=706.7\ \mathrm{rad/s} \). (4) \( N_c = 706.7\times60/2\pi = 6749\ \mathrm{rpm} \). (5) Margin: \( 6749/1450 = 4.65 \).

Answer: \( k=9.99\ \mathrm{MN/m} \), \( N_c=6749\ \mathrm{rpm} \), 4.65 times the operating speed. Sanity check: the static deflection under the impeller is \( \delta=mg/k=20(9.81)/9.989\times10^6=0.0196\ \mathrm{mm} \), and the independent form \( \omega_n=\sqrt{g/\delta}=\sqrt{9.81/1.96\times10^{-5}}=706.7\ \mathrm{rad/s} \) — the same answer from a completely different route, which is the check worth doing. A shaft that sags twenty microns is nowhere near resonance.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

On a rotating shaft carrying a fixed transverse load and a steadily driven torque, the bending and torsional stresses are respectively

A surface fibre travels from the tension side to the compression side once per revolution, so bending is fully reversed — that is what rotation does to a fixed load. The torque, by contrast, is applied continuously by the driver and does not depend on angular position, so the shear stress is steady. This asymmetry is the entire reason the shaft equation puts \( M_a \) over \( S_e \) and \( T_m \) over \( S_{ut} \) rather than treating both loads alike.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

In the DE-Goodman shaft equation, the torque term is divided by \( S_{ut} \) rather than \( S_e \) because

The denominators are not a ranking of which load is nastier — they follow directly from the Goodman criterion of Lesson 4, where the amplitude term carries \( S_e \) and the mean term carries \( S_{ut} \). A steady torque contributes only mean stress, so it lands in the second term. If the torque did fluctuate, its alternating part would move into the \( S_e \) term alongside the bending. The \( \sqrt3 \) is a separate matter: it converts a shear stress to its von Mises equivalent.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A shaft sized for a factor of safety of 2 is re-specified for a factor of safety of 4, all loads unchanged. The diameter increases by about

\( d \propto n^{1/3} \), so doubling \( n \) multiplies the diameter by \( 2^{1/3}=1.26 \) — a 26% increase. The 100% answer treats the relation as linear and 41% uses a square root. This cube-root behaviour is why fatigue margin on a shaft is unusually cheap to buy: doubling the margin costs a quarter more diameter and about 59% more steel by weight, which on a pump shaft is a trivial sum against the cost of a failure.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

A shaft passes its fatigue check comfortably but gears mounted on it mesh badly and bearings run hot. The most likely omission is

These are stiffness symptoms, not strength ones. Bearing slope is normally held near 0.001 rad and gear-mesh deflection to a fraction of a module, and neither limit appears anywhere in a stress calculation. The reason a stronger steel does not help is worth holding onto: deflection depends on \( E \) and \( I \), and every steel has essentially the same modulus, so the only fixes are more diameter or a shorter span.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Two shafts are identical except that one is made from a steel with twice the yield strength. Their first critical speeds are

Critical speed follows \( \omega_n=\sqrt{k/m} \) with \( k=48EI/L^3 \) — elastic modulus, second moment of area, span and mass. Strength does not appear. Since all steels share a modulus near 200 GPa, the stronger steel gives essentially the same critical speed. The same reasoning explains why a resonance problem is never solved by a material upgrade: change the geometry, the span, or the mass.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A 40 mm shaft on 500 mm simple supports deflects 0.0196 mm under its central mass. Its first critical speed is closest to

Using \( \omega_n=\sqrt{g/\delta}=\sqrt{9.81/1.96\times10^{-5}}=707\ \mathrm{rad/s} \), then converting: \( 707\times60/2\pi = 6750\ \mathrm{rpm} \). The 700 rpm answer reports the rad/s figure as though it were rpm, which is the standard trap in this calculation and worth a deliberate habit of checking. 1450 rpm is the operating speed, not a result, and 107 rpm would follow from dividing by \( 2\pi \) twice.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A shaft calculation returns a fatigue margin of 1.6 against a target of 2.0. Which change buys the most fatigue strength for the least cost?

The critical section is governed by \( K_f \) and by surface finish, so a generous fillet radius plus a ground finish attacks both at once — Lesson 3 measured grinding alone at about 11% on \( S_e \), for one extra operation. A higher-strength steel raises \( S_{ut} \) but its rougher-surface and size knockdowns scale too, so the net gain in \( S_e \) is much smaller than the datasheet suggests. Shortening the span helps stiffness and moment but is usually fixed by the machine layout. Raising \( n \) in the calculation changes the requirement, not the shaft.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Why is the critical section for a shaft not always the section of maximum bending moment?

What governs is the product of the nominal stress and the concentration factor at each candidate section. A keyway with \( K_f \) above 2, sitting where the moment is 80% of peak, beats a smooth section at 100% of peak. This is why sizing a shaft means checking every discontinuity — shoulders, keyways, grooves, press-fit ends — rather than the single worst point on the bending-moment diagram. Torque being constant is true on a simple shaft but does not bear on where bending governs.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the shaft that was strong enough and still unusable. A cooling-water pump is re-rated to a higher duty and the shaft is re-sized properly: reversed bending in the amplitude term, steady torque in the mean term, a fatigue margin of two, a ground fillet at the critical shoulder. The calculation is sound and the shaft never cracks. What it does instead is chew through mechanical seals, three in the first year. The cause is not strength at all. The re-rate moved the pump away from its best efficiency point, which raised the radial load, and the larger deflection at the seal faces exceeded what the seal could track — a stiffness failure on a shaft whose stress calculation was faultless. The fix is a stiffer shaft, and stiffness scales with \( d^4 \) and with \( E \), so a stronger steel would have changed nothing; the diameter went up and the bearing span came down. The general lesson is the one the strength calculation cannot tell you: passing the fatigue check answers whether the shaft breaks, not whether the machine works.
