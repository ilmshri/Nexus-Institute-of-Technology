Injection molding

The molding cycle: fill, pack, cool, eject. Gate and runner logic; shrinkage and warpage. Defects — shorts, flash, sinks — mapped to their process causes.

المحاضرة

§1 · The cycle is mostly waiting for heat to leave

An injection moulding cycle runs: close the mould, inject melt to fill the cavity, hold pressure while the part shrinks so more melt can be packed in, cool until the part is rigid enough to handle, open, eject. Filling takes about a second. Cooling takes most of the rest, and it obeys a law with one enormously consequential feature.

Key relation — cooling time

\[ t_{\text{cool}}=\frac{h^{2}}{\pi^{2}\alpha} \ln\!\left[\frac{4}{\pi}\,\frac{T_m-T_w}{T_e-T_w}\right] \]

\( h \) is the wall thickness, \( \alpha \) the polymer's thermal diffusivity, \( T_m \) the melt, \( T_w \) the mould wall and \( T_e \) the ejection temperature. Thickness enters squared; every temperature enters only inside a logarithm.

For a 3 mm wall at 230°C melt, a 40°C mould and 90°C ejection, the logarithm is 1.58 and \( t_{\text{cool}}=14.4\ \mathrm{s} \). Add a second to fill, five to pack and three to open and eject, and the cycle is 23.4 s — 154 parts an hour.

Now halve the wall. Cooling falls to 3.6 s, packing shortens with it, and the cycle becomes 10.6 s: 340 parts an hour from the same machine and the same tool cavity count. That factor of 2.2 is why wall thickness is the first thing a moulding engineer looks at on a new drawing, and why "just make it thicker to be safe" is the most expensive sentence in plastic part design. Note also what the logarithm implies: running the mould colder or the melt hotter changes the cycle hardly at all, because those live inside a log while thickness is squared outside it.

§2 · Sizing the machine, and what the gate decides

Two numbers select a moulding machine. Clamp force must exceed the cavity pressure acting over the projected area, or the mould opens against it.

Key relation — clamp force

\[ F=p_{\text{cav}}\,A_{\text{proj}} \]

\( A_{\text{proj}} \) is the part's area projected onto the parting plane, including runners. Cavity pressures of 20–50 MPa are typical.

A 150 × 100 mm plate at 35 MPa needs \( 35\times15{,}000=525\ \mathrm{kN} \), 53.5 tonnes, so a 65-tonne machine with margin. The other number is shot size: 45 cm³ of part plus about 12 cm³ of sprue and runner, and a machine should use roughly a fifth to four-fifths of its shot capacity — too little and the melt sits degrading in the barrel, too much and there is no cushion to pack with.

The gate is the small restriction where melt enters the cavity, and its size sets something that is easy to miss: how long you are allowed to pack. A gate is just a thin section, so it obeys the same cooling law, and once it freezes no more melt can enter however hard the machine pushes.

A 1.0 mm gate freezes in 1.6 s. A 2.0 mm gate takes 6.4 s — four times as long, by the same square law. So a small gate degates cleanly and leaves a neat witness mark, and it closes the packing window that controls shrinkage; a large gate packs well and leaves a scar someone has to trim. That is the trade, and it is quantitative rather than a matter of taste.

Between sprue and gate sits the runner. A cold runner solidifies with the part and is scrap — 12 cm³ against a 45 cm³ part is 21% of every shot, recoverable as regrind only where the application allows it. A hot runner keeps the melt molten right up to the gate, so there is no runner at all, at the price of a much more expensive and more temperamental tool. The choice is the same fixed-versus-variable crossover as Lesson 4's.

§3 · Shrinkage is easy; differential shrinkage is the problem

Polymers shrink as they cool, and semi-crystalline grades shrink far more than amorphous ones — 1.5 to 2.5% linear for polypropylene and polyethylene against 0.4 to 0.7% for polystyrene or ABS — because crystallising packs the chains closer.

Key relation — cutting the mould oversize

\[ L_{\text{mould}}=\frac{L_{\text{part}}}{1-s} \]

A 150 mm feature in polypropylene at \( s=1.5\% \) needs the steel cut at 152.28 mm. Uniform shrinkage is a tooling calculation, and a solved problem.

What is not solved is differential shrinkage, because a part that shrinks unevenly does not come out smaller — it comes out bent. Three things make shrinkage uneven: wall thickness varying across the part, cooling reaching one face faster than the other, and orientation.

Orientation is the one that answers why gate location decides warpage. Melt flowing into a cavity aligns its molecules — and, in a filled grade, its fibres — along the flow direction. Aligned fibres restrain shrinkage along the flow and do nothing about it across the flow, so shrinkage becomes anisotropic: a 30% glass-filled polypropylene might shrink 0.3% along the flow and 0.9% across it, a factor of three.

On a 150 mm square plate gated at one edge, that is 0.45 mm of shrinkage along the flow against 1.35 mm across it — a 0.9 mm differential, which the part resolves by bowing. Move the gate to the centre and the flow becomes radial, so the anisotropy becomes radial against hoop and the part dishes or saddles instead. The gate did not change the material or the shrinkage; it changed the flow field, and the flow field sets the orientation that sets the differential. That causal chain — gate, flow, orientation, differential shrinkage, warp — is why gate position is a structural decision rather than a convenience.

§4 · Reading the defects backwards

Moulding defects are diagnosable because each one is a specific failure of one phase of the cycle.

Short shot — the cavity did not fill. The melt either could not get there or ran out: too little shot volume, too little injection pressure or speed, melt too cold, a gate too small for the section, or a blocked vent leaving trapped air with nowhere to go. That last cause is worth naming, because it produces a short shot that no amount of extra pressure will fix.

Flash — melt escaped at the parting line, which means the mould opened. Either the clamp force was below \( p_{\text{cav}}A_{\text{proj}} \), or the injection pressure drove \( p_{\text{cav}} \) above what was assumed, or the parting-line steel is worn. On the worked part, a machine set at 450 kN against a 525 kN requirement will flash on every shot, and no process adjustment short of reducing pressure — which risks a short shot — will stop it.

Sink marks and voids — a region shrank after the surface had frozen and nothing fed it. The cause is almost always a local section thicker than the wall around it. A rib or boss joins the wall at a junction whose effective thickness is greater than either, and thickness is squared in the cooling law: a junction 1.3 times the wall thickness stays molten 1.69 times as long as the skin around it. Hence the design rule that a rib should be 0.5 to 0.6 of the wall — 1.8 mm on a 3 mm wall — and hence the process causes too: packing pressure or time too low, or a gate that froze before packing finished, which returns to \( \S2 \).

Weld lines form where two flow fronts meet, around a hole or between two gates, and carry 40 to 90% of the bulk strength depending on how hot the fronts still were. They cannot be eliminated where the part has a hole; they can be placed, by choosing the gate. Burn marks are trapped air compressed and ignited at the last point to fill, and are a venting problem rather than a temperature one. And warpage is \( \S3 \).

The pattern in all of this is that the cycle's phases map onto the defects one to one: filling failures give shorts and burns, clamping failures give flash, packing failures give sinks and voids, and cooling and orientation failures give warp. Diagnosing a moulding problem is mostly a matter of asking which phase the symptom belongs to before touching a setting.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

What the wall thickness is worth

Problem. A part is moulded from a 230°C melt into a 40°C mould and ejected at 90°C, with \( \alpha=10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2/s} \). Find the cooling time for a 3.0 mm wall and for a 1.5 mm wall. Taking 1 s to fill, 5 s of packing at 3 mm and 3 s at 1.5 mm, and 3 s to open and eject, find each cycle time and hourly rate. Then say what running the mould 10°C colder would do, and why.

Solution. (1) The logarithm is common to both: \( \ln[\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{230-40}{90-40}]=\ln[1.273(3.80)]=\ln(4.838)=1.577 \).

(2) At \( h=3.0 \) mm: \( t=\frac{(3\times10^{-3})^{2}}{\pi^{2}(10^{-7})}(1.577) =9.119(1.577)=14.4\ \mathrm{s} \).

(3) At \( h=1.5 \) mm: \( t=2.280(1.577)=3.6\ \mathrm{s} \) — a quarter, because thickness is squared.

(4) Cycles: \( 1+5+14.4+3=23.4\ \mathrm{s} \) and \( 1+3+3.6+3=10.6\ \mathrm{s} \).

(5) Rates: \( 3600/23.4=154 \) and \( 3600/10.6=340 \) parts an hour — a factor of 2.21.

(6) A 30°C mould instead of 40°C changes the logarithm to \( \ln[1.273(200/60)]=\ln(4.244)=1.445 \), so the 3 mm cooling time falls from 14.4 to 13.2 s — about 8%. Temperatures sit inside a logarithm and thickness sits outside it, squared, so geometry dominates and process settings barely move the cycle.

Answer: 14.4 s and 3.6 s of cooling, 23.4 s and 10.6 s cycles, 154 and 340 parts an hour; a 10°C colder mould buys about 8%. Sanity check: the two levers differ by an order of magnitude in effect, which is the whole economics of the process — a designer who halves the wall doubles the factory, and a process engineer who chills the mould gains a few per cent and risks freezing the gate early. Note that the packing time shortens with the wall too, so the cycle gain is slightly better than cooling alone suggests.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Size the machine and choose the gate

Problem. A 150 × 100 × 3 mm plate is moulded at a cavity pressure of 35 MPa, with 12 cm³ of sprue and runner. Find the required clamp force in kilonewtons and tonnes, the shot volume, and the runner's share of it. Then find the packing window available with a 1.0 mm gate and with a 2.0 mm gate, and say which to choose for a part that must hold a tight dimension.

Solution. (1) Projected area \( =150\times100=15{,}000\ \mathrm{mm^2} \), so \( F=p_{\text{cav}}A_{\text{proj}}=35(15{,}000)=525{,}000\ \mathrm{N}=525\ \mathrm{kN} \).

(2) \( 525/9.81=53.5 \) tonnes, so a 65-tonne machine with sensible margin.

(3) Part volume \( =150(100)(3)=45{,}000\ \mathrm{mm^3}=45\ \mathrm{cm^3} \); shot \( =45+12=57\ \mathrm{cm^3} \).

(4) Runner share \( =12/57=21\% \) of every shot.

(5) A gate is a thin section obeying the same cooling law: a 1.0 mm gate freezes in \( 1.597\ \mathrm{s} \), a 2.0 mm gate in \( 6.390\ \mathrm{s} \) — four times longer, by the square.

(6) A tight dimension is held by packing, which compensates shrinkage, so the part needs the longer window: the 2.0 mm gate. The cost is a larger witness mark to trim and more sensitivity to the gate's own orientation effects.

Answer: 525 kN (53.5 t), a 57 cm³ shot of which the runner is 21%, packing windows of 1.6 s and 6.4 s; choose the 2.0 mm gate for dimensional control. Sanity check: the gate is the smallest feature in the tool and it governs the phase that controls shrinkage, which is a good example of a process where the least conspicuous dimension is the most consequential. Note the runner figure too: 21% of every shot is a material cost that recurs forever, and it is the number a hot-runner proposal has to beat.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Why the plate bows, and what the gate has to do with it

Problem. A 150 mm square plate is moulded in a 30% glass-filled polypropylene that shrinks 0.3% along the flow and 0.9% across it. (a) Cut the mould for an unfilled polypropylene at 1.5% uniform shrinkage first, for comparison. (b) For the filled grade gated at one edge, find the shrinkage in each direction and the differential. (c) Explain what changes if the gate is moved to the centre. (d) A 3 mm wall carries a rib; give the maximum rib thickness and justify it from the cooling law.

Solution. (a) Uniform shrinkage is a tooling calculation: \( L_{\text{mould}}=150/(1-0.015)=152.28\ \mathrm{mm} \). Nothing warps, because every direction shrinks alike.

(b) Filled and edge-gated, the flow runs one way across the plate. Along the flow: \( 150(0.003)=0.45\ \mathrm{mm} \). Across it: \( 150(0.009)=1.35\ \mathrm{mm} \). The differential is \( 0.90\ \mathrm{mm} \), a factor of three between directions.

(c) A part cannot shrink by two different amounts and stay flat, so it bows. Moving the gate to the centre makes the flow radial: the anisotropy becomes radial against hoop, and the part dishes or saddles instead of bowing. The material, the shrinkage values and the wall are unchanged — only the flow field, and therefore the orientation, moved.

(d) Rib thickness \( \le0.5 \) to \( 0.6 \) of the wall, so \( \le1.8\ \mathrm{mm} \) on a 3 mm wall. The justification is the square law: a junction whose effective thickness is 1.3 times the wall stays molten \( 1.3^{2}=1.69 \) times as long as the skin around it, so it shrinks after the surface has frozen and pulls it in as a sink.

Answer: 152.28 mm of steel for the unfilled part; 0.45 and 1.35 mm of shrinkage with a 0.90 mm differential for the filled one; a centre gate converts a bow into a dish; ribs no thicker than 1.8 mm. Sanity check: uniform shrinkage is a solved problem and differential shrinkage is not, which is the distinction the whole subject turns on — and it explains why an unfilled part can be tooled from a table while a filled one usually needs a flow simulation. Note the causal chain in (c): the gate sets the flow, the flow sets the orientation, orientation sets the differential and the differential sets the warp, so gate position is a structural decision rather than a convenience.

In the cooling-time relation, wall thickness appears

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

Halving a part's wall from 3.0 mm to 1.5 mm takes the hourly output from about

Cooling falls from 14.4 to 3.6 s and packing shortens with it, so the cycle goes 23.4 to 10.6 s — 154 to 340 parts an hour, a factor of 2.21. It is not the full factor of four, because filling, opening and ejecting do not scale with the wall.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A 150 × 100 mm plate moulded at 35 MPa cavity pressure needs a clamp force of about

\( F=p_{\rm cav}A_{\rm proj}=35\ \mathrm{MPa}\times15{,}000\ \mathrm{mm^2} =525\ \mathrm{kN} \), or 53.5 tonnes — the tonnes figure is the one that gets confused with the kilonewtons. A machine set below it flashes on every shot.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Gate size matters most because it sets

A gate is a thin section obeying the same square law, so a 1.0 mm gate freezes in 1.6 s and a 2.0 mm gate in 6.4 s. Packing is what compensates shrinkage, so the gate silently decides how much dimensional control the process has — the smallest feature in the tool governing the most consequential phase.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A mould cavity for a 150 mm polypropylene part shrinking 1.5% should be cut at

\( L_{\rm mould}=L_{\rm part}/(1-s)=150/0.985=152.28 \) mm. The common slip is \( 150\times1.015=152.25 \), which is close but wrong in principle: the part shrinks from the mould dimension, so the mould is the larger quantity being divided into, not the smaller one being scaled up.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Gate location decides warpage because it decides

Flow aligns molecules and fibres, aligned fibres restrain shrinkage along the flow but not across it, and a part that shrinks 0.3% one way and 0.9% the other cannot stay flat. An edge gate on a 150 mm plate gives a 0.9 mm differential and a bow; a centre gate makes the flow radial and turns the bow into a dish.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A sink mark on the surface opposite a rib is caused by

Thickness is squared in the cooling law, so a junction 1.3 times the wall stays molten 1.69 times as long as the skin around it; it shrinks last, with nothing left to feed it, and pulls the surface in. Hence the rule that a rib is 0.5 to 0.6 of the wall — 1.8 mm on a 3 mm wall.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A part shows flash at the parting line on every shot. The cause is most likely

Flash means the mould opened, which is a force balance: the melt pushed harder over the projected area than the clamp held. On the worked part, 450 kN of clamp against a 525 kN requirement flashes every shot, and no setting short of reducing pressure — which risks a short shot — will stop it.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the rib that was added for stiffness. A moulded housing was failing a deflection test, so the design was revised with a stiffening rib the same thickness as the wall — 3 mm on a 3 mm wall — on the reasonable ground that a thicker rib is a stiffer rib. The next tool produced parts with a visible sink line on the show surface directly opposite the rib, and a cycle time that had grown by several seconds.

Both effects came from the same square law. A rib as thick as the wall makes a junction whose effective thickness is roughly 1.3 times the wall, and cooling goes as thickness squared, so that junction stayed molten \( 1.3^{2}=1.69 \) times as long as the skin around it. It therefore shrank after the surface had frozen, with the gate long since closed and nothing able to feed it, and pulled the surface in. The same thick junction also set the cycle: the part could not be ejected until its slowest region was rigid, so the whole machine waited on the rib.

Two practices follow. Size ribs at 0.5 to 0.6 of the wall — 1.8 mm here — and buy stiffness with rib height and count rather than thickness, since bending stiffness goes as the cube of depth and a taller thin rib beats a thicker short one. And treat any local thickening as a cycle-time decision as well as a cosmetic one: the slowest-cooling point in the part sets the cycle for every cavity in the tool, so one badly proportioned feature is paid for on every shot for the life of the mould.
