SOM 201 — Strength of Materials

SOM 201 · Lesson 01 · Stress and strain under axial load

Lesson 01Stress and strain under axial load

Key points

  • Stress makes force local — \( \sigma=F/A \) — so two parts carrying the same load can be in completely different danger.
  • Strain makes deformation dimensionless, \( \varepsilon=\delta/L \), which is what lets materials be compared at all.
  • Hooke's law \( \sigma=E\varepsilon \) is a contract valid only below the proportional limit — outside it, nothing that follows applies.
  • \( E \) is stiffness, not strength: it sets deflection, and every steel has essentially the same value regardless of grade.
  • The tensile curve is read as a sequence of commitments — proportional limit, yield, ultimate, fracture — each governing a different design question.
  • Yield governs permanent set, ultimate governs rupture, and choosing the wrong one is choosing the wrong failure mode.

SOM 201 · Lesson 01 · Stress and strain under axial load · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Statics — internal force from a free-body cut precedes any stress.
  • Areas of sections — \(A=\tfrac{\pi}{4}d^2\) for round bars.
  • Unit discipline — MPa = N/mm²; keep a consistent system.

Glossary

TermEquationWhen to use
Normal stress\( \sigma=P/A \)Axial force spread over a cross-section.
Normal strain\( \varepsilon=\delta/L \)Size-independent measure of stretch.
Hooke's law\( \sigma=E\varepsilon \)Elastic (straight-line) region only.
Axial deformation\( \delta=PL/AE \)Elongation of a uniform bar.
Factor of safety\( \sigma_y/\sigma_{allow} \)Margin held below yield.

SOM 201 · Lesson 02 · Axial members and thermal stress

Lesson 02Axial members and thermal stress

Key points

  • Bars in series share the load and add their deformations: \( \delta=\sum P_iL_i/A_iE_i \).
  • A problem is statically indeterminate when equilibrium alone cannot find the forces; compatibility of deformation supplies the missing equation.
  • Free thermal expansion \( \delta=\alpha L\Delta T \) produces strain but no stress at all.
  • Stress appears only when expansion is restrained: \( \sigma=E\alpha\Delta T \), independent of length.
  • That independence is the surprise — a short restrained bar is stressed exactly as hard as a long one.
  • It is why pipework needs loops and expansion joints, and why anchoring both ends of a hot line is a design error.

SOM 201 · Lesson 02 · Axial members and thermal stress · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Axial stress/strain (Lesson 1) — the per-segment ingredients.
  • Free-body cuts — to find each segment's internal force.
  • Equilibrium equations — half of every indeterminate problem.

Glossary

TermEquationWhen to use
Series deformation\( \delta=\sum P_iL_i/A_iE_i \)Stepped or composite bars.
Compatibilitygeometry of fitExtra equation for indeterminate bars.
Thermal strain\( \varepsilon_T=\alpha\Delta T \)Free temperature change, no stress.
Thermal stress\( \sigma=E\alpha\Delta T \)Fully restrained temperature change.

SOM 201 · Lesson 03 · Torsion of shafts

Lesson 03Torsion of shafts

Key points

  • Torsion of a circular shaft gives \( \tau=Tr/J \), maximum at the surface and zero on the axis.
  • Because the core carries so little, hollow shafts are far more material-efficient than solid ones.
  • Angle of twist \( \phi=TL/JG \) is the stiffness check, separate from the stress check.
  • Power, torque and speed connect through \( P=T\omega \) — the step that turns a motor rating into a shaft load.
  • Ductile shafts fail on the transverse plane of maximum shear; brittle ones on a 45° helix following the principal tension.
  • The fracture surface therefore reports the failure mode, which makes torsion the cleanest introduction to reading a broken part.

SOM 201 · Lesson 03 · Torsion of shafts · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Axial member logic (Lessons 1–2) — torsion mirrors \(\delta=PL/AE\).
  • Second moments of area — \(J=\pi d^4/32\) for solid circles.
  • Rotational kinematics — \(\omega=2\pi N/60\).

Glossary

TermEquationWhen to use
Torsion formula\( \tau=Tr/J \)Shear stress at radius \(r\).
Surface shear\( \tau_{max}=16T/\pi d^3 \)Solid-shaft sizing.
Angle of twist\( \varphi=TL/GJ \)End-to-end rotation.
Power–torque\( P=T\omega,\ \omega=2\pi N/60 \)Link machine rating to shaft torque.
Polar moment\( J=\pi d^4/32 \)Solid circular section.

SOM 201 · Lesson 04 · Bending and transverse shear in beams

Lesson 04Bending and transverse shear in beams

Key points

  • Plane sections remaining plane gives \( \sigma=My/I \): bending stress varies linearly through the depth and is zero at the neutral axis.
  • Section modulus \( S=I/c \) is the designer's currency — capacity in one number, so beams are chosen by \( S \) not by area.
  • Putting material far from the neutral axis is what makes an I-beam efficient.
  • Transverse shear \( \tau=VQ/It \) is largest at the neutral axis, exactly where bending stress is zero.
  • The two peaks are therefore in different places, which is why both must be checked.
  • Shear flow governs how a built-up section is fastened together — it sizes the welds, bolts or nails holding the flanges to the web.

SOM 201 · Lesson 04 · Bending and transverse shear in beams · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Shear and moment — internal \(V\) and \(M\) from statics.
  • Second moment of area — \(I=bh^3/12\) for rectangles.
  • The flexure logic — plane sections + Hooke's law.

Glossary

TermEquationWhen to use
Flexure formula\( \sigma=My/I \)Bending stress at height \(y\).
Section modulus\( S=I/c,\ \sigma_{max}=M/S \)Max bending stress; beam sizing.
Rect. properties\( I=bh^3/12,\ S=bh^2/6 \)Solid rectangular section.
Transverse shear\( \tau=VQ/Ib \)Shear stress, peaks at neutral axis.
Shear flow\( q=VQ/I \)Sizing fasteners in built-up beams.

SOM 201 · Lesson 05 · Beam deflection

Lesson 05Beam deflection

Key points

  • Deflection follows from curvature: \( EI\,d^2y/dx^2=M(x) \), integrated twice with boundary conditions.
  • The standard cantilever result \( \delta=PL^3/3EI \) shows the dominant dependence — length cubed.
  • Doubling a span multiplies deflection eightfold, which is why stiffness problems are almost always solved by shortening or deepening, not by strengthening.
  • Superposition works because the governing equation is linear — sum the deflections from each load separately.
  • Stiffness is a distinct failure mode: a beam can be perfectly safe in stress and useless in service.
  • Since \( E \) is fixed for a material class, geometry is the only real lever on deflection.

SOM 201 · Lesson 06 · Combined loading

Lesson 06Combined loading

Key points

  • Real components carry several stress types at once, and at a point they superpose — axial, bending and torsional contributions add on the same element.
  • For a shaft, \( \sigma=32M/\pi d^3 \) and \( \tau=16T/\pi d^3 \) act together on the surface element.
  • Superposition is valid only while everything stays elastic.
  • The critical point is hunted, not assumed: it is normally on the surface and at a discontinuity, and rarely where the moment is largest.
  • Bending and torsion peak at different locations around the circumference, so several candidate points must be checked.
  • The shaft-with-pulley pattern recurs throughout machine design and is the template for combining these stresses.

SOM 201 · Lesson 07 · Stress transformation and Mohr's circle

Lesson 07Stress transformation and Mohr's circle

Key points

  • Stress components depend on the axes chosen; the state at a point does not. Transformation converts between them.
  • Mohr's circle is the graphical engine: centre \( (\sigma_x+\sigma_y)/2 \), radius \( R=\sqrt{((\sigma_x-\sigma_y)/2)^2+\tau_{xy}^2} \).
  • Principal stresses are the extremes, \( \sigma_{1,2}=\text{centre}\pm R \), and on those planes the shear stress is zero.
  • Maximum shear equals \( R \) and occurs 45° from the principal planes — which is why ductile parts shear at 45° to the pull.
  • Angles on the circle are twice the physical angles, the single most common slip in using it.
  • Principal stresses are what every failure theory in the next lesson consumes, so this is the gateway to combined-load design.

SOM 201 · Lesson 08 · Pressure vessels: thin-walled theory

Lesson 08Pressure vessels: thin-walled theory

Key points

  • Thin-wall vessel stresses come straight from equilibrium on a cut, not from any elasticity theory.
  • \( \sigma_{hoop}=pr/t \), \( \sigma_{axial}=pr/2t \), so hoop stress is always exactly twice axial.
  • That fixed 2:1 ratio predicts the failure: a cylindrical vessel splits along a longitudinal seam, not a circumferential one.
  • It is also why longitudinal welds are the ones inspected hardest.
  • A sphere carries \( pr/2t \) in every direction, making it the most material-efficient pressure shape — and the most expensive to fabricate.
  • Thin-wall theory stops being honest beyond about \( r/t=10 \); below that, radial stress matters and thick-wall (Lamé) theory is required.

SOM 201 · Lesson 09 · Failure theories

Lesson 09Failure theories

Key points

  • A failure theory converts a multiaxial stress state into one equivalent number comparable with a tensile test result.
  • Tresca uses maximum shear, \( \sigma_{max}-\sigma_{min} \); von Mises uses distortion energy.
  • Von Mises predicts ductile yielding more accurately; Tresca is always the more conservative, by up to about 15%.
  • Pure shear is the clean test case: von Mises predicts yielding at \( 0.577S_y \), Tresca at \( 0.5S_y \).
  • That factor of \( 1/\sqrt3 \) is why a shear allowable is roughly 0.577 of the tensile one.
  • Always name the theory used — a factor of safety quoted without it is an incomplete statement.

SOM 201 · Lesson 10 · Buckling of columns

Lesson 10Buckling of columns

Key points

  • Buckling is an instability, not a strength failure: a column can buckle at a stress far below yield.
  • Euler's critical load is \( P_{cr}=\pi^2EI/L_e^2 \), depending on stiffness and geometry — material strength does not appear.
  • A stronger steel therefore buys nothing against buckling; a larger \( I \) or a shorter effective length does.
  • End conditions enter through effective length: pinned \( L_e=L \), fixed-fixed \( 0.5L \), fixed-free \( 2L \), a fourfold spread in capacity.
  • Slenderness ratio \( L_e/r \) decides the mode — slender columns buckle, stubby ones yield, and the crossover is where Euler stops applying.
  • Buckling gives no warning: it is sudden, sideways and complete, which is why its factor of safety is set higher than for yielding.

SOM 201 · Lesson 11 · Stress concentration and fatigue preview

Lesson 11Stress concentration and fatigue preview

Key points

  • A hole, fillet, groove or keyway multiplies the nominal stress locally: \( \sigma_{max}=K_t\sigma_{nom} \).
  • \( K_t \) is pure geometry — it depends on the shape and proportions of the discontinuity, not on the material.
  • The sharper the transition, the higher the factor, which is why fillet radius is a structural dimension and not a drafting convenience.
  • A keyway is among the worst offenders and sits exactly where torque is transferred, so it usually defines the critical section of a shaft.
  • Notch sensitivity \( q \) is the metallurgy half: it converts the geometric \( K_t \) into the fatigue factor \( K_f=1+q(K_t-1) \).
  • Ductile materials under static load can blunt the peak by local yielding, so \( K_t \) is often set aside there.
  • That permission is withdrawn entirely for brittle materials and under fatigue — the handshake into Machine Design's fatigue lessons.
