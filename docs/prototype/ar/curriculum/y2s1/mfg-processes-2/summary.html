MFG 154 — Manufacturing Processes II — Machining & Joining

MFG 154 · Lesson 01 · Theory of metal machining

Lesson 01Theory of metal machining

Key points

  • Orthogonal cutting reduces machining to two dimensions, where the chip forms by shear on a single plane rather than by anything resembling splitting.
  • Chip thickness ratio gives the shear angle, and the shear angle governs force, temperature and chip form.
  • Merchant's circle resolves the resultant force into cutting, thrust, shear and friction components consistently.
  • Nearly all the cutting work becomes heat, split between the shear zone, the tool-chip interface and the flank.
  • Taylor's tool-life equation \( VT^n=C \) says cutting speed dominates tool life far more strongly than feed or depth.
  • Read as economics, that exponent is why raising speed for output can cost more in tooling and downtime than it earns.

MFG 154 · Lesson 02 · Cutting tools and fluids

Lesson 02Cutting tools and fluids

Key points

  • Every tool material trades hardness against toughness: HSS is tough and slow, carbide harder and faster, ceramics and CBN harder still and more fragile.
  • The trade is unavoidable, so tool selection is a duty question — interrupted cuts want toughness, continuous finishing wants hardness.
  • Wear modes are diagnostic. Flank wear is normal and predictable; crater wear signals diffusion at excessive temperature.
  • Built-up edge signals too low a speed, chipping signals interruption or vibration, and thermal cracking signals inconsistent coolant.
  • An insert designation encodes shape, clearance, tolerance, size, nose radius and chipbreaker — it is readable, not a part number to memorise.
  • Cutting fluid cools, lubricates and flushes chips; which job dominates depends on speed, and dry or minimum-quantity machining is now a real option.

MFG 154 · Lesson 03 · Turning, milling, and drilling

Lesson 03Turning, milling, and drilling

Key points

  • Turning arithmetic runs from cutting speed to spindle speed through \( N=1000V/\pi D \), then to feed rate and material removal rate.
  • Milling adds the tooth count: feed per tooth times teeth times spindle speed gives table feed, and chip thickness varies through the cut.
  • Up-milling and down-milling behave differently in force direction, chip formation and how they load the machine's backlash.
  • Drilling is a special case in which cutting speed falls to zero at the centre of the web, which is why the chisel edge extrudes rather than cuts.
  • Machining time follows from length divided by feed rate, and it is what actually costs money.
  • Workholding must resist cutting forces without distorting the part — a thin-walled component clamped hard is machined round and springs oval.

MFG 154 · Lesson 04 · Machine tools and CNC basics

Lesson 04Machine tools and CNC basics

Key points

  • A lathe rotates the work and feeds the tool; a machining centre rotates the tool and moves the work through several axes.
  • Machine anatomy — spindle, ways, ballscrews, turret or tool changer — is what determines achievable accuracy.
  • G-code commands motion and M-code commands machine functions; the important habit is being able to read a program, not write one blind.
  • Accuracy is closeness to the commanded value; repeatability is consistency between attempts, and they are independent.
  • A repeatable machine can be compensated; an unrepeatable one cannot be, at any price.
  • Backlash, thermal growth and way wear are machine-health parameters a maintenance engineer owns directly.

MFG 154 · Lesson 05 · Grinding and abrasive processes

Lesson 05Grinding and abrasive processes

Key points

  • Grinding cuts with many small, randomly oriented abrasive grains at high negative rake, so it removes material inefficiently by design.
  • Specific energy is far higher than in turning or milling, and nearly all of it becomes heat at the surface.
  • That is why abused grinding burns parts — thermal damage, tempering and re-hardened white layer are grinding defects, not material defects.
  • A wheel designation is readable: abrasive type, grit size, grade, structure and bond.
  • Dressing restores wheel form and exposes fresh grain; a glazed wheel rubs and heats instead of cutting.
  • Wheel balancing matters because an unbalanced wheel at speed leaves chatter marks and destroys spindle bearings.

MFG 154 · Lesson 06 · Surface finish and integrity

Lesson 06Surface finish and integrity

Key points

  • Ra is the arithmetic mean roughness and Rz the mean peak-to-valley height; Ra is common but insensitive to isolated deep scratches.
  • For an idealised triangular profile Ra works out to a quarter of the peak height — a useful check on any measurement.
  • Each process has a capability band, so specifying a finish tighter than the process can hold silently adds an operation.
  • Machining leaves residual stress: gentle cutting tends to leave compression, abusive grinding leaves tension.
  • Tensile residual stress at a surface directly reduces fatigue life, which is why surface integrity matters more than the roughness number alone.
  • A bearing journal's ground finish is therefore a reliability parameter — it sets film formation and fatigue resistance together.

MFG 154 · Lesson 07 · Nontraditional machining

Lesson 07Nontraditional machining

Key points

  • Non-traditional processes remove material without a harder cutting tool, which is what makes them work on hardened and exotic materials.
  • EDM erodes by controlled sparks in dielectric; the workpiece must be conductive, and there is no cutting force at all.
  • That zero force is why EDM machines delicate and thin features that would deflect under a milling cutter.
  • EDM leaves a recast layer, which matters for fatigue-critical parts; wire EDM cuts complex profiles through full thickness.
  • ECM dissolves anodically with no heat-affected zone or tool wear, but needs a shaped tool and handles the electrolyte as a process problem.
  • Laser suits thin sheet and fine detail, waterjet cuts anything with no heat at all — select on material, thickness, tolerance and whether a heat-affected zone is acceptable.

MFG 154 · Lesson 08 · Welding processes I — arc processes

Lesson 08Welding processes I — arc processes

Key points

  • Arc welding is an electrical circuit whose load is the arc: current sets melting rate, voltage sets arc length and bead profile.
  • SMAW is portable and tolerant, GMAW fast and cleaner, FCAW combines flux protection with continuous wire.
  • GTAW gives the highest quality and the lowest deposition rate, so it is chosen for root passes and thin or reactive material.
  • SAW buries the arc under flux for very high deposition on long, thick, flat joints.
  • Heat input \( H=60VI/(1000v) \) governs cooling rate, and cooling rate governs the microstructure of the heat-affected zone.
  • Defects map to causes — porosity to contamination, undercut to excess current or wrong angle, lack of fusion to insufficient heat or poor technique.

MFG 154 · Lesson 09 · Welding II, thermal cutting, and joint preparation

Lesson 09Welding II, thermal cutting, and joint preparation

Key points

  • Resistance welding melts at the interface by \( I^2Rt \), needing no filler and giving very short cycle times.
  • Spot welding's variables are current, time and electrode force, and electrode condition is the quiet one that drifts.
  • Friction welding joins in the solid state, so it avoids solidification defects entirely and joins dissimilar metals that cannot be fusion welded.
  • Brazing and soldering do not melt the parent metal at all — the filler wets and is drawn in by capillary action, so joint clearance is the design variable.
  • The distinction is temperature: above about 450 °C is brazing, below is soldering.
  • Thermal cutting leaves an edge condition that becomes the next weld's joint preparation — fit-up and cleanliness quietly decide weld quality more often than technique does.

MFG 154 · Lesson 10 · Mechanical joining

Lesson 10Mechanical joining

Key points

  • A bolted joint holds by clamping friction from preload, not by the bolt acting as a pin in shear.
  • Preload cannot be measured with a hand tool, so torque is used as a proxy: \( T=KDF \).
  • The nut factor \( K \) lumps thread and under-head friction and is an assumption, typically 0.15–0.20 for as-received steel.
  • It is not a constant: plating, condition and especially lubrication move it, so a lubricated bolt torqued to a dry spec is significantly overtightened.
  • Gasketed flanges leak in both directions — too little preload and the joint separates, too much and the gasket crushes.
  • Riveting and adhesive bonding trade differently: rivets are inspectable and permanent, adhesives spread load and join dissimilar materials but depend on surface preparation.

MFG 154 · Lesson 11 · Additive manufacturing first contact

Lesson 11Additive manufacturing first contact

Key points

  • Additive manufacturing builds up layer by layer, which inverts the usual rule that complexity costs money.
  • The families differ fundamentally: powder bed fusion, material extrusion, vat photopolymerisation and directed energy deposition.
  • Properties are anisotropic because the build direction is a material direction — a part is weaker across layers than along them.
  • Build orientation is therefore a design decision, not a printing setting.
  • Support structures, surface finish and post-processing usually dominate the real cost and lead time.
  • AM wins on low volume, high complexity, internal passages and consolidated assemblies — and loses badly on simple parts in quantity.
