Power screws

Torque to raise and lower; efficiency and self-locking conditions derived from the friction analysis. Acme threads and collar friction. Jacks, actuators, and valve stems quantified.

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Derive when a power screw self-locks.
  • Why is a screw jack's efficiency so poor — and why is that useful?

01 · The inclined plane, wrapped

Unwrap one turn of a screw thread and it is a right triangle: base \( \pi d_m \), height the lead \( l \), and the thread's helix as the hypotenuse. Raising a load on a screw is therefore the classic problem of pushing a block up an incline against friction, and the torque follows from resolving forces on that plane. The lead angle is what the triangle defines: \( \tan\lambda = l/\pi d_m \).

Note the distinction between lead and pitch, which is the most common early error. Pitch is the distance between adjacent threads; lead is the axial travel per revolution. They are equal only for a single-start thread. A double-start thread of the same pitch has twice the lead, and therefore twice the travel per turn, a larger lead angle, and — as the diagram shows — meaningfully better efficiency.

02 · Torque to raise and to lower

Resolving on the inclined plane with a thread half-angle \( \alpha \) gives the two working equations. The thread angle enters through \( \sec\alpha \) because a V-form or Acme thread wedges the flanks together, increasing the normal force and therefore the friction — a square thread, with \( \alpha = 0 \), is the most efficient form and the hardest to make.

Key relation — power screw torque

\[ T_R=\frac{Fd_m}{2}\left[\frac{l+\pi\mu d_m\sec\alpha}{\pi d_m-\mu l\sec\alpha}\right], \qquad T_L=\frac{Fd_m}{2}\left[\frac{\pi\mu d_m\sec\alpha-l}{\pi d_m+\mu l\sec\alpha}\right] \]

The two differ only in signs — friction opposes motion, so it adds when raising and subtracts when lowering. If \( T_L \) comes out positive, torque is needed to lower the load and the screw is self-locking.

03 · Self-locking, and what it costs

A screw is self-locking when the load cannot drive it backwards — release the handle and the jack stays up. Setting \( T_L>0 \) and simplifying gives a condition that involves no load, no diameter and no torque, only geometry and friction:

Key relation — self-locking condition

\[ \mu > \tan\lambda\cos\alpha \]

Independent of the load entirely. A self-locking screw holds a light load and a heavy one equally well — but the condition depends on \( \mu \), which is not a constant.

The worked screw needs \( \mu > 0.064 \) and has 0.15, a margin of 2.35. That margin is the thing to watch, because it is a margin on friction. Flooding a self-locking screw with the wrong lubricant, or letting a stem polish itself over years of service, moves the one number the safety property depends on. A screw that has been self-locking for a decade can stop being so without any visible change.

And the property is expensive. Because self-locking requires \( \tan\lambda \) below the friction coefficient, efficiency in that region is bounded — at the boundary itself it is 48.9%, and it falls away rapidly below. The worked screw runs at about 30%. Two-thirds of the work put into the handle is heating the threads. That is the price of a mechanism that holds its position with no brake, no ratchet and no power, and for a valve stem or a jack it is usually worth paying.

04 · The collar, which is often the larger loss

A screw must react its axial load against something, and that thrust face — a collar, a thrust washer, sometimes a plain shoulder — has friction of its own. It contributes no useful work whatever, only torque:

Key relation — collar torque and overall efficiency

\[ T_c=\frac{F\mu_c d_c}{2}, \qquad e=\frac{Fl}{2\pi(T_R+T_c)} \]

Note that \( T_c \) scales with the collar diameter, not the screw's, and that it appears in the efficiency denominator without appearing in the numerator.

On the worked screw the collar contributes 67.5 N·m against the thread's 80.9 — 46% of the total torque — and takes overall efficiency from 29.5% down to 16.1%. This is the routine situation, not an extreme one, and it points at the cheapest available improvement: a rolling thrust bearing in place of a plain collar can cut \( \mu_c \) by an order of magnitude and very nearly double the efficiency of the whole assembly, without touching the screw at all. When a power screw runs hot or needs an unreasonable handle force, look at the thrust face before redesigning the thread.

Closing. A screw is an inclined plane, so friction sets everything. Self-locking is a geometry-and-friction condition that caps efficiency below 50% and depends on a coefficient that drifts. And check the collar — it is frequently the bigger half of the torque.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Friction on an inclined plane (Statics L5) — the model the whole screw analysis is built from.
  • Coefficient of friction (Statics) — including that it is an empirical band, not a material constant.
  • Torque, work and efficiency (Physics I) — work in per revolution is \( 2\pi T \), work out is \( Fl \).
  • Thread geometry (Drawing & CAD L8) — pitch, lead, starts, and the Acme form.
  • Bearing pressure (Lesson 6) — the check on thread flank pressure that governs wear life.

Glossary

TermEquationWhen to use
Mean diameter\( d_m=d-p/2 \)The diameter every screw formula uses; not the nominal size.
Lead\( l = n_{starts}\times p \)Axial travel per revolution; equals pitch only for a single start.
Lead angle\( \tan\lambda=l/\pi d_m \)The unwrapped incline; sets both efficiency and self-locking.
Raising torque\( T_R=\frac{Fd_m}{2}\frac{l+\pi\mu d_m\sec\alpha}{\pi d_m-\mu l\sec\alpha} \)Lifting or clamping; friction adds.
Lowering torque\( T_L=\frac{Fd_m}{2}\frac{\pi\mu d_m\sec\alpha-l}{\pi d_m+\mu l\sec\alpha} \)Releasing; positive result means self-locking.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Torque to raise a load

Problem. A single-start Acme power screw is 32 mm nominal with 6 mm pitch, so \( d_m = 29\ \mathrm{mm} \) and \( l = 6\ \mathrm{mm} \). The thread half-angle is \( \alpha = 14.5° \) (\( \sec\alpha = 1.0329 \)) and \( \mu = 0.15 \). It raises 25 kN. Find: the lead angle and the torque required at the thread.

Solution. (1) \( \tan\lambda = l/\pi d_m = 6/(\pi\times29) = 0.06586 \), so \( \lambda = 3.77° \). (2) Numerator: \( l+\pi\mu d_m\sec\alpha = 6 + \pi(0.15)(29)(1.0329) = 6+14.115 = 20.115\ \mathrm{mm} \). (3) Denominator: \( \pi d_m-\mu l\sec\alpha = 91.106-0.930 = 90.177\ \mathrm{mm} \). (4) \( T_R = \frac{25{,}000(0.029)}{2}\times\frac{20.115}{90.177} = 362.5\times0.22307 = 80.9\ \mathrm{N\,m} \).

Answer: \( \lambda = 3.77° \), \( T_R = 80.9\ \mathrm{N\,m} \). Sanity check: a frictionless screw would need only \( Fl/2\pi = 25{,}000(0.006)/2\pi = 23.9\ \mathrm{N\,m} \), so friction is consuming about 70% of the input — consistent with the 30% efficiency this screw turns out to have, and a useful independent check on the torque figure.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Does it hold, and how much margin is there?

Problem. For the same screw, find: the torque required to lower the load, whether the screw is self-locking, the minimum friction coefficient that keeps it so, and the margin on \( \mu \).

Solution. (1) Numerator: \( \pi\mu d_m\sec\alpha - l = 14.115-6 = 8.115\ \mathrm{mm} \). (2) Denominator: \( \pi d_m+\mu l\sec\alpha = 91.106+0.930 = 92.036\ \mathrm{mm} \). (3) \( T_L = 362.5\times(8.115/92.036) = 32.0\ \mathrm{N\,m} \), positive — torque is needed to lower, so the screw is self-locking. (4) Condition: \( \mu > \tan\lambda\cos\alpha = 0.06586(0.96815) = 0.0638 \). (5) Margin: \( 0.15/0.0638 = 2.35 \).

Answer: \( T_L = 32.0\ \mathrm{N\,m} \); self-locking, needing \( \mu>0.064 \) against an actual 0.15 — a margin of 2.35. Sanity check: the self-locking criterion contains no load term, and indeed nothing in step 4 refers to the 25 kN — the screw would hold 2.5 kN or 250 kN equally. What it does depend on is \( \mu \), so the honest statement is that this screw is self-locking while its friction coefficient stays above 0.064, which is a maintenance condition rather than a permanent property.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

What the collar costs

Problem. The same screw reacts its 25 kN thrust through a plain collar of mean diameter 45 mm with \( \mu_c = 0.12 \). Find: the collar torque, the total raising torque, and the efficiency with and without the collar.

Solution. (1) \( T_c = F\mu_cd_c/2 = 25{,}000(0.12)(0.045)/2 = 67.5\ \mathrm{N\,m} \). (2) Total: \( T = 80.9+67.5 = 148.4\ \mathrm{N\,m} \). (3) Screw only: \( e = Fl/2\pi T_R = 25{,}000(0.006)/(2\pi\times80.9) = 150/508.1 = 29.5\% \). (4) With collar: \( e = 150/(2\pi\times148.4) = 150/932.2 = 16.1\% \).

Answer: \( T_c = 67.5\ \mathrm{N\,m} \), total 148.4 N·m; efficiency falls from 29.5% to 16.1%. Sanity check: the collar is 46% of the total torque while doing none of the work, which is why it nearly halves the efficiency. It is worth noticing that \( T_c \) depends on the collar diameter — at 45 mm against the screw's 29 mm mean, the thrust face has the longer moment arm. Replacing it with a rolling thrust bearing at \( \mu_c\approx0.01 \) would cut \( T_c \) to about 5.6 N·m and lift overall efficiency back to roughly 27.6%.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

For a double-start thread of 6 mm pitch, the lead is

Lead is axial travel per revolution, \( l = n_{starts}\times p = 2(6) = 12\ \mathrm{mm} \). Pitch — the distance between adjacent thread crests — stays at 6 mm; that is the distinction the two terms exist to make, and confusing them is the most common error in screw calculations. A double start doubles the lead angle's tangent, which raises efficiency substantially but moves the screw toward losing self-locking.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

The self-locking condition \( \mu>\tan\lambda\cos\alpha \) contains no load term. This means

Load cancels out of the derivation entirely: it raises both the driving and the resisting effect in the same proportion. So a self-locking screw holds 2 kN and 200 kN alike. What the condition does depend on is \( \mu \), an empirical quantity that shifts with lubricant, surface finish and wear — which is why a mechanism can lose self-locking years after installation without any dimension having changed.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

The maximum efficiency achievable by a self-locking power screw is

Self-locking requires \( \tan\lambda \) below the friction coefficient, and evaluating the efficiency expression right at that boundary gives 48.9% — approaching but never reaching 50%, for any \( \mu \). Better lubrication does not escape the trade-off: reducing \( \mu \) raises efficiency at a given lead angle but simultaneously lowers the lead angle at which self-locking is lost. Roughly 75% is the ceiling for a non-locking screw at a large lead angle.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Collar friction on a power screw

The collar reacts thrust; it lifts nothing. So \( T_c \) adds to the input torque while the useful output \( Fl \) is unchanged, and efficiency falls. Nor is it small: collars are typically larger in diameter than the screw's mean diameter, and on the worked example the collar supplies 46% of the total torque. Neglecting it produces an efficiency figure roughly double the truth.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A square thread is more efficient than an Acme thread of the same size because

A flank angle wedges the mating threads together, raising the normal force above the axial load by \( \sec\alpha \) and raising the friction force with it. A square thread has \( \alpha=0 \), so \( \sec\alpha=1 \) and that penalty disappears. Acme survives in general use anyway because the flank angle makes the thread far easier to cut and to fit with an adjustable split nut — an efficiency loss traded for manufacturability.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A screw raising 25 kN at 6 mm lead requires 80.9 N·m at the thread. The efficiency of the thread alone is closest to

Work out per revolution is \( Fl = 25{,}000(0.006) = 150\ \mathrm{J} \); work in is \( 2\pi T_R = 2\pi(80.9) = 508\ \mathrm{J} \); the ratio is 29.5%. The 16% answer is the overall efficiency once the collar is included — correct for the assembly, but not for the thread alone, and the distinction is worth stating explicitly whenever an efficiency is quoted. Roughly 49% is the self-locking ceiling, not this screw's performance.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A valve stem that has been reliably self-locking for years begins to drift open after a change of stem lubricant. The most likely explanation is

Self-locking depends on \( \mu \) exceeding a fixed geometric threshold — 0.064 for the worked screw. A lower-friction lubricant moves the one parameter the property rests on, and once \( \mu \) crosses that line the load can drive the stem backwards. Wear does not change the lead angle appreciably, and a higher load would not matter since the condition is load-independent. The general point is that changing a lubricant on a self-locking mechanism is a design change.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A power screw assembly runs hot and needs an excessive handle force. Of the following, the change most likely to give the largest improvement is

The collar routinely supplies something like half the total torque while doing none of the useful work — 46% on this lesson's screw. Cutting \( \mu_c \) from 0.12 to roughly 0.01 with a rolling element takes the collar torque from 67.5 to about 5.6 N·m and nearly doubles the assembly's efficiency, without touching the thread. A larger screw diameter would actually increase both thread and collar torque, and hardness does not change friction in any dependable way.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the gate valve that would not stay shut. A large gate valve on a utility header is found partly open on a routine round, having been closed and signed off two days earlier. No one had touched it. The stem is an Acme screw that had been comfortably self-locking since installation, needing \( \mu>0.064 \) against a design value of 0.15. What changed was maintenance practice: a switch to a high-performance low-friction stem lubricant, applied generously at every service, had brought the running coefficient down over about two years. Once \( \mu \) approached 0.064 the line pressure acting on the disc could back-drive the stem, and vibration from a nearby pump did the rest. Nothing was worn, nothing was broken, and every dimension was in tolerance. The failure was in a parameter nobody owned — a friction coefficient that a lubricant decision had silently re-specified. Two lessons transfer. Self-locking is a property of the friction pair, not of the hardware, so a lubricant change on a self-locking mechanism is a design change and should be reviewed as one. And where the consequence of back-driving matters, the correct answer is not a friction margin at all but a positive mechanical lock — a handwheel lock, a gear operator, or a detent — that does not depend on a coefficient.
