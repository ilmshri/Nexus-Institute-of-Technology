Gear trains

Simple, compound, and planetary trains; ratio arithmetic including the tabular planetary method. Gearbox ratios decomposed. The gear-mesh frequencies that will reappear in vibration spectra.

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Compute a compound train's ratio: what multiplies?
  • Why do planetary trains need the tabular method?

§1 · Simple and compound trains

A gear pair changes speed by its tooth-count ratio; a train chains pairs together to reach ratios one pair cannot. The arithmetic is bookkeeping.

Key relation — train value

\[ e=\frac{\omega_{\text{out}}}{\omega_{\text{in}}} =\pm\frac{\text{product of driving tooth numbers}}{\text{product of driven tooth numbers}} \]

The sign is negative for each external mesh and positive for each internal (ring) mesh; count the external meshes and the sign follows.

In a simple train every gear sits on its own shaft, so intermediate gears cancel completely — an idler changes the direction of rotation and nothing else. In a compound train two gears share a shaft and turn together, so their ratios multiply. That is the only way to reach a large reduction in a sensible envelope.

Take a two-stage reducer: a 17-tooth pinion driving 51 teeth, then a 19-tooth pinion on the same intermediate shaft driving 76 teeth. Stage ratios are \( 51/17 = 3.00 \) and \( 76/19 = 4.00 \), so the train reduces by 12.00. An input of 1750 rev/min leaves at 145.83 rev/min, and the intermediate shaft runs at \( 1750/3 = 583.33 \) rev/min — a speed that belongs to no nameplate and is exactly the number needed to interpret a vibration spectrum.

Torque goes the other way. Ideally output torque is 12 times input torque; at a realistic 97% per mesh the train delivers \( 12 \times 0.97^2 = 11.29 \) times, and the missing 6% leaves as heat that the casing has to reject.

§2 · Epicyclic trains, where the ordinary rule fails

Let a gear's centre move and the tooth-count bookkeeping collapses, because the ratios were derived for fixed centres. In a planetary (epicyclic) set — sun, planets on a carrier, and a ring — the planets both spin and orbit. The fix is to change observer: ride the carrier, and from there every centre is fixed again.

Key relation — train equation for epicyclics

\[ e=\frac{\omega_L-\omega_A}{\omega_F-\omega_A} \]

\( \omega_A \) is the carrier (arm), \( F \) and \( L \) the first and last gears of the train, and \( e \) is the ordinary train value computed as if the carrier were fixed. Two of the three speeds must be known; the third follows.

Work the standard set: sun 20 teeth, planets 20, ring 60 — the geometric requirement being \( N_r = N_s + 2N_p \), which these satisfy. Taking the sun as first and the ring as last, \( e = -N_s/N_r = -1/3 \): one external mesh sun-to-planet, one internal mesh planet-to-ring, so one sign change, and the planet's own tooth count cancels — a planet in this arrangement is an idler.

Hold the ring, drive the sun at 1500 rev/min, take the output from the carrier:

\[ \frac{0-\omega_A}{1500-\omega_A}=-\frac{1}{3} \;\Longrightarrow\; -3\omega_A = -1500+\omega_A \;\Longrightarrow\; \omega_A = 375\ \text{rev/min} \]

The reduction is 4:1, not 3:1. In closed form, ring held gives \( 1+N_r/N_s \), and the extra 1 is the carrier's own rotation. Reading \( N_r/N_s = 3 \) off the tooth counts is the standard planetary mistake and lands 33% out.

The same set gives other ratios by changing what is held. Hold the carrier instead and it becomes an ordinary reversing train: \( \omega_{\text{ring}} = e\,\omega_{\text{sun}} = -500 \) rev/min. Lock any two members together and the whole set turns as one, at 1:1 — which is how an automatic transmission gets a direct drive out of the same hardware.

The planets themselves are worth a number. From \( (\omega_P-\omega_A)/(\omega_S-\omega_A) = -N_s/N_p = -1 \), the planets spin at \( \omega_P = 375 - (1500-375) = -750 \) rev/min: backwards, and at twice the carrier speed. Planet bearings run at that relative speed while orbiting, which is why they, not the teeth, are often what limits an epicyclic set's life.

§3 · What a train is worth: torque, size and load sharing

Three planets share the load between sun and ring, so each mesh carries roughly a third of the torque — the reason an epicyclic set of a given rating is markedly smaller and lighter than a parallel-shaft one, and the reason it is used where space or weight dominates. The word roughly is doing real work: equal sharing requires accurate spacing and some compliance, and a set with three rigidly located planets is statically indeterminate. Practice floats the sun, or the ring, to let the load equalise.

The input and output are coaxial, which suits an inline drive and removes the offset a parallel-shaft box forces on the layout. Against that, an epicyclic set has more parts, needs tighter geometry, and its ratio range per stage is narrower — roughly 3:1 to 10:1 with the ring held.

§4 · Gear-mesh frequency: the train in a spectrum

Every train writes its own signature into the machine's vibration. Teeth engage at a rate set by the shaft speed and the tooth count, and Lesson 5 supplied the mechanism: contact ratio has a fractional part, so mesh stiffness rises and falls once per tooth.

Key relation — gear-mesh frequency

\[ f_{\text{GMF}}=\frac{N\times \text{rev/min}}{60} \]

Computed on either gear of a mesh — the two give the same number, which is the check that the shaft speed used was the right one.

For the two-stage reducer: the first mesh runs at \( 17 \times 1750/60 = 495.8 \) Hz, and the second at \( 19 \times 583.33/60 = 184.7 \) Hz. Neither equals a shaft speed, and both are predictable before the machine is ever started. An analyst who has computed them can name a peak; one who has not sees an unexplained line at 496 Hz.

Epicyclic sets are computed the same way, in the carrier's frame: \( f = N_r|\omega_A-\omega_{\text{ring}}|/60 = 60(375)/60 = 375 \) Hz, and equally \( N_s|\omega_S-\omega_A|/60 = 20(1125)/60 = 375 \) Hz. Two routes, one answer — the same kind of independent check the instant centre gave in Lesson 2.

One design choice hides in the tooth counts. This train uses 17 and 51, and \( \gcd(17,51)=17 \), so each pinion tooth meets only \( 51/17 = 3 \) gear teeth, the same three for the life of the box. Any damage on that pinion tooth is hammered into those three and nowhere else. Choosing 52 instead of 51 makes the counts coprime — a hunting pair — so every pinion tooth eventually meets every gear tooth and wear distributes. The cost is only that the ratio becomes \( 52/17 = 3.0588 \) and the train 12.235, with the output at 143.03 rev/min instead of 145.83. Ratios that come out exact are a warning sign, not a convenience.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Gear tooth geometry (Lesson 5) — module, pressure angle and the contact-ratio fluctuation that becomes gear-mesh vibration here.
  • Relative motion (Lesson 2) — the train equation is an observer change onto the carrier, exactly the reasoning behind instant centres.
  • Power and torque (Physics I) — \( P=T\omega \), so a speed reduction is a torque multiplication less the losses.
  • Frequency and rotational speed (Electronics & Sensors L9) — converting rev/min to Hz, and what a spectrum line means.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

A two-stage compound reducer

Problem. A 17-tooth pinion drives a 51-tooth gear; on the same intermediate shaft a 19-tooth pinion drives a 76-tooth gear. The input runs at 1750 rev/min and delivers 45 N·m. Find the overall ratio, the output speed, the intermediate shaft speed, and the output torque at 97% efficiency per mesh.

Solution. (1) Stage ratios: \( 51/17 = 3.000 \) and \( 76/19 = 4.000 \); overall \( 3.000 \times 4.000 = 12.000 \).

(2) Output speed \( = 1750/12 = 145.83\ \mathrm{rev/min} \).

(3) Intermediate shaft \( = 1750/3 = 583.33\ \mathrm{rev/min} \).

(4) Efficiency \( = 0.97^2 = 0.9409 \), so the torque multiplication is \( 12 \times 0.9409 = 11.29 \) and \( T_{\text{out}} = 45 \times 11.29 = 508\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \).

Answer: 12:1, 145.83 rev/min out, 583.33 rev/min on the intermediate shaft, and 508 N·m. Sanity check: the ideal output torque would be \( 45\times12 = 540 \) N·m, so 32 N·m — about 6% — is lost. Check it as power: input \( P = T\omega = 45(1750)(2\pi/60) = 8.25 \) kW, output \( 508(145.83)(2\pi/60) = 7.76 \) kW, a difference of 0.49 kW that leaves as heat through the casing. That number is the one the thermal design needs, and it is invisible if only speeds are computed.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

A planetary set, three ways

Problem. A planetary set has a 20-tooth sun, 20-tooth planets and a 60-tooth ring. (a) Confirm the set is geometrically possible. (b) With the ring held and the sun driven at 1500 rev/min, find the carrier speed and the ratio. (c) Find the planet speed. (d) With the carrier held instead and the sun still at 1500, find the ring speed.

Solution. (a) \( N_r = N_s + 2N_p \): \( 60 = 20 + 2(20) \) ✓.

(b) With the carrier notionally fixed, sun to ring is one external and one internal mesh, so \( e = -N_s/N_r = -20/60 = -1/3 \). Then \( \frac{\omega_r-\omega_A}{\omega_s-\omega_A} = \frac{0-\omega_A}{1500-\omega_A} = -\frac{1}{3} \), giving \( -3\omega_A = -1500 + \omega_A \), so \( \omega_A = 375\ \mathrm{rev/min} \) and the ratio is \( 1500/375 = 4.00 \).

(c) For sun to planet, \( e = -N_s/N_p = -1 \), so \( \frac{\omega_P-375}{1500-375} = -1 \) and \( \omega_P = 375 - 1125 = -750\ \mathrm{rev/min} \).

(d) With the carrier held, the set becomes an ordinary train: \( \omega_r = e\,\omega_s = -\tfrac{1}{3}(1500) = -500\ \mathrm{rev/min} \).

Answer: (a) valid; (b) 375 rev/min, 4.00:1; (c) −750 rev/min; (d) −500 rev/min. Sanity check: the closed form \( 1+N_r/N_s = 1+3 = 4 \) confirms (b), and the difference from the tooth-count ratio of 3 is the carrier's own rotation — a 33% error if it is forgotten. The planets turning at −750 rev/min, twice the carrier speed and backwards, is the number the planet bearings actually see; it is easy to miss because no shaft outside the gearbox turns at it.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Naming the lines in a gearbox spectrum

Problem. For the compound reducer above (17/51 then 19/76, input 1750 rev/min), find the gear-mesh frequency of each stage. For the planetary set above, find its mesh frequency by two independent routes. Then say what \( \gcd(17,51) \) implies, and what changing 51 to 52 would cost.

Solution. (1) First stage: \( f = 17(1750)/60 = 495.8\ \mathrm{Hz} \). Checked on the other gear: \( 51(583.33)/60 = 495.8\ \mathrm{Hz} \) ✓.

(2) Second stage: \( f = 19(583.33)/60 = 184.7\ \mathrm{Hz} \); on the 76-tooth gear, \( 76(145.83)/60 = 184.7\ \mathrm{Hz} \) ✓.

(3) Planetary, from the ring: \( f = N_r|\omega_A-\omega_r|/60 = 60(375-0)/60 = 375\ \mathrm{Hz} \). From the sun: \( N_s|\omega_s-\omega_A|/60 = 20(1500-375)/60 = 20(18.75) = 375\ \mathrm{Hz} \) ✓.

(4) \( \gcd(17,51) = 17 \), so each pinion tooth meets only \( 51/17 = 3 \) gear teeth, the same three always. With 52 teeth, \( \gcd(17,52)=1 \) and every pinion tooth eventually meets every gear tooth. The cost is the ratio: \( 52/17 = 3.0588 \), overall 12.235, output 143.03 rev/min instead of 145.83, and the first-stage mesh unchanged at 495.8 Hz while the second becomes 181.2 Hz.

Answer: 495.8 Hz and 184.7 Hz; the planetary at 375 Hz confirmed twice; 17/51 is not a hunting pair, and 17/52 costs a 1.9% ratio change to become one. Sanity check: computing each mesh frequency on both of its gears is a genuine check, because the two calculations use different tooth counts and different shaft speeds and can only agree if the speeds were right. Note that no mesh frequency coincides with any shaft speed here (29.2, 9.7 and 2.4 Hz), so a spectrum from this box has three well-separated families of lines.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

In a simple gear train, an idler gear

The idler is driven by the gear before it and drives the gear after it, so its tooth count appears once in the numerator and once in the denominator and cancels exactly. What it does change is the sign: each external mesh reverses direction, so an idler flips the output's sense and lets a designer bridge a centre distance without altering the ratio.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

A planetary set has a 20-tooth sun and a 60-tooth ring. The ring is held and the sun is driven. The reduction to the carrier is

\( 1 + N_r/N_s = 1 + 3 = 4 \). The extra 1 is the carrier's own rotation: the output member is itself moving while the sun turns, which is exactly what the fixed-centre tooth-count rule cannot represent. Reading 3:1 straight off the tooth counts is the standard planetary error and puts every downstream speed and torque 33% out.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

In the train equation \( e=(\omega_L-\omega_A)/(\omega_F-\omega_A) \), \( e \) is

\( e \) is the fixed-centre ratio from first to last gear, sign included, worked out as though an observer were riding the carrier. Subtracting \( \omega_A \) from both speeds is what puts the equation into that observer's frame. The actual input-to-output ratio is what the equation is then solved for, and it generally differs from \( e \).

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

The geometric condition for a standard planetary set to assemble at all is

The planet has to span the gap between the sun and the ring, and it sits on both sides of its own centre, so the ring's radius exceeds the sun's by two planet diameters — in tooth counts, \( N_r = N_s + 2N_p \). The worked set, 20 + 2(20) = 60, satisfies it. A second condition, that the planets be equally spaced, requires \( (N_s+N_r) \) to divide by the number of planets.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A two-stage reducer has stage ratios 3.00 and 4.00 and 97% efficiency per mesh. The output torque is

Speed reduction multiplies torque by the ratio and losses take some back: \( 12 \times 0.97^2 = 12 \times 0.9409 = 11.29 \). Efficiencies multiply through a train exactly as ratios do, one factor per mesh. The 6% that does not arrive as torque leaves as heat, and on an 8.25 kW input that is about 0.49 kW the casing has to reject.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A gearbox with a 17-tooth input pinion runs at 1750 rev/min. A vibration spectrum shows a strong line at 496 Hz. This is

\( f = N\,n/60 = 17(1750)/60 = 495.8 \) Hz. The shaft itself turns at \( 1750/60 = 29.2 \) Hz, nowhere near it. Mesh frequencies are computable before the machine is started, which is what makes them the first thing to calculate when a spectrum is taken — an unexplained line becomes a named one, and only the genuinely unexplained lines then need chasing.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A gear pair with 17 and 51 teeth is described as "not a hunting pair". The consequence is

\( \gcd(17,51)=17 \), so a given pinion tooth returns to the same \( 51/17 = 3 \) gear teeth for the life of the box. Any damage on it is transferred into those three and nowhere else, and wear never distributes. Coprime counts — 17 and 52 — make every tooth eventually meet every other tooth. The ratio is exact either way; that is the point, since an exactly integer ratio is usually a sign the counts share a factor.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Compared with a parallel-shaft reducer of the same rating, an epicyclic set is smaller mainly because

",

With three planets each mesh carries roughly a third of the torque, so the same rating fits into much less face width and diameter; the coaxial input and output save further space. The word "roughly" matters — equal sharing needs accurate spacing and some deliberate compliance, which is why the sun or ring is usually left to float. Tooth strength and lubrication requirements are the same as for any other gear.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the planetary that was specified 33% wrong. A pump skid was re-rated and the drive reducer replaced with a compact planetary unit: 20-tooth sun, 20-tooth planets, 60-tooth ring, ring held, motor on the sun at 1500 rev/min, output from the carrier. The specification sheet recorded the reduction as 3:1 from the tooth counts \( N_r/N_s = 60/20 \), and the pump was selected for 500 rev/min. Commissioned, the pump ran at 375 and never reached its duty point.

The train equation is not optional for a moving-centre train. With the carrier notionally fixed, \( e = -N_s/N_r = -1/3 \); with the ring actually held, \( (0-\omega_A)/(1500-\omega_A) = -1/3 \) gives \( \omega_A = 375 \) rev/min, a reduction of \( 1+N_r/N_s = 4 \), not 3. The extra unit is the carrier's own rotation, and no amount of tooth-count arithmetic will produce it. Every downstream figure inherited the error: flow, head and absorbed power were all quoted against a shaft speed 33% too high.

Two practices follow. An epicyclic ratio is never read off tooth counts — it is solved from \( e=(\omega_L-\omega_A)/(\omega_F-\omega_A) \), and the answer states which member is held, because the same hardware gives 4:1, 3:1 reversing or 1:1 depending on that alone. And a commissioning check should include a measured output shaft speed against the calculated one: it takes a tachometer and one minute, and it is the only test that catches a ratio error before the process does.
