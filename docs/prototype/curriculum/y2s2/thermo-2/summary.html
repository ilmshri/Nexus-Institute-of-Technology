THM 202 — Thermodynamics II — Cycles & Utilities

THM 202 · Lesson 01 · Rankine cycle

Lesson 01Rankine cycle

Key points

  • The Rankine cycle is four devices and four states: pump, boiler, turbine, condenser — every steam plant is this loop with detail added.
  • Each device is one application of the steady-flow energy equation, so the whole cycle is one bookkeeping exercise, not four separate problems.
  • Thermal efficiency is net work over heat added: \( \eta=\frac{(h_3-h_4)-(h_2-h_1)}{h_3-h_2} \).
  • Pump work is small against turbine work — the reason a vapour cycle beats a gas cycle for stationary power.
  • Efficiency has three levers: raise boiler pressure, raise superheat temperature, lower condenser pressure.
  • Condenser pressure is the most powerful and the most constrained — it is set by the temperature of whatever cooling medium is available.

THM 202 · Lesson 02 · Real steam cycles: reheat, regeneration, losses

Lesson 02Real steam cycles: reheat, regeneration, losses

Key points

  • Reheat takes partly expanded steam back to the boiler and expands it again: it raises mean heat-addition temperature and keeps turbine exit quality acceptable.
  • Wet steam erodes last-stage blades, so exit quality is a hardware limit, not a nicety.
  • Regeneration bleeds steam to preheat feedwater, removing the coldest part of heat addition — the single largest practical efficiency gain.
  • Real machines are rated by isentropic efficiency, \( \eta_t=(h_3-h_4)/(h_3-h_{4s}) \); irreversibility shows on the h–s diagram as expansion drifting to the right.
  • Operators speak heat rate, not efficiency: \( \text{heat rate}=3600/\eta \) kJ/kWh — a smaller number is better.
  • Reheat and regeneration both buy efficiency by changing where heat enters the cycle, not by making any component better.

THM 202 · Lesson 03 · Industrial steam systems

Lesson 03Industrial steam systems

Key points

  • A plant's steam system is one connected network — boiler, headers at several pressures, pressure-reducing valves, and the condensate return.
  • Dropping pressure through a PRV or trap flashes some liquid to vapour; the quality is \( x=(h-h_f)/h_{fg} \) at the downstream pressure.
  • Flash steam is recoverable energy, not a nuisance — capturing it is one of the cheapest utility savings available.
  • Steam traps must pass condensate and block steam; a failed-open trap vents live steam continuously and is costed directly with the SFEE.
  • Trap failures are invisible without a survey, which is why trap population surveys are routine plant practice.
  • This is the backbone lesson: almost every later utility topic connects back to the steam header.

THM 202 · Lesson 04 · Gas turbines and Brayton cycle

Lesson 04Gas turbines and Brayton cycle

Key points

  • The Brayton cycle is the gas-turbine loop: compress, burn, expand, reject — open to atmosphere rather than condensing.
  • Ideal efficiency depends only on pressure ratio: \( \eta=1-r_p^{-(\gamma-1)/\gamma} \).
  • Because compression consumes a large share of turbine output, the back-work ratio is far worse than a steam cycle's.
  • That makes component efficiencies decisive — a few points lost in the compressor costs far more here than in a Rankine plant.
  • Raising pressure ratio shows diminishing returns while hardware cost climbs faster, so the optimum is economic, not thermodynamic.
  • Intercooling cuts compression work and regeneration recovers exhaust heat; both raise efficiency by rearranging where heat and work cross the boundary.

THM 202 · Lesson 05 · Vapor-compression refrigeration

Lesson 05Vapor-compression refrigeration

Key points

  • Vapour compression is the Rankine cycle run backwards: evaporator, compressor, condenser, expansion device.
  • Performance is a coefficient of performance, not an efficiency: \( COP=q_{evap}/w_{comp}=(h_1-h_4)/(h_2-h_1) \), and it is normally well above 1.
  • COP is not a violation of anything — the machine moves heat, it does not create it.
  • Lifting between closer temperatures raises COP, so condensing temperature and evaporating temperature are the two numbers that matter.
  • Superheat at the compressor suction protects it from liquid; subcooling at the condenser outlet guarantees liquid at the expansion device.
  • Both are read as health indicators in the field — they diagnose charge, airflow and fouling long before a failure.

THM 202 · Lesson 06 · Psychrometrics and HVAC loads

Lesson 06Psychrometrics and HVAC loads

Key points

  • Moist air is tracked by dry-bulb temperature and humidity ratio; enthalpy is \( h=1.006T+W(2501+1.86T) \) kJ per kg of dry air.
  • Working per kilogram of dry air is what makes the bookkeeping consistent, because dry air is what is conserved through a coil.
  • The psychrometric chart is a working tool: every HVAC process is a path drawn on it.
  • Cooling and dehumidification is a two-part process — sensible cooling along constant \( W \), then latent removal once the surface is below dew point.
  • In a Gulf-summer design condition the latent share is large; the worked case lands near 48% latent, which reshapes coil selection entirely.
  • Equipment rated at temperate conditions is not rated for that split — design conditions must be stated, not assumed.

THM 202 · Lesson 07 · Cooling towers and cooling water systems

Lesson 07Cooling towers and cooling water systems

Key points

  • A cooling tower rejects heat by evaporating a little of the water, so its floor is the ambient wet-bulb, not the dry-bulb.
  • Range is the temperature drop across the tower; approach is how close the cold water gets to the wet bulb.
  • Range is set by the load and the flow; approach is set by the tower's size — a smaller approach needs disproportionately more tower.
  • Evaporation leaves dissolved solids behind, so the circulating water concentrates.
  • Cycles of concentration link makeup and blowdown by a mass balance: more cycles saves water but risks scaling.
  • The condenser cooling loop is a managed system — thermal duty, water chemistry and blowdown are one problem, not three.

THM 202 · Lesson 08 · Compressed air systems

Lesson 08Compressed air systems

Key points

  • Compression work depends on the path: isothermal \( w=RT\ln(P_2/P_1) \) is the least, adiabatic the most, and real machines sit between.
  • Multistaging with intercooling moves a real compressor toward the isothermal ideal — that is the whole reason intercoolers exist.
  • Compressed air is the most expensive utility per joule delivered in most plants, because so little input energy survives to useful work.
  • Receivers buffer demand; dryers set pressure dew point, which must be below the coldest pipe the air will see or water drops out in the line.
  • Leaks run continuously, are invisible, and are costed straight from the compression-work equation — the arithmetic is what makes leak surveys pay.
  • Reducing system pressure by a small amount saves energy across every hour the plant runs.

THM 202 · Lesson 09 · Combustion fundamentals

Lesson 09Combustion fundamentals

Key points

  • Stoichiometric air comes from the balanced combustion equation and air being 21% oxygen — it is derived, never recalled.
  • Real burners run excess air because mixing is imperfect and unburnt fuel is both waste and a hazard.
  • Too much excess air is its own loss: every extra kilogram is heated and sent up the stack.
  • Higher and lower heating values differ by the latent heat of the water formed — quote which one an efficiency uses or the number is ambiguous.
  • Stack-loss efficiency is the practical field measurement: flue temperature and oxygen give combustion efficiency directly.
  • Boiler tuning is applied chemistry — trim excess air to the point where CO just begins to appear, and no further.

THM 202 · Lesson 10 · Exergy analysis of utilities

Lesson 10Exergy analysis of utilities

Key points

  • Energy is conserved, so a first-law balance cannot tell you where a plant is wasting opportunity. Exergy can.
  • Exergy is the maximum useful work extractable relative to the dead state; it is destroyed by irreversibility, never conserved.
  • Gouy–Stodola quantifies it: \( \dot X_{destroyed}=T_0\dot S_{gen} \).
  • Throttling destroys exergy while conserving enthalpy — the clearest case of a first-law-invisible loss.
  • Large temperature differences across a heat exchanger are comfortable thermally and expensive exergetically.
  • Ranking components by exergy destroyed gives an audit its priority list, and it rarely matches the first-law loss ranking.

THM 202 · Lesson 11 · Utility system audit project

Lesson 11Utility system audit project

Key points

  • A utility audit is one connected balance across steam, condensate, compressed air, cooling water and fuel — not a set of separate surveys.
  • Close the balances first: unclosed mass or energy means a stream is unmeasured or a meter is wrong, and every conclusion downstream inherits that error.
  • Rank losses by exergy destroyed, not by energy quantity, so effort goes where recoverable work actually is.
  • Every proposal carries a payback: the engineering is not finished until the cost and the saving are both stated.
  • The cheapest measures are usually operational — trap surveys, leak repair, excess-air trim, pressure reduction — before any capital project.
  • This capstone practises the whole course end to end: SFEE, cycles, psychrometrics, compression, combustion and exergy applied to one plant.
