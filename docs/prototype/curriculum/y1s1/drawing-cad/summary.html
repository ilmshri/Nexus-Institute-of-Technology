DRW 102 — Engineering Drawing & CAD

DRW 102 · Lesson 01 · Why drawings rule the plant

Lesson 01Why drawings rule the plant

Key points

  • A drawing is not a picture of a part; it is a contract about one — binding the designer's intent, the fabricator's obligation, the inspector's acceptance criteria, and later the maintenance engineer's only honest witness to what was built.
  • The revision letter is the most important character on the sheet. Torque a coupling to Rev B when the shop built Rev D and the bolts may be the wrong length.
  • Work only from the revision the document register calls current, and treat uncontrolled prints — the ones taped inside panel doors — as rumours.
  • As-built claims decay from the day they are stamped. Verify the two or three dimensions that matter against the metal before cutting steel, and red-line the drawing back to document control when reality wins.

DRW 102 · Lesson 01 · Why drawings rule the plant · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • None yet — this is the course's gate. It defines the vocabulary the next ten lessons use.

Glossary

  • Title block — the sheet's identity card: number, title, scale, projection, approvals.
  • Revision — a controlled change to a released drawing, lettered and logged.
  • Document register — the authoritative list of current revisions.
  • As-built — drawing updated to reflect what was actually constructed.
  • Red-line — field markup recording reality for document control to absorb.
  • Uncontrolled copy — any print outside the register's custody; convenient and dangerous.

DRW 102 · Lesson 02 · Projection theory and orthographic views

Lesson 02Projection theory and orthographic views

Key points

  • Orthographic projection flattens three dimensions honestly: each view looks along one axis, so every view measures truly in its own plane.
  • First angle versus third angle changes where the views are placed, and the title-block symbol tells you which convention is in force. Reading a first-angle drawing as third-angle mirrors the part.
  • Choose the minimum set of views that shows the function, and put the most descriptive view as the front.

DRW 102 · Lesson 02 · Projection theory and orthographic views · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Drawing custody (Lesson 1) — you are reading the current revision.
  • Basic solid visualization — rotating simple prisms and cylinders mentally.

Glossary

  • Orthographic projection — parallel-ray mapping preserving true size per view.
  • First/third angle — the two view-arrangement conventions; declared by symbol.
  • Hidden line — dashed; an edge behind material.
  • Center line — chain line marking axes and symmetry.
  • Auxiliary view — projection onto an inclined plane to show a slanted face true-size.

DRW 102 · Lesson 03 · Isometric and pictorial views

Lesson 03Isometric and pictorial views

Key points

  • Pictorials are for communication, not measurement — they show a reader what the part looks like, and are not the document a machinist works from.
  • They mislead predictably: in isometric, angles are not true angles and only edges along the three principal axes are to scale.
  • Freehand sketching is the engineer's fastest tool. A clear sketch on a scrap of paper settles more site arguments than any model.

DRW 102 · Lesson 03 · Isometric and pictorial views · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Orthographic fluency (Lesson 2) — pictorials complement, never replace, the views.
  • Axis discipline — holding 120° axes steady on paper.

Glossary

  • Isometric — pictorial with equal-scaled axes at 120°.
  • Exploded view — assembly drawn apart along its assembly axes.
  • Foreshortening — apparent shrinkage of lines not parallel to a projection plane.
  • Boxing-in — sketch technique: overall proportions first, features second.

DRW 102 · Lesson 04 · Sections and conventions

Lesson 04Sections and conventions

Key points

  • A section cuts the part open on paper so interior features can be dimensioned properly instead of guessed at through hidden lines.
  • The section family: full, half, offset, revolved, removed and broken-out — each answers a different question about what needs exposing.
  • Conventions that lie honestly: ribs and webs are not hatched when cut along their length, and fasteners are shown uncut. The drawing departs from strict geometry deliberately, because the strictly correct view would read as a solid block and mislead.

DRW 102 · Lesson 04 · Sections and conventions · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Orthographic views (Lesson 2) — sections are views with a knife.
  • Hidden-line reading — to appreciate what sectioning removes.

Glossary

  • Cutting plane — the imaginary knife; its line, arrows, and letters index the section.
  • Hatching — thin parallel lines marking cut material; pattern hints at material family.
  • Full/half/offset section — the main slice strategies.
  • Broken-out section — a local peel, bounded by a freehand break line.
  • Convention — a standardized deliberate simplification (unhatched ribs, bolts, shafts).

DRW 102 · Lesson 05 · Dimensioning practice

Lesson 05Dimensioning practice

Key points

  • Dimensions carry function. Dimension what the part must achieve, not merely what is convenient to measure on the model.
  • Chains accumulate tolerance and baselines do not: a chain of five dimensions stacks five tolerances into the last gap, which is how parts pass every individual check and still fail to assemble.
  • Callouts have a grammar — diameters, radii, counterbores, depths and threads each have a defined form, and using it removes the need for a note explaining what you meant.

DRW 102 · Lesson 05 · Dimensioning practice · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Views and sections (Lessons 2, 4) — dimensions live where shape shows true.
  • Tolerance intuition — every dimension implies a permitted variation (Lesson 6 formalizes).

Glossary

  • Datum — the reference a dimension originates from.
  • Chain dimensioning — feature-to-feature; accumulates tolerance.
  • Baseline dimensioning — all from one datum; kills accumulation.
  • Reference dimension — parenthesized; informative, not binding.
  • Basic dimension — boxed; exact by definition, toleranced through GD&T.

DRW 102 · Lesson 06 · Tolerances, fits, and limits

Lesson 06Tolerances, fits, and limits

Key points

  • Tolerance is a purchase. Every tightened band costs machining time, tooling and scrap, so specify the loosest tolerance that still works.
  • The ISO fit system names the intent: clearance fits always have a gap, interference fits never do, and transition fits may go either way within their limits.
  • Hole-basis is the usual default, because it is cheaper to vary a shaft than to vary a reamed hole.
  • Read limits like a fitter: convert the fit code to actual maximum and minimum sizes before deciding whether a shaft will enter a bore.

DRW 102 · Lesson 06 · Tolerances, fits, and limits · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Dimensioning (Lesson 5) — limits attach to dimensions.
  • Decimal discipline — thousandths of a millimetre matter here.

Glossary

  • IT grade — tolerance-zone width class (IT6 tighter than IT8).
  • Hole basis — hole held at H; shaft letter sets the fit.
  • Clearance/transition/interference — always loose / could be either / always tight.
  • Allowance — the tightest intended condition of a fit.
  • Limits — the max and min values a dimension may take.

DRW 102 · Lesson 07 · GD&T essentials

Lesson 07GD&T essentials

Key points

  • Coordinate dimensioning alone cannot express what a functional part actually requires — it constrains points, not relationships, and it makes the tolerance zone a square when the function wants a circle.
  • Datums are the measurement contract: they state where the part is held and in what order, so that the designer, machinist and inspector all measure from the same place.
  • Position, form and orientation controls say what the feature must be, independently of how it happens to be measured.
  • Runout is the rotating machine's own control — it asks the question a spinning shaft actually poses, which is how far the surface moves as it turns.

DRW 102 · Lesson 07 · GD&T essentials · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Limits and fits (Lesson 6) — GD&T rides on toleranced features.
  • Datum thinking (Lesson 5) — formalized here into A|B|C frames.

Glossary

  • Feature-control frame — the boxed callout: symbol | zone | datums.
  • Datum reference frame — the seating sequence that arrests the part for measurement.
  • Position tolerance — a zone (usually ⌀) the feature's axis must live in.
  • MMC — maximum material condition; enables bonus tolerance and functional gauging.
  • Circular vs total runout — per-section wobble vs whole-surface sweep.

DRW 102 · Lesson 08 · Fasteners, welds, and surface symbols

Lesson 08Fasteners, welds, and surface symbols

Key points

  • Symbols are high-density engineering language: one weld symbol replaces a paragraph, and reading it wrongly builds the wrong joint.
  • Thread callouts state form, diameter, pitch and class of fit; weld symbols state process, size, length, spacing and which side of the joint.
  • Surface finish is a functional requirement, not a cosmetic one — it governs sealing, fatigue life and friction, and it is specified where it matters rather than everywhere.

DRW 102 · Lesson 08 · Fasteners, welds, and surface symbols · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Sections and dimensions (Lessons 4–5) — symbols attach to defined geometry.
  • Fits (Lesson 6) — thread classes are the fit system's cousins.

Glossary

  • Pitch — axial advance per thread turn (mm).
  • Arrow side / other side — the weld symbol's two shores of the reference line.
  • Throat / leg — a fillet weld's two size languages (a ≈ 0.7z).
  • Field weld flag — welded at site, not in the shop.
  • Ra — arithmetic average roughness, µm.

DRW 102 · Lesson 09 · Parametric CAD: parts

Lesson 09Parametric CAD: parts

Key points

  • Parametric means rebuildable. A model is a recipe of features and constraints, not a frozen shape, so changing a driving dimension rebuilds everything downstream.
  • Design intent is the recipe's grammar: constrain the model so that the relationships you actually care about survive a change. Poor intent produces a model that breaks the moment a real dimension moves.
  • From model to manufacture, the geometry must carry the information the process needs — draft, radii and access included.

DRW 102 · Lesson 09 · Parametric CAD: parts · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Views and dimensioning (Lessons 2, 5) — sketch constraints are dimensions with intent.
  • Datum thinking (Lesson 7) — model references mirror measurement datums.

Glossary

  • Feature tree — the ordered recipe of operations.
  • Fully defined sketch — zero degrees of freedom; nothing drifts.
  • Design intent — the declared invariants under change.
  • Reference geometry — planes/axes/points created to anchor features stably.
  • Rebuild error — the tree failing after an upstream change; the tax on lazy references.

DRW 102 · Lesson 10 · Parametric CAD: assemblies and drawings

Lesson 10Parametric CAD: assemblies and drawings

Key points

  • Assembly constraints are kinematics in disguise: mates that remove degrees of freedom describe how the mechanism is allowed to move, and over-constraining hides real interference.
  • The bill of materials is the assembly's ledger. If it is wrong, the right drawing still procures the wrong parts.
  • Drawings generated from models are still a craft. The software places views; the engineer decides which views, which sections, and which dimensions carry the function.

DRW 102 · Lesson 10 · Parametric CAD: assemblies and drawings · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Part modeling (Lesson 9) — assemblies consume disciplined parts.
  • Exploded views (Lesson 3) — the assembly drawing's teaching view.

Glossary

  • Mate/constraint — a positional relation removing degrees of freedom.
  • Degrees of freedom — the six motions a free rigid body owns.
  • BOM — the ballooned parts ledger of an assembly.
  • Sub-assembly — a rolled-up unit with its own BOM.
  • Interference check — the assembly's clash audit before metal exists.

DRW 102 · Lesson 11 · Reading plant drawings

Lesson 11Reading plant drawings

Key points

  • The plant has an ecosystem of drawings, not one drawing: general arrangements, detail drawings, P&IDs, isometrics and loop diagrams, each answering a different question.
  • Navigating a general arrangement: find the grid and the elevations first, then the section marks that lead to the details.
  • Cross-references are the point — a GA tells you where, a detail tells you what, and a P&ID tells you why it is connected that way.
  • The drawing package is a system, and reading it competently means moving between sheets deliberately rather than studying one in isolation.

DRW 102 · Lesson 11 · Reading plant drawings · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • The whole course — Lessons 1–10 supply custody, views, sections, dimensions, fits, GD&T, symbols, and CAD; this lesson deploys them at plant scale.

Glossary

  • GA drawing — general arrangement: equipment in plant space.
  • P&ID — piping & instrumentation diagram; process logic, not geometry.
  • Isometric (piping) — fabrication drawing of a pipe run.
  • Column grid — the lettered/numbered address system of a plant.
  • Withdrawal clearance — reserved space to pull bundles, rotors, filters.
  • Drawing package — the cross-checked set of current sheets for one job.
