STS 206 — Engineering Statistics

STS 206 · Lesson 01 · Describing data

Lesson 01Describing data

Key points

  • Engineering measurements always scatter, so a single reading is a sample, never the truth.
  • Statistics is the discipline for saying what a scattered set of numbers does and does not support.
  • Centre is described by mean, median or mode; the median resists outliers, the mean does not.
  • Spread matters as much as centre — two processes with identical means can behave completely differently.
  • Variance is the mean squared deviation; standard deviation returns it to the original units, which is why it is the one quoted.
  • Always plot the data before computing anything: a histogram exposes skew, bimodality and outliers that summary statistics hide.

STS 206 · Lesson 02 · Probability rules

Lesson 02Probability rules

Key points

  • Probability is the language for chance, defined on a sample space of outcomes.
  • The addition rule handles "or" and must subtract the overlap unless the events are mutually exclusive.
  • The multiplication rule handles "and" and only becomes a simple product when the events are independent.
  • Independence is an assumption to be justified, not assumed — common-cause failures are exactly where it breaks.
  • Conditional probability \( P(A|B) \) updates a belief given evidence, and Bayes' theorem inverts the conditioning.
  • That inversion is the basis of diagnostics: the probability of a fault given an alarm is not the probability of the alarm given the fault.

STS 206 · Lesson 03 · Discrete distributions

Lesson 03Discrete distributions

Key points

  • A random variable assigns a number to each outcome; its distribution says how likely each value is.
  • The binomial applies to a fixed number of independent trials with constant success probability — pass/fail inspection is the standard case.
  • Its mean is \( np \) and its variance \( np(1-p) \).
  • The Poisson applies to counts of events in a fixed interval when the rate is constant: defects per metre, breakdowns per month.
  • Poisson has the striking property that its mean and variance are equal, which is itself a quick test of whether it fits.
  • Choosing the distribution is the modelling decision — the arithmetic after it is routine.

STS 206 · Lesson 04 · Continuous distributions

Lesson 04Continuous distributions

Key points

  • Continuous variables need a density function, where probability is the area under the curve and any exact value has probability zero.
  • The normal distribution dominates because sums of many small effects tend toward it.
  • The z-score \( z=(x-\mu)/\sigma \) converts any normal problem to the standard normal, making one table serve all cases.
  • The empirical rule gives the working feel: roughly 68%, 95% and 99.7% within one, two and three standard deviations.
  • Not everything is normal — lifetimes are skewed and better served by exponential, lognormal or Weibull models.
  • Assuming normality for a skewed quantity is one of the most common and most consequential errors in engineering data.

STS 206 · Lesson 05 · Sampling distributions and the CLT

Lesson 05Sampling distributions and the CLT

Key points

  • Inference is the leap from a sample to a statement about the population it came from.
  • A sample statistic is itself a random variable with its own distribution — the sampling distribution.
  • The standard error \( \sigma/\sqrt n \) measures how much the sample mean itself scatters.
  • The square root is the practical sting: quartering the uncertainty needs sixteen times the data.
  • The central limit theorem says the sample mean tends to normal as \( n \) grows, whatever the parent distribution.
  • That is why normal-based methods work so widely, and it is the theoretical licence for everything in the next two lessons.

STS 206 · Lesson 06 · Estimation and confidence intervals

Lesson 06Estimation and confidence intervals

Key points

  • A point estimate alone is unfalsifiable; an interval states the uncertainty with it.
  • A confidence interval is estimate ± critical value × standard error.
  • The confidence level describes the procedure: 95% of intervals built this way contain the true value.
  • It is not a probability that the parameter lies in this particular interval — a distinction worth keeping straight in any report.
  • With small samples and unknown \( \sigma \), the t-distribution replaces the normal, with heavier tails and wider intervals.
  • The t-distribution approaches the normal as degrees of freedom grow, which is why the correction stops mattering above roughly 30 samples.

STS 206 · Lesson 07 · Hypothesis testing

Lesson 07Hypothesis testing

Key points

  • A hypothesis test asks whether data are consistent with a stated claim, the null hypothesis.
  • The test statistic measures how far the data sit from the null in standard-error units, and the p-value is the probability of a result at least that extreme if the null is true.
  • A p-value is not the probability the null is true, and "failing to reject" is not proof of it.
  • Type I error rejects a true null; Type II error fails to reject a false one, and reducing one raises the other at fixed sample size.
  • Only more data improves both at once — which is what power analysis quantifies before an experiment runs.
  • Statistical significance is not engineering significance: a tiny, useless difference becomes significant with a large enough sample.

STS 206 · Lesson 08 · Regression

Lesson 08Regression

Key points

  • Regression describes how one variable moves with another and lets it be predicted.
  • Least squares fits the line minimising the sum of squared vertical residuals.
  • \( R^2 \) reports the fraction of variance explained, but a high \( R^2 \) does not mean the model is right.
  • Always plot the residuals: structure in them means the model form is wrong, however good the fit statistic looks.
  • Extrapolating beyond the fitted range is unsupported by the data, no matter how strong the correlation inside it.
  • Correlation is not causation — a lurking variable can produce a strong relationship between two effects of a common cause.

STS 206 · Lesson 09 · Design of experiments first contact

Lesson 09Design of experiments first contact

Key points

  • Designed experiments change factors deliberately instead of watching whatever the process happens to do.
  • Changing one factor at a time is inefficient and, worse, cannot detect interactions at all.
  • A full factorial runs every combination, revealing main effects and interactions together.
  • An interaction means a factor's effect depends on another's level — common in real processes and invisible to one-at-a-time testing.
  • Fractional factorials cut the run count by deliberately confounding high-order effects that are rarely important.
  • Randomisation, replication and blocking protect against lurking variables and give a valid estimate of experimental error.

STS 206 · Lesson 10 · Reliability statistics preview

Lesson 10Reliability statistics preview

Key points

  • Reliability is the probability of surviving to a stated time under stated conditions, so the statement is meaningless without both.
  • The exponential model assumes a constant hazard rate and gives \( R(t)=e^{-\lambda t} \) with \( MTBF=1/\lambda \).
  • Constant hazard means memoryless — a used item is as good as new, which is a strong claim and often false.
  • MTBF is not a service life, and reading it as one is a routine and expensive mistake.
  • The bathtub curve has three regimes — infant mortality, useful life, wearout — and only the middle one is exponential; Weibull covers all three.
  • Components in series multiply reliabilities, so a chain of good parts can still be an unreliable system; redundancy is what counters it.

STS 206 · Lesson 11 · Statistical malpractice and honest reporting

Lesson 11Statistical malpractice and honest reporting

Key points

  • Statistics goes wrong in predictable ways, and knowing them is part of using it honestly.
  • P-hacking is testing until something is significant; with enough comparisons, significance appears by chance alone.
  • Twenty independent tests at the 5% level give roughly a two-in-three chance of at least one false positive.
  • State the hypothesis before seeing the data, and correct for multiple comparisons when several tests are run.
  • Ignoring base rates makes a good test look decisive on a rare fault, when most positives are actually false.
  • Simpson's paradox shows a trend can reverse when a confounder is accounted for — which is why the analysis must report what was controlled, what was assumed, and what remains unexplained.
