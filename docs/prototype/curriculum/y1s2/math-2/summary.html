MTH 151 · Lesson 01 · Vectors and vector geometry

Lesson 01Vectors and vector geometry

Key points

  • A vector carries magnitude and direction, and engineering quantities divide sharply into those that do and those that do not.
  • The dot product measures alignment and returns a scalar: \( \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=|a||b|\cos\theta \).
  • It gives work directly, and it is zero exactly when two vectors are perpendicular — the fastest orthogonality test available.
  • Projection follows from the same product, which is how a force is resolved along any chosen direction.
  • The cross product measures area and returns a vector perpendicular to both, with magnitude \( |a||b|\sin\theta \).
  • It gives moment \( \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F} \), and its direction encodes the axis of rotation — which is why moments are vectors at all.

MTH 151 · Lesson 02 · Partial derivatives

Lesson 02Partial derivatives

Key points

  • A partial derivative is the rate of change with respect to one variable while the others are held fixed.
  • Computing one is ordinary one-variable calculus with the other symbols treated as constants.
  • The gradient assembles the partials into a vector pointing in the direction of steepest increase.
  • Its magnitude is that steepest rate, and it is always perpendicular to the level curves — the reason heat flows down a gradient.
  • Mixed second partials are equal for well-behaved functions, which is a genuine check on algebra.
  • Linear approximation via the total differential is what makes error propagation possible, and it is the everyday engineering use.

MTH 151 · Lesson 03 · Chain rule and total differentials

Lesson 03Chain rule and total differentials

Key points

  • The total differential sums each variable's contribution: \( df=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}dx+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}dy \).
  • The chain rule extends it when the variables themselves depend on something else — one term per path through the dependency.
  • Drawing the dependency tree first makes the number of terms obvious and prevents the usual omissions.
  • Error propagation is the everyday payoff: uncertainties combine through the partial derivatives, weighted by sensitivity.
  • Independent random errors add in quadrature, so the largest single contributor dominates the result.
  • That identifies which measurement to improve — refining anything else is wasted effort.

MTH 151 · Lesson 04 · Multiple integrals

Lesson 04Multiple integrals

Key points

  • A double integral sums a quantity over a two-dimensional region; the hard part is the limits, not the integration.
  • Sketch the region first and choose the integration order that keeps the limits simple.
  • Mass is the integral of density over the region, and dividing the first moment by mass gives the centroid.
  • Moment of inertia is the integral of \( r^2 \) over the mass — the \( r^2 \) is why distribution matters so much more than amount.
  • The parallel-axis theorem shifts a computed inertia to any parallel axis without redoing the integral.
  • Triple integrals extend all of this to volumes, and choosing cylindrical or spherical coordinates to match the geometry is what makes them tractable.

MTH 151 · Lesson 05 · Vector fields, flux, and circulation

Lesson 05Vector fields, flux, and circulation

Key points

  • A vector field assigns a vector to every point — velocity, force and heat flux are all fields.
  • A line integral accumulates a field along a path, giving work along it or circulation around a loop.
  • Flux measures how much of the field crosses a boundary, which is the mathematical form of every conservation statement.
  • Divergence measures net outflow at a point: positive means a source, negative a sink, zero means incompressible.
  • Curl measures local rotation — the swirl a paddle wheel placed in the flow would feel.
  • A field can circulate without diverging and diverge without circulating; Green's theorem links the boundary integral to the region integral, the two-dimensional ancestor of the divergence and Stokes theorems.

MTH 151 · Lesson 06 · Matrices and linear systems

Lesson 06Matrices and linear systems

Key points

  • A system of linear equations is one matrix equation \( A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} \), which is what makes it a computational object.
  • A non-zero determinant means the inverse exists and the solution is unique.
  • Gaussian elimination is the practical method; inverting the matrix is almost never the efficient route.
  • A zero determinant means singularity — either no solution, or infinitely many.
  • Singularity is a physical warning, not just an algebraic one: in a structure it means a mechanism, in a network an unconstrained loop.
  • Near-singular systems are ill-conditioned: small data errors produce large solution errors, which is why the condition number matters in numerical work.

MTH 151 · Lesson 07 · Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Lesson 07Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Key points

  • An eigenvector is a direction a matrix leaves unrotated: \( A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v} \), with the eigenvalue giving the scaling.
  • Eigenvalues come from the characteristic equation \( \det(A-\lambda I)=0 \).
  • Each eigenvalue then gives its eigenvector by solving the singular system that results.
  • Eigenvectors are directions, so any scalar multiple is the same eigenvector.
  • Engineers compute them because they are the natural modes of a system — mode shapes in vibration, principal axes in stress and inertia.
  • Diagonalising in the eigenvector basis decouples the equations, which is exactly how a multi-degree-of-freedom vibration problem becomes several independent one-degree problems.

MTH 151 · Lesson 08 · Second-order linear ODEs

Lesson 08Second-order linear ODEs

Key points

  • A second-order linear ODE with constant coefficients reduces to a quadratic characteristic equation — calculus becomes algebra.
  • The discriminant sorts the behaviour into three regimes: overdamped, critically damped and underdamped.
  • Overdamped returns slowly without oscillating; critically damped returns fastest without overshoot; underdamped oscillates with a decaying envelope.
  • Written in standard form, the parameters are the natural frequency \( \omega_n \) and damping ratio \( \zeta \).
  • \( \zeta<1 \), \( =1 \) and \( >1 \) name the three regimes, and \( \zeta \) alone sets the shape of the response.
  • This single equation models the mass-spring-damper, the RLC circuit and any first-cut control loop.

MTH 151 · Lesson 09 · Forced oscillations and resonance

Lesson 09Forced oscillations and resonance

Key points

  • Adding a forcing term gives a steady-state response at the forcing frequency, plus a transient that decays away.
  • The amplitude ratio plotted against frequency is the frequency response, and its peak is resonance.
  • At resonance the response is limited only by damping, so a lightly damped system can amplify enormously.
  • Phase lags behind the forcing and passes through 90° exactly at the natural frequency, whatever the damping.
  • Damping lowers the peak and shifts it slightly, but it cannot move resonance far — only mass or stiffness can.
  • Every vibration spectrum on a plant screen is this curve, which is why a measured peak identifies a natural frequency.

MTH 151 · Lesson 10 · Laplace transforms

Lesson 10Laplace transforms

Key points

  • The Laplace transform converts a differential equation in time into an algebraic equation in \( s \).
  • The derivative rule is what does the work, turning \( dy/dt \) into \( sY(s)-y(0) \).
  • Initial conditions therefore enter automatically instead of being fitted afterwards.
  • The method is: transform, solve algebraically, then invert with a table and partial fractions.
  • It handles step, impulse and discontinuous inputs cleanly, which classical methods struggle with.
  • The ratio of output to input transform is the transfer function — the foundation of the entire controls sequence.

MTH 151 · Lesson 11 · Fourier series first contact

Lesson 11Fourier series first contact

Key points

  • Any periodic signal, however jagged, is a sum of sinusoids at multiples of its fundamental frequency.
  • The Fourier coefficients are found by integrating the signal against each sinusoid over one period.
  • A square wave contains only odd harmonics, with amplitudes falling as \( 1/n \) — which is why it never quite becomes square with finite terms.
  • Sharp edges need high harmonics, so bandwidth and rise time are the same statement in two languages.
  • Time domain and frequency domain hold the same information presented differently.
  • An FFT screen shows lines because a periodic machine signal genuinely contains discrete frequencies — and identifying which line belongs to which fault is the whole of vibration diagnostics.
