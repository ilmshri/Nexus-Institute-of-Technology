DYN 152 — Engineering Dynamics

DYN 152 · Lesson 01 · Particle kinematics

Lesson 01Particle kinematics

Key points

  • Position, velocity and acceleration are linked by differentiation in time and integration back again — everything else in kinematics is bookkeeping on those three.
  • On a curved path, acceleration splits into two perpendicular parts with different jobs.
  • \( a_t=dv/dt \) changes speed; \( a_n=v^2/\rho \) changes direction and always points toward the centre of curvature.
  • A body moving at constant speed round a bend is still accelerating — the normal component never vanishes.
  • The \( v^2 \) in \( a_n \) is why cornering loads climb so steeply with speed.
  • Projectile motion is just the two axes solved separately: uniform horizontally, uniformly accelerated vertically.

DYN 152 · Lesson 02 · Newton's second law for particles

Lesson 02Newton's second law for particles

Key points

  • \( F=ma \) is applied through a free-body diagram, and the diagram is the analysis — not a preliminary to it.
  • Draw only forces acting on the body, and never include \( ma \) as one of them.
  • On an incline, resolving along and perpendicular to the surface reduces the problem to two scalar equations.
  • Friction opposes relative motion or its tendency, and the static limit \( \mu_sN \) is a maximum available force, not one that is always present.
  • Centripetal force is a requirement, not a new force: some real force — friction, tension, normal reaction — must supply \( mv^2/\rho \).
  • The method never changes: isolate the body, draw every force, choose axes, then write the equations.

DYN 152 · Lesson 03 · Work and energy

Lesson 03Work and energy

Key points

  • Work is force acting through distance, counting only the component along the displacement.
  • A force perpendicular to motion does no work — which is why the normal reaction and centripetal force never appear in an energy balance.
  • The work–energy theorem: \( W_{net}=\Delta KE=\tfrac12mv_2^2-\tfrac12mv_1^2 \).
  • It relates speeds to distances without ever solving for time, which is exactly when to reach for it instead of \( F=ma \).
  • Conservative forces such as gravity and springs store energy recoverably as potential energy; friction does not, and its work is path-dependent.
  • Power is the rate of doing work, \( P=Fv \) — the quantity that actually sizes a motor.

DYN 152 · Lesson 04 · Impulse, momentum, and impact

Lesson 04Impulse, momentum, and impact

Key points

  • Impulse is force integrated over time and equals the change in momentum: \( J=\int F\,dt=m\Delta v \).
  • Use it when the force is large, brief and poorly known — impacts, where \( F=ma \) is impractical.
  • With no external impulse, total momentum is conserved: \( \sum m_iv_i=\sum m_iv_i' \).
  • Momentum is conserved in an impact even when energy is not; only a perfectly elastic collision conserves both.
  • The coefficient of restitution measures that, from \( e=1 \) for perfectly elastic to \( e=0 \) for perfectly plastic.
  • Extending impact duration reduces peak force for the same momentum change — the principle behind every crumple zone and shock mount.

DYN 152 · Lesson 05 · Systems of particles

Lesson 05Systems of particles

Key points

  • A system of particles obeys \( \sum F_{ext}=Ma_{cm} \): the centre of mass moves exactly as a single particle carrying the total mass.
  • Internal forces cancel in pairs by Newton's third law and can never move the centre of mass.
  • With no external force, total momentum stays constant however violently the parts interact.
  • Steady mass flow turns momentum change into a sustained force: a jet striking a surface exerts \( \dot m\Delta v \).
  • That is the bridge into fluid mechanics — thrust, nozzle reaction and pipe-bend forces are all this one result.
  • Choosing the system boundary is again the decisive step: it fixes which forces count as external.

DYN 152 · Lesson 06 · Rigid-body kinematics

Lesson 06Rigid-body kinematics

Key points

  • Rigid-body motion comes in three kinds: translation, rotation about a fixed axis, and general plane motion combining both.
  • General plane motion is handled by relative velocity: \( v_B=v_A+\omega\times r_{B/A} \).
  • Every body in plane motion has an instantaneous centre of zero velocity, and about it the motion is pure rotation at that instant.
  • Locating that centre often turns a two-equation problem into one line.
  • Gear trains apply the same idea at the contact point: no slip means equal contact-point velocities, giving the ratio.
  • Linkages are solved the same way — the four-bar is the standard exercise, and its velocity analysis is pure relative-velocity work.

DYN 152 · Lesson 07 · Relative acceleration and Coriolis

Lesson 07Relative acceleration and Coriolis

Key points

  • Differentiating the relative-velocity equation gives the relative-acceleration equation, which adds a centripetal \( \omega^2r \) term alongside the tangential \( \alpha r \).
  • When the observer's frame itself rotates, an extra term appears: the Coriolis acceleration.
  • \( a_{Cor}=2\,\omega\times v_{rel} \), perpendicular to the sliding velocity and proportional to both the rotation rate and that velocity.
  • It exists only when something moves relative to a rotating frame — a body fixed in the frame never sees it.
  • The worked bead sliding along a rotating arm shows all the terms at once.
  • Slotted links, governors and any mechanism with sliding-in-rotating contact need this treatment or the answer is simply wrong.

DYN 152 · Lesson 08 · Rigid-body kinetics: Newton–Euler

Lesson 08Rigid-body kinetics: Newton–Euler

Key points

  • Rigid-body kinetics needs two equations: \( \sum F=ma_{cm} \) for translation and \( \sum M=I\alpha \) for rotation.
  • Moment of inertia measures how mass is distributed about the axis, not how much there is — geometry dominates.
  • The parallel-axis theorem \( I=I_{cm}+md^2 \) shifts it to any parallel axis, and the \( d^2 \) grows fast.
  • Take moments about the centre of mass, or about a fixed point, and be explicit which — mixing the two is the standard error.
  • Newton–Euler solved together gives both the motion and the reactions, which is what bearings and mounts must be sized for.
  • The same pairing reappears throughout machine design whenever an accelerating component's supports are checked.

DYN 152 · Lesson 09 · Rotational energy and momentum

Lesson 09Rotational energy and momentum

Key points

  • A rotating body stores \( KE=\tfrac12I\omega^2 \), the rotational twin of \( \tfrac12mv^2 \).
  • Angular momentum is \( L=I\omega \), and angular impulse changes it: \( \int M\,dt=\Delta L \).
  • With no external moment, angular momentum is conserved — so reducing \( I \) must raise \( \omega \).
  • That single statement explains the spinning skater, the governor, and the behaviour of any rotor that sheds or gathers mass.
  • A flywheel is an energy buffer sized by \( \tfrac12I\omega^2 \), smoothing a fluctuating torque between strokes.
  • Because energy goes as \( \omega^2 \), speed matters far more than mass — the reason flywheels run fast rather than heavy.

DYN 152 · Lesson 10 · Balancing of rotors

Lesson 10Balancing of rotors

Key points

  • Unbalance is any offset between the mass centre and the axis of rotation, from casting tolerance, machining, keyways, wear or deposits.
  • It produces a rotating force \( F=mr\omega^2 \), synchronous with shaft speed and growing with the square of it.
  • Doubling speed quadruples the force — why a rotor acceptable at one speed is destructive at another.
  • Static unbalance is a single offset correctable in one plane; dynamic unbalance is a couple and needs correction in two planes.
  • A rotor can be statically balanced and still shake, which is exactly why two-plane balancing exists.
  • Because the force is at 1× shaft speed, vibration analysis identifies unbalance by its frequency — the diagnostic link into condition monitoring.

DYN 152 · Lesson 11 · Introduction to vibration

Lesson 11Introduction to vibration

Key points

  • The single-degree-of-freedom mass-spring-damper is the model behind nearly every vibration problem.
  • Its natural frequency \( \omega_n=\sqrt{k/m} \) depends on stiffness and mass alone — not on how hard it is driven.
  • The damping ratio \( \zeta \) sorts the response into underdamped oscillation, critical damping, and overdamped crawl.
  • Resonance is forcing near \( \omega_n \), where response is limited only by damping and can grow far beyond the static deflection.
  • Fixing a resonance means changing stiffness, mass or forcing frequency; adding damping limits the peak but does not move it.
  • This is the foundation the Vibrations course and every critical-speed check in machine design build on.
