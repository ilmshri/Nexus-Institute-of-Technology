MAT 153 · Lesson 01 · Phase diagrams

Lesson 01Phase diagrams

Key points

  • A phase diagram is a map of equilibrium: which phases exist at a given composition and temperature, given unlimited time.
  • Inside a two-phase field the tie line gives each phase's composition, and the lever rule gives how much of each is present.
  • The lever rule is inverted — the longer arm gives the larger fraction — which is the usual place it is got backwards.
  • An isomorphous system is completely soluble in both states; a eutectic has a single lowest-melting composition that freezes at one temperature.
  • The eutectic reaction produces a characteristic two-phase lamellar structure, and it is the reason casting alloys sit near that composition.
  • Gibbs' phase rule states how many variables are still free, which is why a pure metal melts at a fixed temperature while an alloy melts over a range.

MAT 153 · Lesson 02 · The iron–carbon system and heat treatment

Lesson 02The iron–carbon system and heat treatment

Key points

  • The iron–carbon diagram is the phase map every steel decision starts from: ferrite, austenite, cementite and their mixtures.
  • Austenite dissolves far more carbon than ferrite — the entire basis of heat treating steel.
  • The eutectoid reaction at 727 °C turns austenite into pearlite, alternating ferrite and cementite lamellae.
  • Steels below 0.77% C are hypoeutectoid, above it hypereutectoid, and the lever rule gives the phase amounts either side.
  • The diagram is an equilibrium map, so it describes slow cooling only.
  • Quenching escapes it entirely: martensite appears nowhere on the diagram, which is the point of the next lesson.

MAT 153 · Lesson 03 · Annealing, normalizing, and surface hardening

Lesson 03Annealing, normalizing, and surface hardening

Key points

  • Full annealing softens and homogenises; normalizing air-cools to a finer, stronger pearlite; stress relieving removes residual stress without changing structure; spheroidizing maximises machinability.
  • Cooling rate sets lamellar spacing, and finer spacing means harder, stronger steel — the same composition can span a wide property range.
  • Quenching to martensite gives maximum hardness and unusable brittleness, so tempering always follows.
  • Tempering trades hardness back for toughness, and the temperature chosen is the design decision.
  • Case hardening — carburizing, nitriding, induction — puts a hard shell on a tough core, which is what a gear tooth or a cam actually needs.
  • The case also carries compressive residual stress, which is a direct fatigue benefit on top of the wear resistance.

MAT 153 · Lesson 04 · Carbon and alloy steels

Lesson 04Carbon and alloy steels

Key points

  • The designation is readable: in AISI/SAE four-digit steels the first two digits give the alloy family and the last two the carbon content in hundredths of a percent.
  • Carbon buys hardness and strength and costs ductility, toughness and weldability.
  • Alloying elements mostly buy hardenability — depth of hardening — rather than raw hardness.
  • That distinction matters: a plain-carbon steel can be as hard at the surface and soft a few millimetres in.
  • The Jominy end-quench test measures hardenability directly by hardness against distance from a quenched end.
  • Selection therefore starts from section thickness and required core properties, not from a strength number alone.

MAT 153 · Lesson 05 · Stainless steels and cast irons

Lesson 05Stainless steels and cast irons

Key points

  • Stainless steels split into austenitic, ferritic, martensitic and duplex families with genuinely different properties — non-magnetic, formable, hardenable, and strong respectively.
  • Corrosion resistance comes from a passive chromium-oxide film, which needs at least about 11% chromium and oxygen to maintain itself.
  • Sensitization is the classic failure: chromium carbides form at grain boundaries in the 500–800 °C range, leaving those boundaries chromium-depleted.
  • The bulk plate is untouched while the boundary corrodes — which is why heat-affected zones fail and the fix is low-carbon or stabilised grades.
  • Cast irons are one base metal with three graphite shapes: flakes (grey), nodules (ductile), and compacted.
  • Graphite shape governs everything — flakes act as internal cracks giving brittleness and excellent damping, nodules give real ductility.

MAT 153 · Lesson 06 · Nonferrous alloys

Lesson 06Nonferrous alloys

Key points

  • Aluminium alloys are wrought or cast, and the temper designation records the treatment: T6 is solution-treated and artificially aged.
  • Age hardening precipitates fine second-phase particles that obstruct dislocations — the strengthening mechanism.
  • The age-hardening curve peaks and then falls: past the peak the precipitates coarsen and the alloy gets weaker.
  • More ageing time is therefore not better, and overaged material cannot be recovered by ageing further.
  • Copper alloys bring conductivity, corrosion resistance and bearing behaviour; brasses and bronzes differ in the main alloying element.
  • Nickel alloys hold strength and corrosion resistance where steels cannot, at a cost that has to be justified by the duty.

MAT 153 · Lesson 07 · Fatigue, fracture, and creep

Lesson 07Fatigue, fracture, and creep

Key points

  • Fatigue is failure under cyclic stress well below yield, described by the S–N curve.
  • Steels show a genuine endurance limit; aluminium does not, so an aluminium part has a finite life at any stress.
  • Fracture mechanics adds the missing variable — crack size — through \( K=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a} \) compared against \( K_{IC} \).
  • It answers what a stress calculation cannot: how large a flaw a part can tolerate.
  • A fracture surface is evidence: beach marks mean fatigue, cup-and-cone means ductile overload, cleavage facets mean brittle fracture.
  • Creep is time-dependent deformation under sustained load above roughly 0.4 of the melting temperature — a design limit for anything hot.

MAT 153 · Lesson 08 · Polymers and elastomers

Lesson 08Polymers and elastomers

Key points

  • Thermoplastics soften and can be reprocessed; thermosets are cross-linked and cannot, degrading rather than melting.
  • The glass transition \( T_g \) separates glassy from rubbery behaviour and is the defining temperature for any polymer part.
  • Below \( T_g \) an elastomer is brittle, not elastic — a seal below its \( T_g \) simply stops sealing.
  • Polymers creep at room temperature under sustained load, so a bolted plastic joint relaxes with time.
  • O-ring selection is a chemical-compatibility problem first and a temperature problem second.
  • In Gulf service the real risks are the upper thermal-oxidative ceiling and explosive decompression in gas duty, not \( T_g \).

MAT 153 · Lesson 09 · Ceramics and composites

Lesson 09Ceramics and composites

Key points

  • Ceramics are strong in compression, weak in tension, and fail from the largest flaw present rather than at a fixed stress.
  • That makes their strength statistical: nominally identical specimens break at scattered loads.
  • The Weibull distribution describes it, and the Weibull modulus measures scatter — a higher modulus means more consistent material.
  • A single measured strength is therefore close to meaningless for a ceramic; a distribution and a survival probability are required.
  • Bigger parts are weaker because they contain more flaws, so specimen data must be scaled to component size.
  • Refractories serve as linings; fibre composites put strong fibres in a matrix so properties depend on direction, which must be designed for deliberately.

MAT 153 · Lesson 10 · Environmental degradation preview

Lesson 10Environmental degradation preview

Key points

  • Corrosion is electrochemical: it needs an anode, a cathode, an electrolyte and a return path, and removing any one stops it.
  • Dry oxidation follows different rules, and a protective oxide is judged by whether it is adherent and coherent.
  • In a galvanic couple the less noble metal corrodes, at a rate set by the area ratio.
  • A small anode against a large cathode is the trap — steel fasteners in a stainless plate fail fast, while the reverse arrangement is tolerable.
  • Passivity is both shield and trap: the film protects, but if it breaks locally the tiny bare spot becomes an intense anode and pits.
  • Screening a risk means naming the mechanism — galvanic, pitting, crevice, stress-corrosion, erosion — because each has a different remedy.

MAT 153 · Lesson 11 · Materials selection in practice

Lesson 11Materials selection in practice

Key points

  • Materials are screened by property indices — ratios matched to the load case — not by a single headline number.
  • The index follows from the objective: minimum mass for a stiff beam is \( E^{1/2}/\rho \), for a strong beam \( \sigma_y^{2/3}/\rho \).
  • Build the index by writing the objective, the constraint and the free variable, then eliminating the free variable.
  • Plotted on a property chart, an index is a straight line, and the best candidates sit furthest along it.
  • Cost, availability, joinability and supply chain then reduce the shortlist further — the theoretically optimum material is often unbuyable.
  • Document the constraint, the index, the shortlist and the reason for the final choice, or the selection cannot be reviewed later.
