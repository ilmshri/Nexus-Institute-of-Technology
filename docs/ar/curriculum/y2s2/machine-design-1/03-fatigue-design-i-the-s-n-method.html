Fatigue design I: the S–N method

Endurance limits and the modification factors — surface, size, load, temperature, reliability. Where the knockdowns come from and why they stack. Infinite-life design for steel done properly.

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Name three endurance-limit knockdown factors and what each punishes.
  • Why does a ground surface out-live a machined one in fatigue?

01 · Why static strength is not the answer

A shaft that never approaches yield can still fail, and usually does. Fatigue is damage accumulated by fluctuating stress: a crack initiates at a surface discontinuity, grows a little on each cycle, and severs the section at a nominal stress the static calculation declared safe by a wide margin. Most rotating-machinery failures are fatigue failures, and none of them are visible to a yield check.

02 · The endurance limit and where it comes from

Test enough polished specimens and steels show a stress amplitude below which life becomes effectively unlimited — the endurance limit. For wrought steels a serviceable first estimate is \( S_e' = 0.5\,S_{ut} \) (capped near 700 MPa). The prime matters: that value belongs to a mirror-polished 7.6 mm rotating-bending specimen tested at room temperature, which is not the part you are designing.

03 · The Marin factors — paying for reality

Key relation — corrected endurance limit

\[ S_e = k_a k_b k_c k_d k_e \, S_e' \]

\( k_a \) surface finish, \( k_b \) size, \( k_c \) load type, \( k_d \) temperature, \( k_e \) reliability. Each is at most 1.0, so they only ever reduce — and because they multiply, they stack fast.

Their physical logic is worth holding onto rather than memorising. Surface finish dominates because fatigue cracks start at the surface, so roughness supplies ready-made initiation sites: a machined finish costs about 17% at 600 MPa, and a hot-rolled or forged surface costs far more. Size reduces strength because a larger section exposes more highly-stressed volume, so the worst flaw present is statistically worse. Reliability is not a physical property at all but a choice about how far into the scatter band you design: 50% reliability means half of parts fail sooner than predicted, which is why 90%, 99% or 99.9% carry escalating penalties.

04 · The estimating method

One: get \( S_{ut} \) for the actual material and condition, not the generic grade. Two: take \( S_e' = 0.5 S_{ut} \). Three: evaluate each Marin factor against the real part — its finish, its diameter, its loading, its service temperature, and the reliability the duty demands. Four: multiply. Five: compare the stress amplitude against \( S_e/n \), never the peak stress, because fatigue is driven by the alternating component. And carry \( K_f \), the fatigue concentration factor, always — the ductile-static permission of Lesson 2 does not survive here.

The characteristic number to remember is the size of the total knockdown. In the worked case below, five factors that each look modest combine to leave 58% of the specimen value. An engineer who designs to \( 0.5 S_{ut} \) directly is not being slightly optimistic; they are claiming nearly twice the fatigue strength the part has.

Closing. The endurance limit is a laboratory number until you have paid for your part's surface, size and required reliability. Then it is a design stress.

Glossary

TermEquationWhen to use

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

What the specimen value costs after correction

Problem. A machined steel shaft of 30 mm diameter runs in rotating bending at room temperature. The steel has \( S_{ut} = 600\ \mathrm{MPa} \); the duty requires 99% reliability (\( k_e = 0.814 \)). Use \( k_a = 4.51\,S_{ut}^{-0.265} \) and \( k_b = 1.24\,d^{-0.107} \). Find: \( S_e' \), each Marin factor, and the corrected endurance limit \( S_e \).

Solution. (1) \( S_e' = 0.5\times600 = 300\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (2) \( k_a = 4.51\times600^{-0.265} = 0.828 \). (3) \( k_b = 1.24\times30^{-0.107} = 0.862 \). (4) \( k_c = 1 \) (bending), \( k_d = 1 \) (room temperature), \( k_e = 0.814 \). (5) \( S_e = 0.828\times0.862\times1\times1\times0.814\times300 = 174.2\ \mathrm{MPa} \).

Answer: \( S_e \approx 174\ \mathrm{MPa} \). Sanity check: the total knockdown is \( 174.2/300 = 0.581 \) — the corrected limit is 58% of the specimen value, and 29% of \( S_{ut} \). Designing against \( 0.5S_{ut} = 300 \) would have claimed 1.72 times the fatigue strength this shaft actually possesses.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Buying strength with surface finish

Problem. The same shaft is ground rather than machined, so \( k_a = 1.58\,S_{ut}^{-0.085} \). All other factors are unchanged. Find: the new \( k_a \), the new \( S_e \), and the percentage gain in endurance limit.

Solution. (1) \( k_a = 1.58\times600^{-0.085} = 0.917 \). (2) \( S_e = 0.917\times0.862\times0.814\times300 = 193.0\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (3) Gain: \( 193.0/174.2 - 1 = 0.108 \), i.e. 10.8%.

Answer: \( k_a = 0.917 \), \( S_e = 193\ \mathrm{MPa} \), a 10.8% gain. Sanity check: grinding buys about a tenth more fatigue strength for one extra manufacturing operation — often the cheapest strength available, and far cheaper than the larger diameter that would otherwise be needed. It is also why a drawing note specifying surface finish in a fillet is a structural requirement, not cosmetic.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Amplitude, not peak

Problem. A component cycles between \( \sigma_{min} = 20\ \mathrm{MPa} \) and \( \sigma_{max} = 140\ \mathrm{MPa} \). Find: the stress amplitude and mean stress, and the factor of safety on infinite life against \( S_e = 174\ \mathrm{MPa} \), ignoring the mean-stress effect for now.

Solution. (1) Amplitude: \( \sigma_a = (140-20)/2 = 60\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (2) Mean: \( \sigma_m = (140+20)/2 = 80\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (3) \( n = S_e/\sigma_a = 174/60 = 2.9 \).

Answer: \( \sigma_a = 60 \), \( \sigma_m = 80\ \mathrm{MPa} \), \( n = 2.9 \). Sanity check: comparing the peak 140 MPa against \( S_e \) instead would have reported \( n = 1.24 \) — needlessly alarming — while ignoring that the 80 MPa mean stress is itself damaging in a way this calculation has not yet accounted for. Lesson 4 supplies the Goodman treatment that reconciles the two.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

For a wrought steel with \( S_{ut} = 800\ \mathrm{MPa} \), the first estimate of the specimen endurance limit \( S_e' \) is

The standard first estimate for wrought steel is \( S_e' = 0.5\,S_{ut} = 400\ \mathrm{MPa} \), subject to the cap near 700 MPa that applies to very high-strength steels. Answering 800 MPa confuses the endurance limit with the ultimate strength itself. The 240 MPa figure applies 0.3 rather than 0.5, and 1600 MPa doubles instead of halving — fatigue strength is always below static strength, so any answer above \( S_{ut} \) can be rejected on sight.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

Which Marin factor is typically the largest single knockdown for an as-forged component?

Fatigue cracks initiate at the surface, so surface condition dominates: a rough as-forged skin supplies ready-made initiation sites and can cut the endurance limit by more than half on a strong steel. Size is real but milder over ordinary shaft diameters — around 0.86 at 30 mm. Load type applies a fixed adjustment for axial or torsional loading rather than a finish-dependent one, and temperature is 1.0 until the part runs hot. This ranking is why grinding a fillet is such a cost-effective structural intervention.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

Five Marin factors of 0.83, 0.86, 1.0, 1.0 and 0.81 are applied to \( S_e' = 300\ \mathrm{MPa} \). The corrected endurance limit is closest to

The factors multiply: \( 0.83\times0.86\times0.81 = 0.578 \), and \( 0.578\times300 \approx 174\ \mathrm{MPa} \). The 250 MPa answer comes from averaging the factors instead of multiplying, which badly understates the stacking; 300 MPa ignores the correction entirely. The 90 MPa answer roughly adds the shortfalls rather than compounding the factors, over-penalising the part. Multiplication is the point: several individually modest factors combine to remove more than 40% of the specimen value.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Fatigue design compares which stress quantity against the corrected endurance limit?

Fatigue damage is driven by the alternating component, so the stress amplitude \( \sigma_a = (\sigma_{max}-\sigma_{min})/2 \) is the quantity compared against \( S_e \). Using the maximum stress conflates the steady and fluctuating parts and reports a needlessly low factor of safety. The mean stress is not ignored — it genuinely reduces allowable amplitude — but it enters through a Goodman or Gerber construction rather than by direct comparison. The yield stress governs a different failure mode entirely.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Raising the required reliability from 50% to 99% changes \( k_e \) from 1.000 to 0.814. What does this represent physically?

Reliability is not a property of the material but a decision about scatter. The endurance limit measured across many specimens is a distribution; designing at 50% reliability means half of parts are expected to fail earlier than predicted, which is rarely acceptable, so the design stress is moved down the distribution and \( k_e \) falls. The steel is unchanged. Temperature is handled separately by \( k_d \), and size by \( k_b \); neither has anything to do with the reliability choice.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Why must the fatigue stress-concentration factor be carried through a fatigue calculation, when \( K_t \) is often set aside for static ductile design?

The static permission rests on local yielding redistributing the peak once. Under cyclic loading the notch root is exactly where a crack initiates and then grows a little on every cycle, so the concentration governs the life rather than being blunted away. Fatigue loads are typically smaller than the static ones a part survives, which is what makes fatigue failures surprising. \( K_t \) is a purely geometric factor defined regardless of loading, and the last option inverts the logic of nominal stress.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A shaft's diameter is increased from 30 mm to 60 mm. Using \( k_b = 1.24\,d^{-0.107} \), the size factor

The exponent is negative, so \( k_b \) falls as diameter grows: from 0.862 at 30 mm to about 0.800 at 60 mm, a reduction of roughly 7%. The physical reason is statistical — a larger section exposes a greater volume of highly-stressed material, so the worst flaw present is likely to be worse. Note this concerns strength per unit area: the bigger shaft still carries far more load, because section modulus grows with the cube of diameter while \( k_b \) declines only weakly. The factor certainly depends on geometry, and the decline is nothing like proportional.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A designer sizes a machined 30 mm shaft directly against \( 0.5\,S_{ut} \), omitting the Marin factors. Compared with the corrected value of 174 MPa, the claimed fatigue strength is too high by a factor of about

The claim is 300 MPa against an actual 174 MPa, a ratio of \( 300/174 = 1.72 \). This is the practical significance of the whole lesson: the omission is not a rounding matter but nearly a factor of two, and it is invisible in the arithmetic because every individual step is correct. A part designed this way can pass every check in the calculation package and still fail in service, which is precisely the pattern that makes fatigue the dominant failure mode in rotating machinery.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the shaft that passed every static check. A cooling-water pump shaft fails through a keyway after eighteen months. The original calculation is retrieved and is arithmetically flawless: peak stress well below yield, factor of safety above four. It contains no fatigue check at all. Re-worked under this lesson's method, the picture inverts — the corrected endurance limit for the machined 30 mm section at 99% reliability is about 174 MPa, not the 300 MPa a bare \( 0.5S_{ut} \) would have claimed, and the keyway's fatigue concentration applies in full because the ductile-static permission does not survive cyclic loading. The replacement shaft is ground at the fillet and the keyway radius increased; both changes buy fatigue strength rather than section. The instructive part is that no one made a calculation error. They answered the wrong question, thoroughly.
