Design project: a driven shaft assembly

Integrative project: size the shaft, keys, bolts, and welds of a belt-driven assembly against static and fatigue criteria. Calculations documented for review. The pattern of defensible design work.

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • In the shaft-assembly project, which element failed first on paper, and what did you change?
  • What must design documentation let a reviewer do?

01 · The duty statement comes first

An 11 kW motor drives a countershaft at 970 rpm through a V-belt onto a 250 mm pulley. The shaft runs in two bearings 300 mm apart with the pulley at midspan, and delivers its torque through a bolted flange coupling. The bearing pedestals are fillet-welded to a base plate. Size the shaft, the key, the coupling bolts and the pedestal welds.

Before any sizing, nail down the duty, because every number downstream inherits it. \( T = P/\omega = 11{,}000/101.6 = 108.3\ \mathrm{N\,m} \). That is the one quantity the whole assembly shares, and it is worth writing at the top of the calculation sheet where nobody can recompute it differently on page four.

02 · Follow the load path

The discipline that makes an integrative problem tractable is to trace force and torque from where they enter to where they leave, and to size whatever they pass through on the way. Here the path is: belt → pulley → key → shaft → bearings → pedestals → welds → base plate, with torque branching off through the coupling.

The belt does two things at once, and forgetting the second is the classic error. It delivers torque through the tension difference: \( T_1-T_2 = T/r = 866\ \mathrm{N} \). But it also pulls on the shaft with the tension sum. At a typical V-belt ratio \( T_1/T_2 = 3 \), that gives \( T_2 = 433 \) and \( T_1 = 1300\ \mathrm{N} \), so the shaft carries a transverse pull of \( T_1+T_2 = 1733\ \mathrm{N} \) — twice the force that does the useful work. With the pulley at midspan the bearing reactions are 866 N each and the peak bending moment is \( 866 \times 0.150 = 130\ \mathrm{N\,m} \).

Note that the bending moment is larger than the torque. This is normal for a belt drive and it is why belt-driven shafts are almost always bending-governed.

03 · Sizing, component by component

The shaft (Lesson 5). Reversed bending, steady torque, and a keyway at the pulley seat giving \( K_f=2.2 \), \( K_{fs}=1.8 \). DE-Goodman at \( n=2 \) returns \( d=34.0\ \mathrm{mm} \); specify 35 mm, at which the actual margin is 2.19.

The key (Lesson 6). A 10 × 8 mm key runs the full 55 mm hub. The tangential force is \( 2T/d = 6188\ \mathrm{N} \) and the bearing stress 28.1 MPa, giving a margin of 6.0. As always, the hub length set the key, not the torque.

The coupling bolts (Lesson 7). Four M10 grade 8.8 bolts on a 90 mm bolt circle, preloaded to \( 0.75A_tS_p = 26.1\ \mathrm{kN} \) each. The joint is friction-driven — the bolts are not in shear — so capacity is \( T=\mu n F_i r = 0.15(4)(26{,}100)(0.045) = 705\ \mathrm{N\,m} \), a margin of 6.5.

The pedestal welds (Lesson 8). The weld group is a 120 × 80 mm rectangle carrying 866 N of direct shear and a 130 N·m tipping moment. At unit throat the resultant is 9.28 N/mm, needing 0.064 mm of throat. The minimum 5 mm fillet the plate thickness demands gives a margin of 55.

04 · Reading the answer, and writing it down

Line those four margins up — 2.2, 6.0, 6.5, 55 — and the design tells you something no individual calculation does. It lives entirely in the shaft. Every other component is governed by something that is not the load: the hub length, the smallest sensible bolt, the minimum fillet for the plate. If the duty rose 20% tomorrow, the shaft would need attention and nothing else would move.

That is worth knowing before anyone proposes an improvement. Heavier coupling bolts would buy nothing. A larger weld would buy nothing. Attention belongs on the shaft — and, per Lesson 5, the cheapest attention there is the keyway and the fillet radius, not more diameter.

The other half of the work is the documentation, and it is the half that gets skipped. A calculation package that another engineer can check needs the duty stated once and referenced everywhere; every assumption visible and attributed (\( K_f=2.2 \) for the keyway, \( T_1/T_2=3 \) for the belt, \( \mu=0.15 \) at the flange faces); the governing criterion named for each component; and the answer separated from the specification, because \( d=34.0\ \mathrm{mm} \) computed and 35 mm specified are two different facts and a reviewer needs both. Anything you would have to reconstruct from memory in a year is not documented.

Closing. One duty, one load path, four components, and a set of margins that says where the design actually is. The arithmetic here is Lessons 3 through 10 with nothing new added; what is new is the obligation to make it reviewable.

الأسس

What this lesson assumes

  • Every preceding lesson in this course. Lesson 3 for \( S_e \), Lesson 4 for Goodman, Lesson 5 for the shaft, Lesson 6 for the key, Lesson 7 for the bolted flange, Lesson 8 for the welds. Nothing here is new theory.
  • Belt drive mechanics — that torque comes from \( T_1-T_2 \) while shaft load comes from \( T_1+T_2 \).
  • Beam reactions and bending moments (Statics) — locating the peak moment on a simply supported shaft.
  • Power, torque and speed (Physics I) — \( T=P/\omega \) with \( \omega \) in rad/s.

Glossary

TermEquationWhen to use
Duty torque\( T=P/\omega \)The single quantity the whole assembly shares; state it once.
Belt driving force\( T_1-T_2=T/r \)The part of belt tension that does useful work.
Belt shaft load\( T_1+T_2 \)The transverse pull the shaft and bearings actually carry — larger, and easily forgotten.
Shaft sizing\( d^3=\frac{16n}{\pi}\left[\frac{2K_fM_a}{S_e}+\frac{\sqrt3K_{fs}T_m}{S_{ut}}\right] \)Reversed bending with steady torque (Lesson 5).

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

From motor rating to bending moment

Problem. An 11 kW motor drives a countershaft at 970 rpm through a V-belt onto a 250 mm pulley with tension ratio \( T_1/T_2 = 3 \). The shaft runs in bearings 300 mm apart with the pulley at midspan. Find: the duty torque, the two belt tensions, the transverse shaft load, and the maximum bending moment.

Solution. (1) \( \omega = 970(2\pi)/60 = 101.6\ \mathrm{rad/s} \), so \( T = 11{,}000/101.6 = 108.3\ \mathrm{N\,m} \). (2) \( T_1-T_2 = T/r = 108.3/0.125 = 866\ \mathrm{N} \). (3) With \( T_1=3T_2 \): \( 2T_2 = 866 \), so \( T_2 = 433 \) and \( T_1 = 1300\ \mathrm{N} \). (4) Transverse load \( T_1+T_2 = 1733\ \mathrm{N} \); each bearing reacts \( 866\ \mathrm{N} \). (5) \( M = 866(0.150) = 130.0\ \mathrm{N\,m} \).

Answer: \( T=108.3\ \mathrm{N\,m} \), \( T_1=1300 \), \( T_2=433\ \mathrm{N} \), shaft pull 1733 N, \( M=130.0\ \mathrm{N\,m} \). Sanity check: the transverse pull is exactly twice the driving force, which is what \( T_1/T_2=3 \) implies — \( (T_1+T_2)/(T_1-T_2) = 4/2 = 2 \). Note that the bending moment ends up larger than the torque. A designer who sized this shaft on torque alone would be ignoring the bigger of the two loads.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

The shaft, and what it is really made of

Problem. Size the shaft for \( M_a = 130.0\ \mathrm{N\,m} \) reversed and \( T_m = 108.3\ \mathrm{N\,m} \) steady, with \( S_e = 174 \), \( S_{ut} = 600\ \mathrm{MPa} \), \( K_f = 2.2 \), \( K_{fs} = 1.8 \), \( n = 2.0 \). Find: the required diameter and the actual margin if 35 mm is specified.

Solution. (1) Amplitude term: \( 2K_fM_a/S_e = 2(2.2)(130.0)/174\times10^6 = 3.286\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3} \). (2) Mean term: \( \sqrt3K_{fs}T_m/S_{ut} = 1.732(1.8)(108.3)/600\times10^6 = 5.627\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^3} \). (3) \( d^3 = 10.186(3.849\times10^{-6}) = 3.920\times10^{-5} \), so \( d = 34.0\ \mathrm{mm} \). (4) At 35 mm: \( \sigma_a = 32(2.2)(130.0)/\pi(0.035)^3 = 67.9\ \mathrm{MPa} \) and the von Mises mean is 40.1 MPa, so \( n = 1/(67.9/174 + 40.1/600) = 2.19 \).

Answer: \( d = 34.0\ \mathrm{mm} \) computed, 35 mm specified, actual margin 2.19. Sanity check: the amplitude term is 5.8 times the mean term, confirming the shaft is bending-governed as the load figures predicted. Note the two distinct numbers worth recording: 34.0 mm is the calculation's answer and 35 mm is the specification, and a reviewer needs both to tell a rounded-up standard size from a padded one.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

The coupling that carries torque without shearing a bolt

Problem. The 108.3 N·m is transmitted through a flange coupling with four M10 grade 8.8 bolts (\( A_t = 58.0\ \mathrm{mm^2} \), \( S_p = 600\ \mathrm{MPa} \)) on a 90 mm bolt circle. The flange faces have \( \mu = 0.15 \) and the bolts are preloaded to 75% of proof. Find: the preload per bolt, the friction torque capacity, and the margin.

Solution. (1) \( F_i = 0.75(58.0\times10^{-6})(600\times10^6) = 26{,}100\ \mathrm{N} \) per bolt. (2) Friction acts at the bolt-circle radius \( r = 0.045\ \mathrm{m} \): \( T = \mu nF_ir = 0.15(4)(26{,}100)(0.045) = 705\ \mathrm{N\,m} \). (3) \( n = 705/108.3 = 6.5 \).

Answer: \( F_i = 26.1\ \mathrm{kN} \) per bolt, capacity 705 N·m, margin 6.5. Sanity check: the bolts never appear in shear in this calculation, and that is the design intent — a properly preloaded flange transmits torque by friction between the faces, with the bolts supplying clamp force only. Note the consequence for maintenance: the coupling's entire capacity is the preload, so a bolt tightened by feel rather than to specification removes a quarter of it.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

A V-belt drive with \( T_1/T_2 = 3 \) delivers a driving force of 866 N. The transverse load it applies to the shaft is

Torque comes from the tension difference, but the shaft is pulled by the tension sum. With \( T_1 = 3T_2 \) and \( T_1-T_2 = 866 \), we get \( T_2 = 433 \), \( T_1 = 1300 \), and \( T_1+T_2 = 1733\ \mathrm{N} \) — twice the driving force. Taking 866 N as the shaft load, the most common error here, would halve the bending moment and undersize the shaft. The 2599 N answer is \( 3T_2\times2 \) and has no physical meaning.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

In this assembly the margins come out as shaft 2.2, key 6.0, bolts 6.5, welds 55. The correct reading is

Only the shaft was sized by the load. The key was made the length of the hub, the bolts are the smallest sensible size for a flange of that diameter, and the fillet is the minimum the plate thickness permits — so their margins are outcomes, not choices, and cannot be 'reduced' without changing something other than strength. Averaging margins is meaningless: a chain's strength is its weakest link, and any uprate or improvement must go to the shaft.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

On a belt-driven countershaft, the bending moment frequently exceeds the torque. The design consequence is that

Bending on a rotating shaft is fully reversed, so it lands in the amplitude term over \( S_e \); steady torque lands in the much more forgiving mean term over \( S_{ut} \). With the moment already larger in magnitude, the amplitude term ends up 5.8 times the mean term here. Torsion is not negligible — it still sizes the key and the coupling — and the tension ratio matters a great deal, since it sets how much transverse load accompanies a given torque.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

A flange coupling's bolts are described as carrying torque "by friction". This means

A properly preloaded flange transmits torque across the joint faces, exactly as Lesson 7 described for any preloaded joint — the bolts are tension members whose job is clamp force. Capacity is \( \mu nF_ir \), containing no bolt shear area at all. The maintenance implication is direct and often missed: the coupling's rating is the preload, so under-tightened bolts reduce torque capacity in proportion even though nothing looks loose.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

The pedestal weld group needed a throat of 0.064 mm but a 5 mm fillet was specified. This means

Minimum fillet sizes exist for reasons unrelated to the applied load — heat input, fusion into thick plate, and practical control of the process. When the load requires far less, the correct outcome is to specify the minimum and record why. The calculation still earns its place: without it nobody knows whether the fillet is comfortable or marginal, and it is the evidence that a future duty increase has room here. This is the same pattern as the key in Lesson 6.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

The duty on this assembly rises by 30%. Which component requires attention first?

Uprating is governed by the smallest margin. The shaft at 2.19 drops to roughly 1.7, below the design basis, while the key, bolts and welds remain comfortable. The last option is the trap: it is true that every margin exceeded 2 at the original duty, but that says nothing about the new one. Note too that a belt drive's bending moment grows with the duty alongside its torque, so both terms of the shaft equation increase together.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

The key on this 35 mm shaft carries a tangential force of 6188 N at a bearing stress of 28.1 MPa, against an allowable of 170 MPa. The right conclusion is

The margin of 6.0 is an outcome of making the key the full length of the hub, exactly as Lesson 6 described — no one chose it from the torque. That surplus is doing real work: it covers motor starting and jam transients that running torque never shows, while still leaving the key weaker than the shaft so an overload costs the cheap part. Shortening it to the load requirement would delete the transient capacity. The allowable belongs to the key's own material, since it is the key being crushed.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Which of these belongs in a calculation package a colleague can check a year later?

Reviewability means someone else can reach the same numbers without asking questions. That requires the shared duty in one place, visible assumptions — \( K_f=2.2 \), \( T_1/T_2=3 \), \( \mu=0.15 \) — since they are judgements another engineer may contest, the criterion each component was judged against, and both the computed and specified dimensions, because a rounded-up standard size and a padded one look identical on a drawing. Final sizes alone cannot be checked, and picking the most conservative of several methods after the fact selects the answer before the method.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the drive that was uprated on paper. A countershaft assembly of exactly this kind is asked to carry a 30% duty increase after a process change. The review is quick: the coupling bolts have a margin of 6.5, the key 6.0, the welds far more, so the assembly is declared adequate and the motor is changed. The shaft fails in fatigue through the pulley keyway inside a year. Nothing in the review was arithmetically wrong; the error was reading four margins as one number. Only the shaft was near its limit at 2.19, and a 30% duty rise took it to about 1.7 — below the design basis, while every other component remained comfortable and kept the summary looking healthy. Two habits would have caught it. First, uprating is governed by the smallest margin, not the average or the impression: the question "which component governs, and what does it become at the new duty?" has one answer, and it is the only one that matters. Second, a belt drive's bending moment rises with the duty as well as its torque, so both terms of the shaft equation grow together — a detail an average-based review never surfaces. The correct outcome was a larger shaft with a ground fillet and a relocated keyway, at a fraction of the eventual repair.

Unit versatility

The theory above is in SI. Engineering practice also runs in US/Imperial and CGS — convert each quantity, then check. Get one wrong and the engine hands you a fresh variant of the same conversion.
