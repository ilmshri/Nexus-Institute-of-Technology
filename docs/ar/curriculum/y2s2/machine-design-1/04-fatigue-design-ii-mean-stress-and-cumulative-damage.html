Fatigue design II: mean stress and cumulative damage

Goodman and Gerber treatments of mean stress. Miner's rule with its known dishonesty acknowledged. Fluctuating-load components assessed end to end.

Lecture 15 Dynamic Failure Theories – Goodman Diagram, Fatigue Mean Stress, Concentration Factors anJOSHUA TARBUTTON

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • What does the Goodman line trade between mean and alternating stress?
  • State Miner's rule and its known dishonesty.

01 · The gap Lesson 3 left open

Lesson 3 finished with a factor of safety of 2.9 on a component cycling between 20 and 140 MPa, and flagged the omission plainly: the calculation compared the amplitude against \( S_e \) and ignored the 80 MPa mean stress entirely. That mean stress is not harmless. A steady tensile load holds a crack open, so each cycle does more damage than the same amplitude would about zero mean. Fatigue strength therefore depends on two numbers, and a one-dimensional comparison cannot express the answer.

02 · Constant-life criteria

The standard treatment plots mean stress horizontally and amplitude vertically, and draws a boundary between the survivable region and the rest. Every criterion in use passes through the same two anchors — \( S_e \) on the amplitude axis, where there is no mean stress, and \( S_{ut} \) on the mean axis, where there is no alternation and the part simply pulls apart. They differ only in the curve drawn between them.

Key relation — the Goodman criterion

\[ \frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sigma_m}{S_{ut}} = \frac{1}{n} \]

A straight line between the two anchors. Gerber replaces the second term with \( (\sigma_m/S_{ut})^2 \), bulging above the line; Soderberg replaces \( S_{ut} \) with the yield strength \( S_y \), pulling below it. Setting \( \sigma_m = 0 \) collapses all three to \( n = S_e/\sigma_a \) — Lesson 3's calculation, recovered exactly.

Which to use is a judgement, not a lookup. Goodman is linear, always conservative against test data, and trivially invertible for design — which is why it dominates practice. Gerber follows the experimental scatter band more closely and returns a less punishing answer: on the worked case below, 2.56 against Goodman's 2.09, a 22% difference that is real money in material. Soderberg alone guarantees no yielding at the first peak, because it is anchored at \( S_y \), but it is conservative enough that most designers run Goodman for fatigue and a separate first-cycle yield check instead.

03 · Variable amplitude and Miner's rule

Real duty is not one amplitude. A crane hoists light loads often and heavy loads rarely; a pump shaft sees startup transients on top of running load. The standard accounting device is the Palmgren–Miner rule: each block of \( n_i \) cycles at an amplitude whose life is \( N_i \) consumes the fraction \( n_i/N_i \), and failure is predicted when the fractions sum to one.

Key relation — cumulative damage

\[ D = \sum_i \frac{n_i}{N_i} \qquad \text{failure predicted at } D = 1 \]

\( N_i \) comes from the finite-life line \( S_f = a N^{\,b} \), fitted between \( 0.9 S_{ut} \) at \( 10^3 \) cycles and \( S_e \) at \( 10^6 \).

04 · Miner's rule is known to be wrong

It is worth being blunt about this, because the rule is used everywhere and defended nowhere. Miner's rule assumes damage accumulates linearly and independently of order. Neither is true. Test data puts failure anywhere from \( D \approx 0.5 \) to \( D \approx 2 \), and sequence matters: a few high-amplitude cycles early can drive a crack that subsequent low-amplitude cycles then extend, whereas the same high cycles applied last may find nothing to grow. A large overload can even be beneficial, leaving a compressive residual stress at the notch root that retards later growth.

The rule survives because it is simple, because it is roughly unbiased across many tests, and because nothing appreciably better is available without crack-growth analysis. Use it — and treat a computed \( D \) of 0.9 as "no margin demonstrated", not as "passed".

Closing. Mean stress costs amplitude, and the constant-life diagram is where that trade is priced. Variable duty is summed with Miner's rule, whose answer is an estimate with a factor of two around it, not a number to design to the last digit.

الأسس

What this lesson assumes

  • The corrected endurance limit (Lesson 3) — \( S_e \) is the vertical anchor of every diagram drawn here; this lesson does not re-derive it.
  • Stress amplitude and mean stress (Lesson 3) — \( \sigma_a \) and \( \sigma_m \) as the two coordinates a fatigue state is expressed in.
  • Ultimate and yield strength (Materials I L7) — \( S_{ut} \) and \( S_y \), the two candidate anchors on the mean-stress axis.
  • Logarithms (Mathematics I) — the S–N line is a power law; solving it for life means taking logs.

Glossary

TermEquationWhen to use
Goodman criterion\( \sigma_a/S_e + \sigma_m/S_{ut} = 1/n \)Default fatigue check with mean stress present; conservative and easy to invert for design.
Gerber criterion\( n\sigma_a/S_e + (n\sigma_m/S_{ut})^2 = 1 \)When a less punishing, better-centred estimate is wanted and the extra algebra is acceptable.
Soderberg criterion\( \sigma_a/S_e + \sigma_m/S_y = 1/n \)When no first-cycle yielding may be permitted at all.
Finite-life line\( S_f = aN^{\,b} \)Reading a life off the S–N curve between \( 10^3 \) and \( 10^6 \) cycles.
Line coefficients\( a=(0.9S_{ut})^2/S_e \), \( b=-\tfrac13\log_{10}(0.9S_{ut}/S_e) \)Fitting that line from two known endpoints.
Miner's rule\( D=\sum n_i/N_i \)Summing damage over a duty spectrum; failure predicted at \( D=1 \), with real scatter of roughly 0.5–2.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

What the mean stress actually costs

Problem. Lesson 3 left a component cycling between \( \sigma_{min}=20 \) and \( \sigma_{max}=140\ \mathrm{MPa} \) with a reported factor of safety of 2.9 against \( S_e = 174\ \mathrm{MPa} \), the mean stress ignored. The steel has \( S_{ut}=600\ \mathrm{MPa} \). Find: the factor of safety by Goodman and by Gerber, and say what the mean stress cost.

Solution. (1) \( \sigma_a=(140-20)/2=60 \), \( \sigma_m=(140+20)/2=80\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (2) Goodman: \( 60/174 + 80/600 = 0.3448+0.1333=0.4782 \), so \( n=1/0.4782=2.09 \). (3) Gerber: solve \( 0.3448\,n + (0.1333\,n)^2 = 1 \), i.e. \( 0.01778n^2+0.3448n-1=0 \), giving \( n=2.56 \).

Answer: Goodman \( n=2.09 \), Gerber \( n=2.56 \). Sanity check: both are below Lesson 3's 2.9, as they must be — adding a damaging mean stress cannot raise the margin. Goodman costs 28% of the mean-free answer and Gerber 12%. Setting \( \sigma_m=0 \) in either expression returns exactly 2.90, which is the arithmetic confirming the two lessons agree where they overlap.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Adding up a duty cycle

Problem. A shaft of the same steel (\( S_{ut}=600 \), \( S_e=174\ \mathrm{MPa} \)) sees, per year: 10,000 cycles at 300 MPa amplitude, 30,000 at 250 MPa, and 100,000 at 200 MPa, all fully reversed. Fit the finite-life line between \( 0.9S_{ut} \) at \( 10^3 \) cycles and \( S_e \) at \( 10^6 \). Find: the annual damage \( D \), and how many years of this duty Miner's rule allows.

Solution. (1) Endpoints: \( 0.9\times600=540\ \mathrm{MPa} \) at \( 10^3 \), \( 174\ \mathrm{MPa} \) at \( 10^6 \). (2) \( b=-\tfrac13\log_{10}(540/174)=-0.1639 \); \( a=540^2/174=1675.9\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (3) Lives from \( N=(S_f/a)^{1/b} \): at 300 MPa, \( N=36{,}060 \); at 250 MPa, \( N=109{,}647 \); at 200 MPa, \( N=427{,}660 \). (4) \( D = 10{,}000/36{,}060 + 30{,}000/109{,}647 + 100{,}000/427{,}660 = 0.2773+0.2736+0.2338 = 0.785 \). (5) Years to \( D=1 \): \( 1/0.785 = 1.27 \).

Answer: \( D=0.785 \) per year; failure predicted at about 1.3 years. Sanity check: the line is verified at its own endpoint — \( 1675.9\times(10^6)^{-0.1639}=174.0\ \mathrm{MPa} \), the corrected endurance limit it was fitted to. Note how evenly the three blocks contribute, 0.23 to 0.28 each: the rare high-amplitude cycles do as much damage as the hundred thousand mild ones, which is exactly the intuition a running-load-only calculation lacks.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Designing to a target margin

Problem. The same steel and \( S_e=174\ \mathrm{MPa} \). A joint carries an unavoidable mean stress of 80 MPa and must show a Goodman factor of safety of 2.0. Find: the largest stress amplitude permitted, and the peak stress that corresponds to.

Solution. (1) Rearranging Goodman for amplitude: \( \sigma_a = S_e\left(\tfrac1n - \tfrac{\sigma_m}{S_{ut}}\right) \). (2) \( \sigma_a = 174\,(0.5 - 80/600) = 174\times0.3667 = 63.8\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (3) Peak: \( \sigma_{max} = \sigma_m+\sigma_a = 80+63.8 = 143.8\ \mathrm{MPa} \).

Answer: \( \sigma_a \le 63.8\ \mathrm{MPa} \), peak 143.8 MPa. Sanity check: substituting back, \( 63.8/174 + 80/600 = 0.3667+0.1333 = 0.500 = 1/2 \) — the target margin exactly. Note the peak stress of 144 MPa is a quarter of \( S_{ut} \) and well under any plausible yield strength: the part is nowhere near static failure, which is the ordinary condition of a fatigue-governed design.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

On a constant-life diagram, the Goodman line runs between which two anchor points?

With no mean stress the part survives an amplitude of \( S_e \), fixing the vertical anchor; with no alternation it is a static tensile member that fails at \( S_{ut} \), fixing the horizontal one. Soderberg is the criterion that anchors at \( S_y \) instead, and it does so on the mean axis, not the amplitude axis. Using \( 0.5S_{ut} \) would put the uncorrected specimen value on the diagram, discarding the Marin factors Lesson 3 spent its length establishing.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

A component has \( \sigma_a = 90 \) and \( \sigma_m = 150\ \mathrm{MPa} \), with \( S_e = 174 \) and \( S_{ut} = 600\ \mathrm{MPa} \). The Goodman factor of safety is closest to

\( 90/174 + 150/600 = 0.5172 + 0.2500 = 0.7672 \), and \( n = 1/0.7672 = 1.30 \). The 1.93 answer is \( S_e/\sigma_a \), the mean stress ignored — the very error this lesson exists to correct. The 0.77 figure is the sum itself, which is \( 1/n \) and not the margin. A margin of 1.30 on a fatigue calculation is thin: the inputs carry more scatter than that.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

Compared with Goodman, the Gerber criterion generally

Gerber's parabola bulges above Goodman's straight line everywhere between the two shared anchors, so it permits more amplitude at a given mean stress and returns the larger margin — 2.56 against 2.09 on this lesson's worked case. That is the point of it: the straight line is a deliberately conservative simplification of a data set the parabola sits centrally within. The two agree only at the anchors themselves, where \( \sigma_m \) is zero or \( \sigma_a \) is zero.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Which criterion is the one that guarantees no yielding on the first load cycle?

Soderberg anchors the mean-stress axis at \( S_y \) rather than \( S_{ut} \), so satisfying it necessarily keeps \( \sigma_m + \sigma_a \) below yield. Goodman and Gerber both anchor at \( S_{ut} \) and can therefore pass a combination that yields locally on the first cycle, which is why they are normally run alongside a separate static yield check. Miner is not a constant-life criterion at all — it is the damage-summation rule for variable amplitude.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A duty spectrum gives \( n_1/N_1 = 0.45 \) and \( n_2/N_2 = 0.40 \). Miner's rule predicts

The fractions add: \( D = 0.45+0.40 = 0.85 \), below the predicted failure value of 1. The 0.18 answer multiplies them, which has no physical meaning. Miner's rule exists precisely to combine blocks of different amplitude, so the last option inverts its purpose. The honest reading of 0.85 is that the design has consumed most of its predicted life with the rule's own scatter — roughly 0.5 to 2 — wider than the remaining 0.15.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Why can the order in which load blocks are applied change the actual life, contrary to Miner's assumption?

Sequence matters through the crack itself. High cycles applied early initiate damage that subsequent low-amplitude cycles — which alone might never start a crack — are perfectly able to grow; applied last, the same cycles may find nothing to extend. Separately, a single large overload can yield the notch root and leave a compressive residual stress there that retards later growth, so an overload can lengthen life. Neither effect exists in a linear sum. The ultimate strength is not altered by this, and the dimensionlessness of \( D \) is beside the point.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A finite-life line is fitted between 540 MPa at \( 10^3 \) cycles and 174 MPa at \( 10^6 \) cycles. The exponent \( b \) is closest to

\( b = -\tfrac13\log_{10}(540/174) = -\tfrac13(0.4916) = -0.164 \). The \( -0.492 \) answer omits the factor of one third, which is the three decades of life between the endpoints. A positive exponent would make fatigue strength rise with life, which is backwards. The \( -3.00 \) figure inverts the relationship — it is roughly \( 1/b \), the exponent used when solving for \( N \) from a known stress rather than the reverse.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A designer reports a Goodman margin of 2.09 and a Gerber margin of 2.56 for the same component and asks which to put in the calculation package. The defensible answer is

What makes a fatigue number defensible is that the criterion is named and the inputs are traceable, not that a particular curve was chosen. Goodman is the ordinary default because it is conservative against the data and invertible for design, so it is the natural entry — with Gerber available as the argued case if the extra 22% is needed and justified. Averaging two criteria produces a curve nobody has validated. Choosing whichever clears the target is selecting the answer first and the method afterwards, which is the one approach that cannot be defended in review.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the duty cycle nobody added up. A reciprocating-compressor tie rod is specified against its running load and passes comfortably. It fails after three years. The running load was never the problem: the rod also sees a startup transient at roughly 300 MPa amplitude, a few thousand cycles per year of unloaded-to-loaded transition near 250 MPa, and the steady running cycles near 200 MPa. Each block on its own is survivable — the 200 MPa duty alone would run past four hundred thousand cycles. Summed with Miner's rule the annual damage comes to 0.78, which is not a pass; it means the rod is predicted to fail somewhere in the second year of identical duty, and the observed three years sits inside the rule's normal factor-of-two scatter. Two changes follow. The startup ramp is lengthened so the transient amplitude drops, which is free, and the rod is re-specified with a rolled rather than cut thread, which raises \( S_e \) and lengthens every block at once. The instructive part is that the original calculation was not wrong about any single load. It simply never asked what the loads did together.
