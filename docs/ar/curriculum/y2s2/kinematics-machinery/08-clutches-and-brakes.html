Clutches and brakes

Friction torque of plate and cone clutches under uniform-pressure and uniform-wear assumptions — and which one to trust in service. Band and shoe brakes with self-energizing behavior located. The heat released in every engagement, and where it goes.

Design of clutch: Construction, Uniform Pressure theory and Uniform Wear theoryJOKU

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Uniform pressure versus uniform wear: which predicts less torque, and why is it the honest one for a worn clutch?
  • What makes a brake shoe self-energizing — and when does that become self-locking?

§1 · The same device, read in two directions

A clutch connects two rotating shafts; a brake connects a rotating shaft to the frame. Both work by pressing friction surfaces together and both are analysed identically — a normal pressure distribution, a friction coefficient, and an integration for torque. The difference is what happens to the energy: a clutch converts the difference in kinetic energy between two shafts into heat, a brake converts all of it. Everything else is common.

Design asks three questions in order. What torque must it transmit or absorb? What axial force produces that torque? And what happens to the heat? The first two are geometry and friction; the third is usually what actually limits the device, and it is the one most often left until last.

§2 · Uniform pressure and uniform wear, and which one to believe

Consider an annular plate clutch with outer diameter \( D \) and inner diameter \( d \), pressed by an axial force \( F \). To integrate the friction torque, the pressure distribution across the annulus must be assumed, and there are two standard choices.

Uniform pressure assumes \( p \) is constant across the face — true for a rigid, flat, newly-machined pair. Uniform wear assumes that wear rate is proportional to \( pV \), and since sliding velocity \( V \) is proportional to radius, uniform wear requires \( pr = \) constant. The pressure is then highest at the inner radius and falls outward.

Key relations — plate clutch torque, one friction surface

\[ T_{\text{wear}}=\frac{\mu F(D+d)}{4},\qquad T_{\text{pressure}}=\frac{\mu F}{3}\cdot\frac{D^3-d^3}{D^2-d^2} \]

Multiply by the number of friction surfaces — a single plate gripped on both faces has two.

Work an example: \( D = 250 \) mm, \( d = 150 \) mm, \( \mu = 0.30 \), \( F = 5 \) kN, one surface. Uniform wear gives \( 0.30(5000)(0.400)/4 = 150.0 \) N·m. Uniform pressure gives \( \frac{0.30(5000)}{3}\cdot\frac{0.015625-0.003375}{0.0625-0.0225} = 153.1 \) N·m.

Uniform wear predicts the smaller torque, and it is the honest assumption for the same reason it predicts less: a clutch spends its first hour as a uniform-pressure device and the rest of its life as a uniform-wear one. The pressure migrates inward, where the radius arm is shorter, so the same axial force produces less torque. Designing to the uniform-pressure figure specifies a clutch that meets its rating when new and slips later — and the difference here is only 2%, which is exactly what makes the error easy to accept and hard to notice.

The wear assumption also carries a design result worth knowing: for a given outer diameter and a limiting pressure, torque is maximised at \( d = D/\sqrt{3} = 0.577D \), which for a 250 mm clutch is 144 mm. Narrower annuli waste face area; wider ones put material where the pressure limit forces the axial force down.

§3 · Band and shoe brakes: geometry that helps, then takes over

A band brake wraps a flexible band around a drum. The tension is not the same at both ends — friction feeds it — and the relation is the same one that governs a rope on a capstan.

Key relation — band tensions

\[ \frac{T_1}{T_2}=e^{\mu\theta},\qquad T_{\text{brake}}=(T_1-T_2)\,r \]

\( \theta \) is the wrap angle in radians. The exponential is the reason a band brake gives a large torque from a small actuating force.

With \( \mu=0.30 \), a 270° wrap and a 200 mm drum radius, \( e^{\mu\theta}=4.111 \). A slack-side tension of 500 N gives a tight side of 2056 N and a braking torque of \( (2056-500)(0.200)=311 \) N·m — from an actuator holding 500 N. Note that the wrap angle enters exponentially while the radius enters linearly: adding half a turn of wrap buys far more than growing the drum.

Shoe brakes introduce a sharper effect. The friction force on a shoe produces a moment about the shoe's own pivot. If that moment acts in the same sense as the applied force, the friction helps press the shoe on: the shoe is self-energizing, and a small actuating force gives a large braking torque. Reverse the drum's rotation, or place the pivot on the other side, and the same friction moment opposes the actuation: the shoe is de-energizing and needs a much larger force.

Self-energizing is a genuine design tool and a genuine hazard. Push it far enough and the friction moment alone equals the applied moment, at which point the brake grabs with no actuating force at all — self-locking. Since \( \mu \) rises when a lining is cold, wet or glazed, a brake designed close to the self-locking boundary is a brake whose behaviour changes with the weather. Practice keeps a margin, and reserves strongly self-energizing designs for duties where a grab is acceptable.

§4 · Heat, which is what actually limits the device

Every engagement converts kinetic energy to heat in the friction interface. For a clutch bringing a load of inertia \( I \) up to a shaft speed \( \omega \) from rest, the energy dissipated equals the kinetic energy the load ends up with — the driving side does twice that work, and half of it is lost in the slip.

Key relation — energy per engagement

\[ E=\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2 \]

Multiply by the engagement rate for the heat the device must reject continuously.

Take \( I = 0.5\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \) accelerated to 1500 rev/min, so \( \omega = 157.1 \) rad/s: \( E = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.5)(157.1)^2 = 6169 \) J per engagement. One engagement is harmless — spread through a 5 kg steel disc it is a rise of \( 6169/(5\times460) = 2.7\ \mathrm{K} \). Ten engagements a minute is a different machine: \( 6169\times10/60 = 1028 \) W flowing continuously into a component with a modest surface area.

That is why a duty cycle belongs in a clutch or brake specification alongside the torque. Torque capacity is set by geometry and friction and does not care how often the device is used; thermal capacity cares about nothing else. The classic field failure is a device correctly sized for torque and used at a rate nobody specified: the lining overheats, \( \mu \) falls with temperature, the torque falls with it, so it slips longer, which puts in more heat. Fade is that loop, and it converges on a device that no longer works while every number on its datasheet remains true.

الأسس

What this lesson assumes

  • Coulomb friction (Statics L8) — \( F=\mu N \), integrated here over an area rather than applied at a point.
  • Belt friction (Statics L9) — \( T_1/T_2=e^{\mu\theta} \), used unchanged for the band brake.
  • Rotational kinetic energy (Dynamics L8) — \( \tfrac12 I\omega^2 \), the energy that becomes heat at every engagement.
  • Specific heat (Thermodynamics I) — converting that energy into a temperature rise.
  • Contact pressure (Machine Design I L3) — the limit the annulus proportions are chosen against.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Plate clutch torque under both assumptions

Problem. A single-surface plate clutch has \( D = 250 \) mm, \( d = 150 \) mm, \( \mu = 0.30 \) and an axial force of 5 kN. Find the torque under the uniform-wear and uniform-pressure assumptions, say which should be used, and find the maximum contact pressure in each case.

Solution. (1) Uniform wear: \( T = \dfrac{\mu F(D+d)}{4} = \dfrac{0.30(5000)(0.250+0.150)}{4} = \dfrac{600}{4} = 150.0\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \).

(2) Uniform pressure: \( T = \dfrac{\mu F}{3}\cdot\dfrac{D^3-d^3}{D^2-d^2} = \dfrac{1500}{3}\cdot\dfrac{0.015625-0.003375}{0.0625-0.0225} = 500\left(\dfrac{0.012250}{0.040000}\right) = 500(0.30625) = 153.1\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \).

(3) Uniform wear is the design value: it is lower, and it describes the clutch after run-in, which is nearly all of its life.

(4) Uniform pressure: \( p = \dfrac{F}{\pi(r_o^2-r_i^2)} = \dfrac{5000}{\pi(0.015625-0.005625)} = 0.159\ \mathrm{MPa} \), the same everywhere.

(5) Uniform wear: \( F = 2\pi p_{\max}r_i(r_o-r_i) \), so \( p_{\max} = \dfrac{5000}{2\pi(0.075)(0.050)} = 0.212\ \mathrm{MPa} \) at the inner radius, falling to \( 0.212(75/125) = 0.127\ \mathrm{MPa} \) at the outer.

Answer: 150.0 N·m (design) against 153.1 N·m; peak pressure 0.212 MPa on the inner edge, against a uniform 0.159 MPa. Sanity check: the wear assumption gives 2% less torque and 33% more peak pressure, and both differences point the same way — the run-in clutch is the more heavily loaded and the weaker one. The 2% torque gap is small enough to be dismissed and the pressure gap is not, which is the practical reason the wear assumption is standard: it protects the lining, not just the rating.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

A band brake, and what wrap angle is worth

Problem. A band brake has a 200 mm drum radius, \( \mu = 0.30 \) and a 270° wrap. The actuator holds the slack side at 500 N. Find the tight-side tension and the braking torque. Then find what a 360° wrap would give at the same slack-side tension, and compare with increasing the drum radius by the same proportion.

Solution. (1) \( \theta = 270^\circ = 4.712\ \mathrm{rad} \), so \( e^{\mu\theta} = e^{0.30(4.712)} = e^{1.4137} = 4.111 \).

(2) \( T_1 = 4.111(500) = 2056\ \mathrm{N} \).

(3) \( T_{\text{brake}} = (T_1-T_2)r = (2056-500)(0.200) = 311.1\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \).

(4) At 360°, \( \theta = 6.283 \) rad and \( e^{\mu\theta} = e^{1.885} = 6.586 \), so \( T_1 = 3293 \) N and the torque is \( (3293-500)(0.200) = 558.6\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \) — a 79.6% increase for a 33% increase in wrap.

(5) Increasing the radius by the same 33%, to 266.7 mm, gives \( (2056-500)(0.2667) = 414.8\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \) — only 33% more.

Answer: 2056 N and 311.1 N·m; more wrap gives 558.6 N·m against 414.8 for the same proportional increase in radius. Sanity check: the asymmetry is structural, not numerical: wrap angle sits inside an exponential and radius multiplies linearly. That is why band brakes are wrapped as far as the mechanism allows before the drum is enlarged, and why the actuating force needed is so modest — 500 N holding back 311 N·m. The same exponential is what makes the brake sensitive to \( \mu \): a lining whose friction falls 20% when hot loses far more than 20% of its torque.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Thermal duty of a clutch (with a US customary check)

Problem. A clutch accelerates a load of \( I = 0.5\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \) from rest to 1500 rev/min. Find the heat dissipated per engagement, the temperature rise if it all goes into a 5 kg steel disc (\( c = 460\ \mathrm{J/kg\,K} \)), and the continuous heat load at 10 engagements per minute. Express that heat load in horsepower.

Solution. (1) \( \omega = 2\pi(1500)/60 = 157.08\ \mathrm{rad/s} \).

(2) The energy lost in the slip equals the kinetic energy the load gains: \( E = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = \tfrac12(0.5)(157.08)^2 = 0.25(24674) = 6169\ \mathrm{J} \).

(3) \( \Delta T = \dfrac{E}{mc} = \dfrac{6169}{5(460)} = 2.68\ \mathrm{K} \).

(4) At 10 per minute: \( P = 6169(10)/60 = 1028\ \mathrm{W} \).

(5) \( 1028\ \mathrm{W} \div 746 = 1.38\ \mathrm{hp} \).

Answer: 6169 J per engagement, 2.68 K per engagement, 1028 W (1.38 hp) continuous. Sanity check: the single-engagement figure looks harmless and the continuous one does not — a kilowatt into a small friction disc is a serious cooling problem, and that gap is exactly why duty cycle belongs in the specification next to torque. Note also that the driving side does 12 338 J of work, twice the energy the load ends up with; the other half is what is being dissipated. Half of the input energy of every from-rest engagement becomes heat, regardless of how good the clutch is.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

The uniform-wear assumption for a plate clutch requires that

Wear rate goes as \( pV \), and sliding velocity \( V \) is proportional to radius, so uniform wear needs \( pr \) constant — the pressure highest at the inner radius and falling outward. That is the state a clutch settles into after run-in, and it is why the run-in clutch produces slightly less torque than the new one: the pressure has migrated to the shorter moment arm.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

For the same axial force, uniform wear predicts less torque than uniform pressure. The correct design practice is to

A clutch is a uniform-pressure device for its first hour and a uniform-wear device thereafter, so the wear value is both the lower and the representative one. Designing to the pressure figure specifies a device that meets its rating when new and slips later. The gap is only about 2% for the worked geometry, which is precisely what makes the mistake comfortable to make.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

For a given outer diameter and a limiting contact pressure, plate-clutch torque is greatest when the inner diameter is

Under the uniform-wear condition the torque is \( \mu p_{\max}\pi d(D^2-d^2)/8 \), which is maximised at \( d = D/\sqrt3 = 0.577D \) — 144 mm for a 250 mm clutch. Making \( d \) very small does not help: the pressure limit then caps the axial force before the extra area can be used, and the innermost material contributes little torque because its radius arm is short.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

A band brake's wrap angle is increased from 270° to 360° at the same slack-side tension. The braking torque

The ratio \( e^{\mu\theta} \) rises from 4.111 to 6.586, so with a 500 N slack side the tight side goes from 2056 to 3293 N and the torque from 311 to 559 N·m — 79.6%, from a 33% increase in wrap. Wrap angle sits inside an exponential; drum radius multiplies linearly, so the same proportional increase in radius would have given only 33%.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A brake shoe is self-energizing when

It is a geometric relationship between the pivot position and the direction of drum rotation: when the friction force's moment about the pivot helps press the shoe on, a small actuating force produces a large braking torque. Reverse the rotation and the same shoe is de-energizing. Push the effect far enough and the brake self-locks — it grabs with no actuating force at all.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A brake is designed close to its self-locking boundary. The practical danger is that

The self-locking condition depends on \( \mu \), which is not a constant — it rises when a lining is cold, damp or glazed. A design with no margin therefore behaves differently with the weather and the duty history, grabbing when it should modulate. Strongly self-energizing designs are reserved for duties where a grab is acceptable, and everything else is given margin.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A single plate gripped between two pressure plates transmits, compared with a single friction surface at the same axial force,

Torque scales with the number of friction surfaces, and a plate gripped on both faces has two. This is the standard way a clutch is uprated without changing its diameter or its actuating force, and it is why multi-plate clutches are used where space is tight. It does nothing for the thermal problem — the same energy per engagement is now shared between more surfaces, which helps the peak temperature but not the total heat to be rejected.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A clutch correctly sized for torque overheats in service. The most likely cause is

Torque capacity depends on geometry and friction and is indifferent to how often the device is used; thermal capacity depends on nothing else. A clutch dissipating 6169 J per engagement is harmless once and a 1028 W heat source at ten engagements a minute. The failure is self-reinforcing: heat lowers \( \mu \), lower \( \mu \) means longer slip, longer slip means more heat — which is what fade is.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the clutch that met its rating and still slipped. A jaw-crusher drive was fitted with a plate clutch selected against a required torque of 150 N·m. The selection sheet showed the clutch rated at 153 N·m and the order was placed. It worked at commissioning, and within two months it was slipping under load and the lining was glazed.

Two errors compounded, both of them arithmetic. The 153 N·m figure had been computed under the uniform-pressure assumption; the run-in clutch follows uniform wear and delivers \( \mu F(D+d)/4 = 150.0 \) N·m, exactly the required torque and no margin at all. Second, the duty was ten engagements a minute against an inertia of about \( 0.5\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \) at 1500 rev/min, so each engagement dissipated \( \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = 6169 \) J and the clutch was carrying about 1028 W of continuous heat. Hot linings lose friction, and a clutch with zero margin loses its rating the moment \( \mu \) drops a few per cent. It then slipped longer per engagement, which added heat.

Two practices follow. Clutch torque is quoted under an assumption, and the assumption belongs next to the number — a 2% difference matters when the selection margin is zero, which is the case in which someone chose the higher figure. And a clutch specification without an engagement rate is incomplete: the thermal duty is usually the real limit, and it does not appear anywhere in a torque calculation.
