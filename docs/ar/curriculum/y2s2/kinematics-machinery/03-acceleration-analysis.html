Acceleration analysis

Acceleration polygons and the Coriolis component in sliding contacts. Slider-crank acceleration solved completely. The source terms for inertia forces.

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Where does Coriolis acceleration appear in a quick-return mechanism?
  • Why does acceleration, not velocity, size inertia forces?

§1 · The derivative that sends the bill

Position tells you whether the parts fit. Velocity tells you how fast the machine runs. It is acceleration that sends the bill, because acceleration is what inertia responds to: every link that is being accelerated pushes back on whatever accelerates it, with \( ma \) at its centre of mass and \( I_G\alpha \) about it. Those reactions travel through the pins into the bearings and the frame, and they are usually far larger than the useful load.

The scale is easy to underestimate. The slider-crank in the figure — a 50 mm crank and a 200 mm rod at 200 rad/s, about 1910 rev/min — puts 2500 m/s² on the piston at top dead centre. A piston of only 0.5 kg then needs 1250 N to accelerate it, 255 times its own weight. Nothing in the machine cares what the piston weighs; everything cares what it is being accelerated to.

Two more consequences follow from the same number. Inertia forces go as \( \omega^2 \), so raising a machine's speed by 20% raises them by 44% — which is why speeding up existing machinery is a structural question, not a motor question. And they reverse direction twice per revolution, so they are a fatigue load, not a static one.

§2 · The slider-crank, exactly and usefully

Put the crank pin at radius \( r \) and the rod at length \( L \). The piston position follows from the closed loop directly, with \( n = L/r \):

\[ x = r\left(\cos\theta + \sqrt{n^2-\sin^2\theta}\,\right) \]

Differentiating twice with respect to time at constant \( \omega \) gives the exact acceleration, and expanding the square root for \( n \gg 1 \) gives the form actually used.

Key relation — piston acceleration

\[ a = -r\omega^2\left[\cos\theta + \frac{n^2\cos 2\theta + \sin^4\theta}{(n^2-\sin^2\theta)^{3/2}}\right] \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad a \approx -r\omega^2\left(\cos\theta + \frac{\cos 2\theta}{n}\right) \]

The first term is the primary component, at crank frequency; the second is the secondary, at twice crank frequency, and it exists only because the rod has finite length. \( n \to \infty \) would delete it.

The approximation is better than it looks. At \( \theta = 0 \) both give \( -r\omega^2(1+1/n) = -2500 \) m/s² exactly; at \( 180^\circ \) both give \( +1500 \) m/s². The largest disagreement over the whole revolution is at \( \theta = 90^\circ \), where the approximation reads 500 against the exact 516.4 m/s² — 3.2% low, and low at the point where the acceleration is small anyway. That is why the two-term form survives in engine practice: it is accurate where the loads are large and only approximate where they are not.

The secondary term is not a rounding detail. Its amplitude is \( 1/n \) of the primary, so at \( n=4 \) it contributes a quarter as much, at twice the frequency — and it is the reason a four-cylinder engine that is perfectly balanced at crank frequency still shakes at twice it. Lesson 10 returns to exactly this term.

§3 · Acceleration of a linkage: the loop, differentiated twice

For a general linkage there is no closed form worth memorising; there is a procedure. Take the velocity equation and differentiate again. Each link contributes two acceleration terms: a centripetal part \( \omega^2 r \) pointing from the moving pin back at the centre of rotation, and a tangential part \( \alpha r \) perpendicular to the link.

Key relation — acceleration difference

\[ \mathbf{a}_B=\mathbf{a}_A+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_3\times\mathbf{r}_{B/A} -\omega_3^2\,\mathbf{r}_{B/A} \]

Everything on the right except \( \alpha_3 \) is known once the velocity analysis is done, so the two scalar components give two linear equations in the two unknown angular accelerations.

Applied to the four-bar of Lesson 2 — ground 100, crank 40, coupler 120, rocker 90 mm, \( \omega_2 = 10 \) rad/s constant, so \( \alpha_2=0 \) — the solution is \( \alpha_3 = 33.05 \) rad/s² and \( \alpha_4 = 39.93 \) rad/s². Note what a constant input speed did not buy: both driven links are accelerating hard. A motor running at perfectly steady speed still generates inertia loads throughout the mechanism, because the mechanism's own geometry keeps changing the ratio, as Lesson 2 showed.

Drawn rather than solved, the same equation is an acceleration polygon: lay the known vectors head to tail, and the two unknown tangential components close the figure. It is the same arithmetic with a ruler, and it has the same virtue as the instant-centre construction — it is an independent check.

§4 · Coriolis, and why it is the term that gets forgotten

Everything above assumed each pair of links keeps a fixed distance. When one link slides along another that is itself rotating — a block in a swinging slot, the shoe of a quick-return, a follower running in a rotating guide — a fifth term appears.

Key relation — Coriolis acceleration

\[ \mathbf{a}_{\text{cor}}=2\,\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}} \]

Present whenever sliding happens on a rotating member; perpendicular to the sliding velocity; zero only if the guide is not rotating or the block is not sliding.

Its size is routinely underestimated. Take a slotted lever turning at \( \omega = 8 \) rad/s with \( \alpha = 12 \) rad/s², carrying a block 0.25 m from the pivot that is sliding outward at 1.5 m/s with 4 m/s² of its own. The four contributions are: centripetal \( \omega^2 r = 16 \), tangential \( \alpha r = 3 \), relative 4, and Coriolis \( 2\omega v = 24 \) m/s². Coriolis is the largest of them — eight times the tangential term. Collecting components along and across the slot gives \( -12 \) and \( +27 \) m/s², a total of 29.5 m/s². Leave Coriolis out and the answer is 12.4 m/s²: 58% low, and 58% low on a bearing load.

The practical rule is to look for the sliding pair first. If any joint in the chain lets one link slide along another that rotates, the term is there, it is perpendicular to the slot, and it is often the dominant one. Quick-return mechanisms, Scotch yokes and swinging-block linkages all carry it.

الأسس

What this lesson assumes

  • Velocity analysis (Lesson 2) — the acceleration equations are the velocity equations differentiated once more, and reuse their results.
  • Normal and tangential acceleration (Dynamics L2) — \( v^2/\rho \) and \( dv/dt \), reappearing as \( \omega^2r \) and \( \alpha r \).
  • Rotating reference frames (Dynamics L6) — the source of the Coriolis term.
  • Newton's second law (Physics I) — why acceleration, not velocity, sets the force.
  • Binomial expansion (Mathematics I) — expanding \( \sqrt{n^2-\sin^2\theta} \) is what produces the two-term form.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Piston acceleration, exact against approximate

Problem. A slider-crank has crank \( r = 50 \) mm, connecting rod \( L = 200 \) mm and runs at a constant \( \omega = 200 \) rad/s. Find the piston acceleration at \( \theta = 0^\circ \), \( 90^\circ \) and \( 180^\circ \) using the two-term approximation, and compare with the exact values −2500.00, +516.40 and +1500.00 m/s². State where the approximation is worst.

Solution. \( n = L/r = 200/50 = 4 \) and \( r\omega^2 = 0.050(200)^2 = 2000\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \).

(1) \( \theta = 0^\circ \): \( a = -2000\left(\cos 0 + \frac{\cos 0}{4}\right) = -2000(1+0.25) = -2500\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \). Exact value \( -2500.00 \) — error 0.00%.

(2) \( \theta = 90^\circ \): \( a = -2000\left(0 + \frac{-1}{4}\right) = +500\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \). Exact \( +516.40 \) — error \( (500-516.40)/516.40 = -3.18\% \).

(3) \( \theta = 180^\circ \): \( a = -2000\left(-1 + \frac{1}{4}\right) = +1500\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \). Exact \( +1500.00 \) — error 0.00%.

Answer: −2500, +500 and +1500 m/s²; the approximation is worst at \( 90^\circ \), 3.2% low. Sanity check: the errors land where they should. At 0° and 180° the crank and rod are collinear, the geometry is exactly captured by two terms, and the answers are exact. The error appears at 90° — where the acceleration is smallest, so 3.2% of a small number. The peak magnitude is 2500 m/s² at top dead centre, and that is where the approximation has no error at all, which is why it is trusted for sizing.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Angular accelerations of a four-bar

Problem. The Lesson 2 linkage — ground 100, crank 40, coupler 120, rocker 90 mm — runs at a constant \( \omega_2 = 10 \) rad/s, so \( \alpha_2 = 0 \). At \( \theta_2 = 60^\circ \) the earlier analysis gave \( \theta_3 = 24.95^\circ \), \( \theta_4 = 71.34^\circ \), \( \omega_3 = -0.905 \) and \( \omega_4 = 3.525 \) rad/s. Find \( \alpha_3 \) and \( \alpha_4 \).

Solution. Differentiating the loop twice and separating components gives two linear equations in \( \alpha_3, \alpha_4 \). Collecting the known terms:

\( A = r_4\sin\theta_4 = 90\sin 71.34^\circ = 85.27 \); \( B = r_3\sin\theta_3 = 120\sin 24.95^\circ = 50.63 \); \( D = r_4\cos\theta_4 = 28.80 \); \( E = r_3\cos\theta_3 = 108.80 \).

\( C = r_2\omega_2^2\cos\theta_2 + r_3\omega_3^2\cos\theta_3 - r_4\omega_4^2\cos\theta_4 = 2000.0 + 89.15 - 322.10 = 1767.05 \).

\( F = -r_2\omega_2^2\sin\theta_2 - r_3\omega_3^2\sin\theta_3 + r_4\omega_4^2\sin\theta_4 = -3464.10 - 41.48 + 953.65 = -2551.93 \).

\( \alpha_3 = \frac{CD-AF}{AE-BD} = \frac{(1767.05)(28.80) - (85.27)(-2551.93)}{(85.27)(108.80)-(50.63)(28.80)} = \frac{50\,891 + 217\,600}{9277.4-1458.1} = 33.05\ \mathrm{rad/s^2} \).

\( \alpha_4 = \frac{CE-BF}{AE-BD} = \frac{(1767.05)(108.80)-(50.63)(-2551.93)}{7819.3} = \frac{192\,255 + 129\,204}{7819.3} = 39.93\ \mathrm{rad/s^2} \).

Answer: \( \alpha_3 = 33.05 \) rad/s², \( \alpha_4 = 39.93 \) rad/s². Sanity check: differentiating \( \omega_3 \) and \( \omega_4 \) numerically across \( \theta_2 = 60^\circ \) reproduces both to four decimals. The physical reading matters more than the arithmetic: the input shaft is at perfectly constant speed, and the coupler and rocker are still accelerating at 33 and 40 rad/s². Constant input speed does not mean an inertia-free mechanism — the varying velocity ratio of Lesson 2 guarantees the opposite.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

The Coriolis term in a quick-return

Problem. A block slides in the slot of a rotating lever. At the instant of interest the lever turns at \( \omega = 8 \) rad/s with \( \alpha = 12 \) rad/s², and the block is 0.25 m from the pivot, sliding outward along the slot at 1.5 m/s and accelerating along it at 4 m/s². Find the block's total acceleration, and find what it would come to if the Coriolis term were omitted.

Solution. Four contributions, resolved along the slot (radial, outward positive) and across it (transverse):

(1) Centripetal \( \omega^2 r = 8^2(0.25) = 16\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \), radial inward.

(2) Tangential \( \alpha r = 12(0.25) = 3\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \), transverse.

(3) Relative \( a_{\text{rel}} = 4\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \), radial outward.

(4) Coriolis \( 2\omega v_{\text{rel}} = 2(8)(1.5) = 24\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \), transverse, in the same sense as the tangential term for outward sliding.

Radial: \( -16 + 4 = -12\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \). Transverse: \( 3 + 24 = 27\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \).

\( a = \sqrt{12^2+27^2} = \sqrt{144+729} = 29.55\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \).

Without Coriolis: transverse \( = 3 \), so \( a = \sqrt{144+9} = 12.37\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \).

Answer: 29.55 m/s²; omitting Coriolis gives 12.37 m/s², 58% low. Sanity check: Coriolis supplies 24 of the 27 m/s² of transverse acceleration — 89% of it, and eight times the tangential term from the lever's own angular acceleration. An error of that size propagates directly into the block's inertia force and the slot's contact pressure, which is why quick-return and swinging-block mechanisms are the classic place to lose a bearing to an analysis that looked complete.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

Inertia forces in a machine are sized by

\( F = ma \) and \( M = I_G\alpha \): it is acceleration that inertia resists. A link can be moving fast and load nothing extra if it is moving at constant velocity in a straight line, and can be momentarily at rest — at a toggle, say — while carrying its largest inertia load. Jerk matters for smoothness and for cam wear, but it does not appear in a force equation.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

A machine's speed is raised from 1000 to 1200 rev/min. The inertia forces increase by about

Inertia forces go as \( \omega^2 \), so the factor is \( (1.2)^2 = 1.44 \) — a 44% increase for a 20% speed rise. This is why "just run it faster" is a structural and bearing question rather than a motor question: the drive may have the power to spare while the pins, the frame and the foundations do not.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

In \( a \approx -r\omega^2(\cos\theta + \cos 2\theta/n) \), the second term exists because

It is the finite rod ratio \( n = L/r \). An infinitely long rod would keep the piston in pure simple harmonic motion at crank frequency, and the secondary term would vanish. Its amplitude is \( 1/n \) of the primary — a quarter at \( n = 4 \) — and it oscillates at twice crank frequency, which is why it appears as a distinct engine vibration order in Lesson 10.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

For a slider-crank at constant \( \omega \), the piston acceleration is largest

At top dead centre both terms add: \( -r\omega^2(1+1/n) \), which for the worked case is \( -2500 \) m/s², against \( +1500 \) at bottom dead centre where they partly cancel, \( -r\omega^2(-1+1/n) \). The asymmetry between the two ends is entirely the secondary term, and it is why the piston-pin and rod loads at the top of the stroke govern the design.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

The Coriolis component is present whenever

\( 2\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}} \) needs both factors: a rotating frame and a sliding velocity within it. A plain pin joint has no relative sliding, so no Coriolis term; a slider on a fixed guide has no rotation, so none either. A block in a swinging slot has both, and there the term is often the largest of the four.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

An analysis of a swinging-block mechanism omits the Coriolis term and reports 12.4 m/s² where the true value is 29.5. The consequence is

Acceleration multiplies mass to give force, so a 58% low acceleration is a 58% low inertia force and a correspondingly low contact pressure in the slot — the opposite of conservative. The velocity analysis is unaffected: Coriolis appears only when the velocity result is differentiated, which is exactly why the mistake survives a review that checked the velocities and found them right.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A four-bar is driven at perfectly constant \( \omega_2 \). The angular acceleration of the coupler is

Constant input speed makes \( \alpha_2 = 0 \) and nothing else. Because the velocity ratio of a linkage changes continuously with position, \( \omega_3 \) and \( \omega_4 \) vary through the cycle and their derivatives are generally non-zero — 33.05 and 39.93 rad/s² for the worked linkage at \( \theta_2 = 60^\circ \). A steady motor does not give an inertia-free mechanism.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

The centripetal term \( \omega^2 r \) in an acceleration analysis always points

It points from the moving point back at the centre it is turning about, which is why it is fully known as soon as the velocity analysis is finished — both its magnitude \( \omega^2r \) and its direction come from results already in hand. The tangential term \( \alpha r \) is the perpendicular one, and it carries the unknown that the acceleration equations are solved for.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the compressor that failed its pins after an uprate. A reciprocating compressor was run 20% faster to recover throughput after a process change. The drive had the power, the valves were re-selected, and the machine met its new duty. Within a few months the wrist-pin bushes on two cylinders were found spread and the rod small-ends picked up.

Nothing was wrong with the parts. The inertia force on a reciprocating mass goes as \( \omega^2 \), so a 20% speed rise multiplies it by \( 1.2^2 = 1.44 \): every pin in the machine saw 44% more load, on every stroke, forever. Worked in numbers on a comparable geometry — 50 mm crank, 200 mm rod — the peak piston acceleration at top dead centre is \( r\omega^2(1+r/L) \), which at 200 rad/s is 2500 m/s², so a 0.5 kg reciprocating mass already pushes 1250 N through the pin, 255 times its own weight, before any gas load is added. Add 44% and the bearing is running outside the pressure it was selected for while every performance figure on the datasheet still reads correctly.

Two practices follow. A speed change is a re-analysis of the inertia loads, not a control setting: the \( \omega^2 \) law means the machine that is 20% faster is a machine with 44% more load in its running gear. And the inertia load reverses twice per revolution, so it is a fatigue duty — the consequence of getting it wrong is not an immediate failure that would have been caught during commissioning, but a life that has been quietly divided by a large number.
