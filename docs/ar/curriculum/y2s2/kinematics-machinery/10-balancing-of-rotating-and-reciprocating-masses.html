Balancing of rotating and reciprocating masses

Single- and multi-plane rotating balance done with vectors. Reciprocating unbalance and why it can't be fully balanced. The kinematic truth behind field balancing jobs.

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Why can rotating unbalance be fully balanced but reciprocating not?
  • Two-plane balancing: why two?

§1 · What unbalance is

A rotor is balanced when its mass is distributed so that rotation produces no net force or couple on the bearings. It almost never is, as machined: a keyway on one side, a core shift in a casting, a coating applied unevenly, a blade replaced with one a few grams heavier. Each leaves an effective mass \( m \) at an effective radius \( r \), and rotation turns that into a force \( m\omega^2 r \) rotating with the shaft — which the bearings feel as a load at once per revolution, forever.

The \( \omega^2 \) is what makes it a running problem rather than a manufacturing curiosity. A 5 g residual at 100 mm on a rotor at 3000 rev/min produces \( 0.005(314.2)^2(0.1) = 49\ \mathrm{N} \) — five kilograms of force from five grams of metal, cycling fifty times a second. Double the speed and it is 197 N.

§2 · One plane or two

If the whole unbalance lies in a single transverse plane — a thin disc, a fan wheel — a single correction mass in that plane cancels it. The condition is that the \( m r \) vectors sum to zero.

A long rotor is not that. Two equal unbalances at opposite ends and 180° apart sum to zero force and still shake the machine: they form a couple that rocks the rotor about its centre, loading one bearing up while the other goes down. Cancelling force alone is not enough, so two conditions must be satisfied at once, and satisfying two vector conditions needs two correction planes.

Key relations — two-plane balancing

\[ \sum m_ir_i=0 \quad\text{(force)},\qquad \sum m_ir_ia_i=0 \quad\text{(couple)} \]

\( a_i \) is the axial distance from a chosen reference plane. Solve the couple equation first — taking moments about one correction plane removes that plane's unknown — then use the force equation for the remaining one.

Work an example. A shaft carries 5 kg at 100 mm and 0°; 8 kg at 80 mm and 120°, 200 mm along; 6 kg at 120 mm and 240°, 400 mm along. Correction planes are at 0 and 600 mm. The \( mr \) products are 500, 640 and 720 kg·mm, and resolving gives \( \sum mr = (-180.0,\ -69.3) \), a residual of 192.9 kg·mm at 201.1°. Taking moments about the left plane and dividing by the 600 mm span gives the right-plane correction as 416.6 kg·mm at 33.7°; putting that back into the force equation gives the left-plane correction as 232.2 kg·mm at 224.1°. At a 100 mm correction radius those are 4.17 kg and 2.32 kg.

Notice that the single-plane answer — 192.9 kg·mm — matches neither correction. A rotor balanced in one plane when it needed two is not partly fixed; it has had its force cancelled and its couple left behind, and it will still shake.

§3 · Reciprocating masses, and the term that cannot be cancelled

Lesson 3 gave the piston acceleration as \( a=-r\omega^2(\cos\theta+\cos2\theta/n) \). Multiply by the reciprocating mass and the shaking force follows directly.

Key relation — single-cylinder shaking force

\[ F=m\omega^2r\cos\theta+\frac{m\omega^2r}{n}\cos2\theta \]

The first term is the primary, at crank frequency; the second is the secondary, at twice crank frequency, with \( 1/n \) of the amplitude.

For \( m=1.5 \) kg, \( r=50 \) mm, \( \omega=150 \) rad/s and \( n=4 \): primary 1687.5 N, secondary 421.9 N, and 2109.4 N combined at top dead centre against 1265.6 N at the bottom. The asymmetry between the two ends is the secondary term, and it exists only because the connecting rod is of finite length.

Here is the difficulty. A rotating unbalance is a rotating vector, and another rotating vector cancels it exactly. A reciprocating force is not a rotating vector — it oscillates along one line. A rotating balance mass can be made to cancel it in that direction, but the same mass then produces an equal force perpendicular to it, which was not there before. Nothing has been removed; the unbalance has been rotated by 90°.

So a single cylinder is partially balanced: cancel a fraction \( c \) of the primary and accept \( c \) of it appearing transversely. The residual peaks at \( \max(c,\,1-c)\,m\omega^2 r \), which is smallest at \( c=\tfrac12 \) — 843.8 N here, half the unbalanced primary, and the best a single cylinder can do. A locomotive practice of \( c=\tfrac23 \) gives 1125 N and reflects a different judgement: it favours the along-track direction at the expense of the vertical hammer-blow on the rail.

The secondary is worse still. No mass rotating at crank speed can produce a force at twice crank speed, so cancelling it requires shafts geared to run at \( 2\omega \), carrying their own weights. Some large engines carry exactly that; most accept the secondary and place it where the structure can take it.

§4 · Multi-cylinder engines and field balancing

What one cylinder cannot do, several can. Arrange cylinders along a crankshaft with chosen crank angles and the primary forces, primary couples, secondary forces and secondary couples can each be made to sum to zero — four conditions, satisfied by geometry rather than by added mass. An inline four with cranks at 0°, 180°, 180°, 0° cancels the primary forces and both couples, but its four secondaries all peak together and add: the residual is four times one cylinder's secondary, at twice crank speed. That is the characteristic four-cylinder vibration, and it is why balance shafts turning at \( 2\omega \) are fitted to larger fours.

Balancing in service reverses the calculation. The unbalance is unknown, so it is measured: run the rotor, record amplitude and phase at each bearing, add a known trial mass, run again, and the change in the vector response gives the influence coefficient linking correction to response. Two planes need a two-by-two influence matrix and two trial runs. The arithmetic is the same \( mr \) vector algebra worked above — the difference is only that the numbers are measured rather than given.

One reading matters diagnostically. Unbalance produces a once-per-revolution response, so a vibration spectrum with a dominant line at running speed points at balance; the gear-mesh lines of Lesson 6 sit far above it; and a strong twice-per-revolution line on a reciprocating machine is the secondary term of this lesson, not a balance fault at all.

الأسس

What this lesson assumes

  • Piston acceleration (Lesson 3) — the primary and secondary terms come directly from \( \cos\theta + \cos2\theta/n \).
  • Shaking force (Lesson 7) — what balancing exists to remove.
  • Vector addition and moments (Statics L2) — the two-plane conditions are a force polygon and a moment polygon.
  • Centripetal force (Dynamics L3) — \( m\omega^2r \), the whole rotating-unbalance mechanism.
  • Frequency in a spectrum (Lesson 6) — distinguishing a once-per-revolution balance line from a mesh line.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Two-plane balancing of a shaft

Problem. A shaft carries 5 kg at 100 mm radius and 0°, at axial station 0; 8 kg at 80 mm and 120°, at 200 mm; and 6 kg at 120 mm and 240°, at 400 mm. Correction planes are available at 0 and 600 mm. Find both correction \( mr \) products and their angles, and the masses required at a 100 mm radius.

Solution. (1) The \( mr \) products are 500, 640 and 720 kg·mm. Resolving:

\( \sum mr\cos = 500 + 640\cos120^\circ + 720\cos240^\circ = 500-320-360 = -180.0 \);
\( \sum mr\sin = 0 + 640\sin120^\circ + 720\sin240^\circ = 554.26-623.54 = -69.28 \) kg·mm.

(2) Moments about the left plane (station 0), so its correction drops out:

\( \sum mra\cos = 0(500) + 200(-320) + 400(-360) = -208\,000 \);
\( \sum mra\sin = 0 + 200(554.26) + 400(-623.54) = -138\,564 \) kg·mm².

(3) The right-plane correction at 600 mm must cancel those: \( (m_Rr_R)_x = 208\,000/600 = 346.67 \), \( (m_Rr_R)_y = 138\,564/600 = 230.94 \), so \( m_Rr_R = \sqrt{346.67^2+230.94^2} = 416.6 \) kg·mm at \( \arctan(230.94/346.67) = 33.67^\circ \).

(4) Force equation for the left plane: \( (m_Lr_L)_x = -(-180.0+346.67) = -166.67 \), \( (m_Lr_L)_y = -(-69.28+230.94) = -161.66 \), giving 232.2 kg·mm at 224.13°.

(5) At 100 mm radius: 4.17 kg in the right plane and 2.32 kg in the left.

Answer: 416.6 kg·mm at 33.67° (4.17 kg) and 232.2 kg·mm at 224.13° (2.32 kg). Sanity check: substituting both corrections back gives \( \sum mr = 0 \) and \( \sum mra = 0 \) to machine precision — both conditions, which is the test that the couple was not forgotten. Note that a single-plane treatment would have called for 192.9 kg·mm at 201.1°, which is neither of the correct answers: it cancels the force and leaves the couple, so the rotor would still rock its bearings.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Primary and secondary shaking force

Problem. A single cylinder has 1.5 kg of reciprocating mass, a 50 mm crank, a rod ratio \( n = 4 \), and runs at 150 rad/s. Find the primary and secondary force amplitudes and the total shaking force at top dead centre and at bottom dead centre.

Solution. (1) \( m\omega^2r = 1.5(150)^2(0.050) = 1.5(22\,500)(0.050) = 1687.5\ \mathrm{N} \) — the primary amplitude.

(2) Secondary amplitude \( = 1687.5/n = 1687.5/4 = 421.9\ \mathrm{N} \).

(3) At \( \theta = 0 \): \( \cos0 = 1 \) and \( \cos0 = 1 \), so \( F = 1687.5 + 421.9 = 2109.4\ \mathrm{N} \).

(4) At \( \theta = 180^\circ \): \( \cos180^\circ = -1 \) and \( \cos360^\circ = +1 \), so \( F = -1687.5 + 421.9 = -1265.6\ \mathrm{N} \).

Answer: 1687.5 N and 421.9 N; 2109.4 N at top dead centre and 1265.6 N at bottom. Sanity check: the two dead centres differ by 843.8 N, which is exactly twice the secondary amplitude — the primary reverses cleanly while the secondary returns to the same sign, since it goes through two full cycles per revolution. That asymmetry is a direct consequence of the connecting rod's finite length, and it is the reason the top of the stroke governs. An infinitely long rod would give \( \pm1687.5 \) N and nothing else.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Partial balance: choosing the fraction (with a US customary check)

Problem. For the cylinder above, a rotating balance mass cancels a fraction \( c \) of the primary force but introduces the same fraction transversely. Find the peak residual force for \( c = 0 \), \( c = \tfrac12 \) and \( c = \tfrac23 \), state which is best and why the other is used, and give the best value in pounds-force.

Solution. Along the cylinder axis the residual primary is \( (1-c)m\omega^2r \); perpendicular to it the introduced force is \( c\,m\omega^2 r \). Over a revolution the resultant traces an ellipse whose largest value is \( \max(c,\,1-c)\,m\omega^2r \).

(1) \( c=0 \): \( \max(0,1)(1687.5) = 1687.5\ \mathrm{N} \) — the whole primary, along the cylinder axis.

(2) \( c=\tfrac12 \): \( \max(0.5,0.5)(1687.5) = 843.8\ \mathrm{N} \).

(3) \( c=\tfrac23 \): \( \max(0.667,0.333)(1687.5) = 1125.0\ \mathrm{N} \), now transverse rather than axial.

(4) \( 843.8\ \mathrm{N} \div 4.448 = 189.7\ \mathrm{lbf} \).

Answer: 1687.5, 843.8 and 1125.0 N; \( c=\tfrac12 \) minimises the peak at 843.8 N (189.7 lbf), half the unbalanced primary. Sanity check: the minimum of \( \max(c,1-c) \) is at \( c=\tfrac12 \) by inspection, so the result needs no calculus and holds for any engine. The reason \( c=\tfrac23 \) survives in locomotive practice is that the two directions are not equally acceptable there: a vertical force lifts the wheel off the rail (hammer blow) while a longitudinal one merely surges the train, so the larger residual is deliberately placed where it does least harm. "Best" depends on which direction the structure can take.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

A rotating unbalance produces a bearing force that grows as

\( F = m\omega^2 r \). That square is why a residual unbalance which is harmless on a test stand becomes serious at running speed, and why balancing tolerances are quoted against a service speed rather than absolutely. A 5 g residual at 100 mm gives 49 N at 3000 rev/min and 197 N at 6000.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

Two equal unbalances at opposite ends of a rotor, 180° apart, give \( \sum mr = 0 \). The rotor

The forces cancel and the moments do not: the pair forms a couple that loads one bearing up as the other goes down, once per revolution. This is the standard demonstration that \( \sum mr = 0 \) is necessary and not sufficient, and the reason a long rotor needs two correction planes — two vector conditions require two planes to satisfy them.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

In a two-plane balance calculation, moments are taken about one correction plane so as to

A correction in the reference plane has zero moment arm about that plane, so it drops out and the moment equation contains only one unknown, which then solves directly. The force equation afterwards yields the second. Speed never appears — \( \omega^2 \) is common to every term and cancels — and the axial positions are essential, not avoidable.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

The secondary shaking force of a single cylinder acts at

It is the \( \cos2\theta \) term, so it completes two cycles per crankshaft revolution, with an amplitude \( 1/n \) of the primary — 421.9 N against 1687.5 N at \( n=4 \). It exists only because the connecting rod has finite length, and it is why a reciprocating machine shows a strong line at twice running speed that has nothing to do with a balance fault.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A rotating mass cannot fully balance a reciprocating mass because

A rotating unbalance is a rotating vector; a reciprocating force oscillates along one line. A rotating mass matched to cancel the axial component necessarily produces an equal transverse component that was not there before, so the unbalance is rotated rather than removed. That is why single-cylinder practice is partial balance, and why the residual cannot be driven below half the primary.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Balancing a fraction \( c \) of the primary force minimises the peak residual at

The peak resultant over a revolution is \( \max(c,1-c)m\omega^2r \), which is smallest where the two branches cross, at \( c=\tfrac12 \) — 843.8 N in the worked case, half the unbalanced primary. Locomotive practice used \( \tfrac23 \), giving a larger 1125 N residual placed deliberately in the direction the track could better tolerate; "best" depends on which direction the structure can accept.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

An inline four-cylinder engine with cranks at 0°, 180°, 180°, 0° has

The crank arrangement cancels the primary forces and both couples by geometry, but every cylinder's \( \cos2\theta \) term peaks at the same instant, so the four secondaries add to four times one cylinder's value at twice crank speed. That residual is the characteristic four-cylinder vibration and the reason larger fours are fitted with balance shafts geared to run at \( 2\omega \).

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Field balancing determines the required correction by

The unbalance is unknown in service, so the response to a known trial mass is measured and the influence coefficient — response per unit correction, in amplitude and phase — is deduced from the change. Two planes need two trial runs and a two-by-two matrix. The vector algebra is identical to the worked \( mr \) problem; only the source of the numbers differs.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the fan that was balanced twice and still shook. A large double-inlet fan was rebalanced after a blade repair. The technician worked in the plane he could reach, added a correction, and got the vibration at the drive-end bearing down to an acceptable figure. The non-drive-end bearing was worse afterwards than before, and within weeks its housing had cracked.

The unbalance was distributed along a wide rotor, so it had both a force and a couple component, and a single-plane correction can only address the force. Cancelling \( \sum mr \) while leaving \( \sum mra \) untouched leaves a pure couple, which rocks the rotor about its centre: one bearing improves and the other necessarily deteriorates. The worked example in this lesson shows the trap numerically — the single-plane answer, 192.9 kg·mm at 201.1°, is neither of the correct two-plane corrections of 416.6 at 33.7° and 232.2 at 224.1°, and applying it alone would have left a substantial couple behind.

Two practices follow. Whether a rotor needs one plane or two is decided by its geometry and its speed, not by which plane is accessible — a wide rotor is a two-plane job and a single-plane correction on one is not a partial fix but a different fault. And balancing is judged at every bearing, not the one being worked on: a correction that improves one measurement while worsening another is the signature of a couple, and it is telling the technician which calculation was skipped.
