KDM 252 — Kinematics & Dynamics of Machinery

KDM 252 · Lesson 01 · Mechanisms and mobility

Lesson 01Mechanisms and mobility

Key points

  • A link is a rigid body, a joint removes relative freedom, and a chain with one link fixed to earth is a mechanism.
  • Lower pairs (pins, sliders) contact over a surface and remove two freedoms; higher pairs (cam, gear tooth) contact along a line and remove one.
  • Kutzbach: \( M = 3(N-1) - 2j_1 - j_2 \). \( M=1 \) is a one-input mechanism, \( M=0 \) a structure, \( M<0 \) over-constrained.
  • The count ignores where the joints are, so special geometry — parallel or redundant links — can move while counting zero, and does so only while the tolerances hold.
  • Grashof, \( s+\ell \le p+q \), decides whether any link can be driven by a continuously rotating motor.
  • Which inversion you get depends on what is grounded: adjacent to the shortest gives a crank-rocker, the shortest itself gives a drag-link, opposite gives a double-rocker.
  • Inversions preserve relative motion between links and change everything measured against ground — one slider-crank chain gives an engine, a quick-return and a hand pump.
  • The transmission angle \( \mu \) grades a linkage: useful force goes as \( \sin\mu \), and \( \mu \) should stay above about 40° through the whole cycle.
  • The worked linkage is Grashof with \( M=1 \) and still poor: \( \mu \) falls to 29.0° once per revolution, fixed by shortening the crank at the cost of stroke.

KDM 252 · Lesson 01 · Mechanisms and mobility · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Free-body reasoning (Statics L2) — a two-force member carries load along its own line, which is why the transmission angle matters.
  • Vector geometry (Mathematics I) — the cosine rule, used here to find the transmission angle from link lengths alone.
  • Constraint counting (Statics L11) — the same subtract-what-is-removed argument that decides static determinacy decides mobility.

KDM 252 · Lesson 02 · Position and velocity analysis

Lesson 02Position and velocity analysis

Key points

  • Position analysis is one idea: walk round the closed chain as vectors and arrive back at the start, \( \sum r_ie^{i\theta_i}=0 \).
  • Solve it as geometry, not algebra — locate the crank pin, measure \( d \) to the far pivot, then two cosine rules finish the triangle.
  • Two roots exist, the open and crossed circuits; a built linkage lives in one and cannot change without being dismantled.
  • A cosine argument outside \( \pm1 \) is not a rounding error — it means the linkage cannot be assembled at that crank angle.
  • Differentiating the loop gives angular velocities linear in \( \omega_2 \): doubling the input doubles every velocity and changes no ratio.
  • A negative \( \omega_3 \) is a direction. The coupler reverses within one crank revolution as a matter of course.
  • A four-bar has \( 4(3)/2 = 6 \) instant centres; four are the pins, and Kennedy's collinearity theorem locates the other two.
  • \( \omega_4/\omega_2 = |O_2I_{24}|/|O_4I_{24}| \) — a velocity ratio read off a drawing, which reproduced the analytical 0.3525 exactly and shares no arithmetic with it.
  • The ratio varies through the cycle because \( I_{24} \) moves; here it runs from 0.0014 to 0.838.
  • Toggle positions — crank in line with coupler — set the rocker extremes (49.46° to 114.62°, a 65.17° swing) and are where the output velocity passes through zero.

KDM 252 · Lesson 03 · Acceleration analysis

Lesson 03Acceleration analysis

Key points

  • Acceleration, not velocity, sets inertia loads: \( F=ma \) at the centre of mass and \( M=I_G\alpha \) about it.
  • The scale is large — a 0.5 kg piston at 2500 m/s² pushes 1250 N through its pin, 255 times its own weight.
  • Inertia forces go as \( \omega^2 \): a 20% speed rise means 44% more load, and it reverses twice per revolution, so it is a fatigue duty.
  • Slider-crank exactly: \( a=-r\omega^2\left[\cos\theta+\frac{n^2\cos 2\theta+\sin^4\theta}{(n^2-\sin^2\theta)^{3/2}}\right] \), with \( n=L/r \).
  • Two-term working form \( a \approx -r\omega^2(\cos\theta+\cos 2\theta/n) \): exact at both dead centres, worst error 3.2% at \( 90^\circ \) where the acceleration is small.
  • The secondary term exists only because the rod is finite; its amplitude is \( 1/n \) of the primary and its frequency is twice crank speed.
  • Linkage acceleration is the velocity loop differentiated again: a centripetal \( \omega^2r \) that is already known, plus a tangential \( \alpha r \) that carries the unknown.
  • Constant input speed does not mean zero acceleration elsewhere — the worked four-bar gives \( \alpha_3=33.05 \) and \( \alpha_4=39.93 \) rad/s² at \( \alpha_2=0 \).
  • Coriolis \( 2\omega v_{\text{rel}} \) appears wherever sliding happens on a rotating member, perpendicular to the slot.
  • It is easily the dominant term: in the worked block it was 24 of 27 m/s² transverse, and omitting it gave an answer 58% low.

KDM 252 · Lesson 04 · Cams

Lesson 04Cams

Key points

  • A cam's profile is its displacement schedule made physical: complete freedom over the follower's motion, bought with a higher pair and its contact stress.
  • Dwells are usually why a cam is chosen — no linkage holds an output exactly still for a finite part of the cycle.
  • Every motion law delivers the same lift over the same rise; they differ only in the derivatives, and the derivatives break the machine.
  • Uniform motion has the lowest peak velocity, \( h\omega/\beta \), and infinite acceleration at both ends — unusable in pure form.
  • SHM: \( v_{\max}=\pi h\omega/2\beta \), \( a_{\max}=\pi^2h\omega^2/2\beta^2 \) — 0.628 m/s and 39.48 m/s² for the worked cam.
  • Cycloidal: \( v_{\max}=2h\omega/\beta \), \( a_{\max}=2\pi h\omega^2/\beta^2 \) — 0.800 m/s and 50.26 m/s², exactly \( 4/\pi \) times the harmonic values, always.
  • Cycloidal still wins at speed: harmonic acceleration steps at both ends of the rise, which is infinite jerk and rings the follower train.
  • Wear records the acceleration history — bands at the transitions indicate a discontinuity, even wear at mid-rise indicates an honest load.
  • Pressure angle \( \tan\phi = (ds/d\theta)/(R_p+s) \), kept below about 30°; the base circle is its control (29.7° at \( R_p=25 \) mm falls to 16.0° at 60 mm).
  • The spring holds the higher pair together: \( F \ge ma_{\max} \), 78 N with margin here, and the requirement grows as \( \omega^2 \) whenever the machine is sped up.

KDM 252 · Lesson 05 · Gear tooth geometry

Lesson 05Gear tooth geometry

Key points

  • The law of gearing: the contact normal must pass through a fixed pitch point on the line of centres. Profiles that satisfy it are conjugate.
  • The involute is conjugate to itself, so any two involute gears of the same module and pressure angle mesh — which is why gears are catalogue items.
  • Its contact normal stays on one fixed line of action tangent to both base circles, so the tooth-force direction never changes through the mesh.
  • \( m=d/N \), \( p=\pi m \), \( d_b=d\cos\phi \), \( C=m(N_1+N_2)/2 \); US customary uses \( P=N/d \) with \( m=25.4/P \).
  • Contact ratio \( m_c=Z/p_b \) must exceed 1; the worked 20/40 pair at module 4 gives \( Z=19.31 \) mm, \( p_b=11.81 \) mm and \( m_c=1.635 \).
  • The fractional part is the physics: two pairs share the load 63.5% of the time, one pair carries everything for the rest, and the stiffness step between them drives gear-mesh vibration.
  • Interference is a tip gouging the flank below the base circle, where no involute exists; avoid it with \( N_{\min}=2k/\sin^2\phi \) — 31.9 at 14.5°, 17.1 at 20°, 11.2 at 25°.
  • That curve is why 20° is standard; 25° buys smaller pinions at 28% more radial bearing load and less contact ratio.
  • Opening the centre distance changes the operating pressure angle, the contact ratio (1.635 to 1.395 for +1 mm) and the backlash (0.73 mm) — but never the speed ratio.
  • Backlash is required for lubricant and thermal growth: it is controlled, not eliminated.

KDM 252 · Lesson 06 · Gear trains

Lesson 06Gear trains

Key points

  • Train value \( e = \pm\prod N_{\text{driving}}/\prod N_{\text{driven}} \), one sign change per external mesh; idlers cancel and only reverse direction.
  • Compound trains multiply stage ratios: 51/17 then 76/19 gives 12:1, so 1750 rev/min in leaves at 145.83, with the intermediate shaft at 583.33.
  • Torque multiplies by the ratio less the losses: \( 12\times0.97^2 = 11.29 \), and the missing 6% is heat the casing must reject.
  • Fixed-centre arithmetic fails for epicyclics because the planet centres move; the fix is to ride the carrier.
  • Train equation \( e=(\omega_L-\omega_A)/(\omega_F-\omega_A) \), with \( e \) evaluated as if the carrier were fixed.
  • Sun 20, planets 20, ring 60 (satisfying \( N_r=N_s+2N_p \)): ring held and sun at 1500 gives a carrier at 375 rev/min — \( 1+N_r/N_s = 4 \), not 3.
  • The same set gives −500 rev/min at the ring with the carrier held, and 1:1 with any two members locked together.
  • Planets spin at −750 rev/min, twice carrier speed and backwards — the speed the planet bearings actually see.
  • Three planets share the torque, which is why an epicyclic set of a given rating is smaller; equal sharing needs a floating sun or ring.
  • Gear-mesh frequency \( f = Nn/60 \): 495.8 Hz and 184.7 Hz for the two stages, 375 Hz for the planetary confirmed by two independent routes.
  • \( \gcd(17,51)=17 \) means each pinion tooth meets only three gear teeth for life; 17/52 is a hunting pair and distributes wear, at a 1.9% change in ratio.

KDM 252 · Lesson 07 · Static and inertia force analysis

Lesson 07Static and inertia force analysis

Key points

  • d'Alembert converts a moving link into a statics problem: add an inertia force \( -m\mathbf{a}_G \) and an inertia couple \( -I_G\alpha \) to its free body.
  • The two combine into one equivalent offset inertia force, displaced by \( e = I_G\alpha/(ma_G) \) — 18.75 mm for the worked rod, and what splits the load between its two pins.
  • A coupler is a two-force member, so its force acts along its own line and its moment about the rocker pivot is \( Fr_4\sin\mu \).
  • The worked four-bar at \( \theta_2=60^\circ \) needs 767.4 N in the coupler and 17.63 N·m at the crank for 50 N·m at the rocker.
  • The power balance \( T_2\omega_2=T_4\omega_4 \) reproduces 17.63 N·m exactly, sharing no arithmetic with the force route — use it every time.
  • \( F=T_4/(r_4\sin\mu) \) makes the transmission angle a hardware cost: the same duty needs 1148 N at \( \mu=29.0^\circ \) and 561 N at \( 82.3^\circ \), a factor of 2.05.
  • That load cycles once per revolution — \( 854\pm294 \) N — so the pins are a fatigue and fretting problem, not a static bearing-pressure one.
  • Inertia usually dominates: a 0.5 kg piston at 2500 m/s² puts 1250 N through its pin, more than the entire static coupler force.
  • Solve link by link, carrying pin forces along the chain; what emerges at the frame is the shaking force, and at the crank the shaking torque.
  • Bearings are selected on the peak load and its direction, both of which move through the cycle; an average understates the duty and hides the reversal.

KDM 252 · Lesson 08 · Clutches and brakes

Lesson 08Clutches and brakes

Key points

  • Clutches and brakes are the same analysis — pressure distribution, friction, integration for torque. Only the destination of the energy differs.
  • Uniform pressure describes a new, flat, rigid pair; uniform wear (\( pr \) constant) describes the run-in device and puts the peak pressure at the inner radius.
  • \( T_{\text{wear}} = \mu F(D+d)/4 = 150.0 \) N·m against \( T_{\text{pressure}} = 153.1 \) N·m for the worked clutch — wear is lower and is the design value.
  • Peak pressure differs far more than torque does: 0.212 MPa under wear against a uniform 0.159 MPa, 33% higher on the lining.
  • At a limiting pressure, torque is maximised at \( d = D/\sqrt3 = 0.577D \) — 144 mm for a 250 mm clutch.
  • Band brake: \( T_1/T_2 = e^{\mu\theta} \), so 500 N on the slack side gives 2056 N tight and 311 N·m of torque at 270° wrap.
  • Wrap angle enters exponentially and radius linearly: going to 360° adds 80% of torque where the same proportional radius increase adds 33%.
  • A shoe is self-energizing when its friction moment aids the actuation, and self-locking when it equals it — and \( \mu \) rises when cold, wet or glazed, so the margin is a safety item.
  • \( E = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 \) per engagement: 6169 J here, a harmless 2.7 K in a 5 kg disc, but 1028 W continuous at ten engagements a minute.
  • Thermal duty, not torque, is usually the real limit; fade is the loop of heat lowering \( \mu \), which lengthens the slip, which adds heat.

KDM 252 · Lesson 09 · Flywheels and speed fluctuation

Lesson 09Flywheels and speed fluctuation

Key points

  • A turning-moment diagram plots torque against crank angle; its area is work and its mean height is the motor duty.
  • Punching a 20 mm hole in 10 mm of 350 MPa plate takes \( \pi dt\tau = 219.9 \) kN peak and about \( \tfrac12 Ft = 1100 \) J of work.
  • At 30 strokes/min that is 550 W mean, while the demand during the punch is 1167 N·m — 3.67 kW at the crank, 6.7 times the mean.
  • \( \Delta E \) is the area between the curve and the mean line, here 935 J, equal to \( W(1-0.15) \) — two routes, same number.
  • \( C_s=(\omega_{\max}-\omega_{\min})/\omega_{\text{mean}} \) is a specification: 0.2 for a crusher, 0.02 for machine tools, 0.003 for alternators.
  • \( I = \Delta E/(C_s\omega^2) = 32.9\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \) on the 360 rev/min motor-side shaft — about 91 kg of rim at 0.6 m radius, against 4735 kg·m² on the 30 rev/min crank.
  • Tightening \( C_s \) from 0.02 to 0.005 quadruples the inertia to 131.5 kg·m²; inertia buys smoothness linearly.
  • Doubling the shaft speed quarters the inertia to 8.22 kg·m² — fit the wheel to the fastest shaft carrying the fluctuation.
  • A flywheel governs fluctuation within a cycle; a governor sets the mean speed. They are different problems and different devices.
  • Oversizing costs start-up time, starting current, rim stress (\( \sigma\propto\rho v^2 \)) and gyroscopic couple — specify the loosest \( C_s \) the process allows.

KDM 252 · Lesson 10 · Balancing of rotating and reciprocating masses

Lesson 10Balancing of rotating and reciprocating masses

Key points

  • Unbalance is an effective \( mr \) that rotation turns into \( m\omega^2r \) — a once-per-revolution bearing load whose size goes as the square of speed.
  • Single-plane balance requires \( \sum m_ir_i=0 \); that is necessary and not sufficient for a long rotor.
  • Two equal unbalances 180° apart at opposite ends sum to zero force and leave a couple that rocks the bearings — hence two conditions and two correction planes.
  • \( \sum m_ir_ia_i=0 \) is solved first, about one correction plane, which removes that plane's unknown.
  • The worked shaft needs 416.6 kg·mm at 33.67° and 232.2 kg·mm at 224.13° — 4.17 kg and 2.32 kg at 100 mm radius.
  • Its single-plane answer, 192.9 kg·mm at 201.1°, is neither correction: a one-plane fix on a two-plane rotor cancels force and leaves couple.
  • Reciprocating force is \( m\omega^2r\cos\theta + (m\omega^2r/n)\cos2\theta \) — 1687.5 N primary and 421.9 N secondary here, 2109.4 N at top dead centre against 1265.6 N at bottom.
  • A rotating mass rotates a reciprocating unbalance rather than removing it: cancelling \( c \) of the primary introduces \( c \) transversely.
  • The peak residual is \( \max(c,1-c)m\omega^2r \), minimised at \( c=\tfrac12 \) — 843.8 N, half the primary; locomotive practice used \( \tfrac23 \) to steer the residual into a tolerable direction.
  • An inline four cancels primaries and couples by crank geometry and adds four secondaries at twice crank speed — the reason balance shafts run at \( 2\omega \).
  • Field balancing measures amplitude and phase, adds a trial mass and re-measures; the influence coefficient does the rest, on the same vector algebra.

KDM 252 · Lesson 11 · Governors and gyroscopic effects

Lesson 11Governors and gyroscopic effects

Key points

  • A flywheel limits fluctuation within a cycle; a governor sets the mean speed. They are different problems and neither substitutes for the other.
  • A governor is a mechanical feedback loop: the balls' equilibrium is the measurement and the sleeve position is the control action.
  • Watt governor: \( h=g/\omega^2=895/N^2 \) metres. The ball mass cancels completely, so heavier balls change nothing.
  • Height falls as the inverse square of speed — 248.5 mm at 60 rev/min, 62.1 at 120, 22.4 at 200 — and below about 20 mm linkage friction swamps the signal.
  • Porter governor: \( h=\frac{m+M}{m}\cdot\frac{g}{\omega^2} \). A 2 kg ball with a 20 kg sleeve gives a factor of 11 (not 10), restoring 246 mm at 200 rev/min.
  • The dead weight is paid for in response speed, which is why high-speed governors use springs for the same multiplying effect with less moving mass.
  • Sensitiveness is mean speed over speed range; stability requires one radius per speed.
  • Isochronism — full sleeve travel at a single speed — sounds ideal and causes hunting, because there is no unique equilibrium to settle at. Cure with deliberate insensitivity and damping.
  • Gyroscopic couple \( C=I\omega\omega_p \), acting about the axis perpendicular to both spin and precession: a rotor yawed by a turn reacts in pitch.
  • A 25 kg·m² rotor at 3000 rev/min precessed at 0.1 rad/s gives 785 N·m, which is \( \pm393 \) N on bearings 2 m apart — a reversing load that appears only while the machine turns.
  • It scales with \( I \), so the flywheel of Lesson 9 is not free: smoothing a cycle buys gyroscopic couple in every manoeuvre.
