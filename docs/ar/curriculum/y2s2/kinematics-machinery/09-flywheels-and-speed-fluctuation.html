Flywheels and speed fluctuation

Turning-moment diagrams and coefficient of fluctuation. Flywheel sizing from energy variation. Presses and compressors as the plants' flywheel machines.

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Read a turning-moment diagram: what does the shaded area mean?
  • What does the coefficient of fluctuation specify?

§1 · The turning-moment diagram

Machines rarely demand a steady torque. A press punches for a fraction of its cycle and coasts for the rest; an engine delivers torque on one stroke in four; a compressor loads on compression and unloads on suction. Plot the torque required — or delivered — against crank angle and the result is a turning-moment diagram, and everything about speed fluctuation is read from it.

Two readings matter. The area under the curve over a complete cycle is the work per cycle, since \( W=\int T\,d\theta \). Divided by the cycle angle it gives the mean torque, which is what the motor must supply on average, and the horizontal line at that height is the line every flywheel calculation is measured from.

Work the press in the figure. Punching a 20 mm hole in 10 mm plate of 350 MPa shear strength needs \( F=\pi d t\tau = \pi(0.020)(0.010)(350\times10^6) = 219.9\ \mathrm{kN} \) at its peak, and since the force builds and falls as the slug shears, the work is about \( \tfrac12 F t = \tfrac12(219\,911)(0.010) = 1100\ \mathrm{J} \) per hole. At 30 strokes per minute the mean power is only \( 1100(30)/60 = 550\ \mathrm{W} \).

§2 · Where the fluctuation comes from

Suppose the punch occupies 15% of the crank cycle. Spread over that arc the resisting torque is \( 1100/(0.15\times2\pi) = 1167\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \), while the mean over the whole revolution is \( 1100/2\pi = 175\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \). During the punch the machine demands nearly seven times the mean; for the other 85% it demands nothing.

The area between the curve and the mean line is the energy that has to come from somewhere other than the motor:

\[ \Delta E=(1167-175)(0.15\times2\pi)=935\ \mathrm{J} \]

which is also \( 1100\times(1-0.15) \), as it must be. If nothing stores that energy, the motor must be able to deliver 1167 N·m at the crank — \( 1167\times2\pi(30)/60 = 3.67\ \mathrm{kW} \) — against a mean demand of 550 W. The whole economics of intermittent machinery lives in that factor of 6.7.

Read the sign convention carefully: where the curve lies above the mean line the machine takes more than the motor supplies and the shaft slows; where it lies below, the motor runs ahead and the shaft speeds up. The largest accumulated area between two crossings of the mean line is the energy the flywheel must hold, and finding it on a real diagram means adding the areas between successive crossings, not just taking the biggest single lobe.

§3 · Sizing the flywheel

A flywheel absorbs energy by speeding up and returns it by slowing down. How much speed variation is acceptable is a design decision, expressed as the coefficient of fluctuation of speed.

Key relations — flywheel sizing

\[ C_s=\frac{\omega_{\max}-\omega_{\min}}{\omega_{\text{mean}}},\qquad \Delta E=I\,\omega_{\text{mean}}^2\,C_s \]

\( C_s \) is chosen by application: about 0.2 for a crusher or punch where speed hardly matters, 0.02 for machine tools, and 0.003 or less for alternators, where speed is the product.

The relation follows from \( \Delta E = \tfrac12 I(\omega_{\max}^2-\omega_{\min}^2) = \tfrac12 I(\omega_{\max}+\omega_{\min})(\omega_{\max}-\omega_{\min}) \), with the first bracket approximated as \( 2\omega_{\text{mean}} \).

The press crank turns at 30 rev/min, far too slow to store energy in anything of a sensible size — at \( \omega = 3.14 \) rad/s the required inertia would be \( 935/(0.02\times9.87) = 4735\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \). The flywheel therefore goes on the motor side of a 12:1 reduction, turning at 360 rev/min (\( \omega = 37.70\ \mathrm{rad/s} \)), where with \( C_s = 0.02 \):

\[ I=\frac{\Delta E}{C_s\omega^2}=\frac{935}{0.02(37.70)^2} =32.9\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \]

A rim at 0.6 m radius carrying most of that needs about \( 32.9/0.6^2 = 91\ \mathrm{kg} \). The speed swings between 38.08 and 37.32 rad/s — a range of 2% of the mean, which is what \( C_s = 0.02 \) means.

Two sensitivities decide flywheel design, and both are strong. \( I \) is inversely proportional to \( C_s \), so demanding 0.005 instead of 0.02 multiplies the inertia by four, to 131.5 kg·m². And \( I \) is inversely proportional to \( \omega^2 \), so putting the flywheel on a shaft running twice as fast cuts the required inertia to a quarter. That is why flywheels are fitted to the fastest shaft that carries the fluctuation, usually the motor side of the reduction, and why a small high-speed wheel routinely beats a large slow one.

§4 · What a flywheel does not do

A flywheel controls fluctuation within a cycle. It has nothing to say about the mean speed — that is set by the balance between the motor's characteristic and the load, and holding it is a governor's job, which is Lesson 11. The two devices are routinely confused and they solve different problems: a flywheel with no governor holds a smooth speed at whatever value the load happens to dictate; a governor with no flywheel holds the right average while the shaft surges within each revolution.

Nor is a bigger flywheel free. Its own inertia has to be accelerated on every start-up, so start times and starting currents rise with it; its rim stress goes as \( \rho v^2 \), which sets a burst speed that must not be approached; and it adds a large gyroscopic couple to any machine that changes orientation, which Lesson 11 also covers. Sizing to the loosest \( C_s \) the process will tolerate is therefore the correct instinct, not a shortcut.

الأسس

What this lesson assumes

  • Work as an integral of torque (Dynamics L8) — \( W=\int T\,d\theta \), which is why area on a turning-moment diagram is energy.
  • Rotational kinetic energy (Dynamics L8) — \( \tfrac12 I\omega^2 \), the store a flywheel draws on.
  • Mass moment of inertia (Dynamics L9) — \( I = mk^2 \), and why rim mass counts for so much more than hub mass.
  • Shear strength in punching (Manufacturing Processes I L6) — the source of the punch force and hence of the work per stroke.
  • Shaking torque (Lesson 7) — the fluctuating crank torque this lesson smooths.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Punch work and the mean-power argument

Problem. A press punches a 20 mm hole in 10 mm plate of ultimate shear strength 350 MPa, at 30 strokes per minute. Find the peak punch force, the work per stroke, and the mean power. If the punch occupies 15% of the crank cycle, find the peak and mean torques and hence the power a motor would need with no flywheel fitted, at a shaft speed of 360 rev/min.

Solution. (1) The sheared area is the hole circumference times the plate thickness: \( A = \pi d t = \pi(0.020)(0.010) = 6.283\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2} \), so \( F = A\tau = 6.283\times10^{-4}(350\times10^6) = 219\,911\ \mathrm{N} \).

(2) The force rises to that peak and falls as the slug shears, so the work is about \( \tfrac12 F t = \tfrac12(219\,911)(0.010) = 1100\ \mathrm{J} \).

(3) Mean power \( = 1100(30)/60 = 550\ \mathrm{W} \).

(4) Mean torque \( = W/2\pi = 1100/6.2832 = 175.0\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \); punch torque \( = W/(0.15\times2\pi) = 1100/0.9425 = 1166.7\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \).

(5) The crank turns at 30 rev/min, so \( \omega = 2\pi(30)/60 = 3.1416\ \mathrm{rad/s} \) and a motor meeting the peak directly needs \( 1166.7(3.1416) = 3665\ \mathrm{W} = 3.67\ \mathrm{kW} \).

Answer: 219.9 kN, 1100 J, 550 W mean; 175.0 and 1166.7 N·m; 3.67 kW without a flywheel. Sanity check: \( 3665/550 = 6.67 \), exactly \( T_{\text{punch}}/T_{\text{mean}} = 1166.7/175.0 \), as it must be since both powers are taken at the same shaft speed — so the flywheel's job is to let the motor be sized for the smaller of the two. Note also that the sheared area is the hole circumference times the thickness: using \( \pi d^2/4 \) would have given 110 kN, 2.5 times too small, and it is the commonest error in this calculation.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Sizing the flywheel

Problem. For the press above, find the energy fluctuation \( \Delta E \), then the flywheel inertia needed for \( C_s = 0.02 \) with the flywheel on the motor-side shaft at 360 rev/min, the rim mass if the rim sits at 0.6 m radius, and the resulting maximum and minimum angular speeds. Compare with fitting it to the 30 rev/min crank shaft instead.

Solution. (1) The flywheel supplies whatever the punch demands above the mean: \( \Delta E = (1166.7-175.0)(0.15\times2\pi) = 991.7(0.9425) = 934.6\ \mathrm{J} \). Equivalently \( \Delta E = W(1-0.15) = 1100(0.85) = 935\ \mathrm{J} \).

(2) \( \omega = 37.699\ \mathrm{rad/s} \), so \( I = \dfrac{\Delta E}{C_s\omega^2} = \dfrac{934.6}{0.02(1421.2)} = \dfrac{934.6}{28.42} = 32.88\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \).

(3) Treating the rim as carrying it all, \( m = I/r^2 = 32.88/0.36 = 91.3\ \mathrm{kg} \).

(4) \( \omega_{\max} = \omega(1+C_s/2) = 38.08 \) and \( \omega_{\min} = \omega(1-C_s/2) = 37.32\ \mathrm{rad/s} \).

(5) On the crank shaft, \( \omega = 3.1416\ \mathrm{rad/s} \) and \( I = 934.6/(0.02\times9.870) = 4735\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \) — 144 times as much for the same duty, which is \( 12^2 \).

Answer: \( \Delta E = 935 \) J, \( I = 32.9\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \), rim about 91 kg, speed between 37.32 and 38.08 rad/s; 4735 kg·m² if fitted to the crank. Sanity check: \( (\omega_{\max}-\omega_{\min})/\omega = 0.756/37.70 = 0.0200 \) ✓ — the specification recovered from the answer. The two routes to \( \Delta E \) also agree, and the second is worth remembering: the flywheel supplies the whole punch work less the fraction the motor contributes during the punch itself.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

What tightening \( C_s \) and changing shaft speed cost

Problem. Using \( \Delta E = 934.6 \) J, find the flywheel inertia needed if \( C_s \) is tightened from 0.02 to 0.005 at the same 360 rev/min, and the inertia needed at \( C_s = 0.02 \) if the flywheel is moved to a shaft running at 720 rev/min. State the design rule that follows.

Solution. (1) Tightening \( C_s \): \( I = \dfrac{934.6}{0.005(37.699)^2} = \dfrac{934.6}{7.106} = 131.5\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \) — exactly four times the original 32.88, since \( I \propto 1/C_s \) and \( C_s \) was quartered.

(2) Doubling the speed: \( \omega = 75.398\ \mathrm{rad/s} \), so \( I = \dfrac{934.6}{0.02(5684.9)} = 8.22\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \) — a quarter of the original, since \( I\propto1/\omega^2 \).

(3) Rule: fit the flywheel to the fastest shaft that carries the fluctuation, and specify the loosest \( C_s \) the process will accept.

Answer: 131.5 kg·m² at \( C_s=0.005 \); 8.22 kg·m² at 720 rev/min; both follow from the inverse dependences in \( I=\Delta E/(C_s\omega^2) \). Sanity check: the two effects together span a factor of sixteen in inertia for the same job, which is why flywheel decisions are made before the layout is fixed rather than after. The speed rule has a limit the equation does not show: rim stress goes as \( \rho v^2 \), so a flywheel on a fast shaft approaches its burst speed sooner, and the gyroscopic couple it contributes (Lesson 11) grows with \( \omega \) as well.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

On a turning-moment diagram, the area between the curve and the mean-torque line is

\( W=\int T\,d\theta \), so an area on a torque-against-angle plot is an energy. Above the mean line the machine is demanding more than the motor supplies and the deficit comes from the flywheel; below it, the motor runs ahead and the flywheel takes the surplus back. The largest accumulated area between two crossings of the mean line is \( \Delta E \).

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

The coefficient of fluctuation of speed \( C_s \) is defined as

It is the speed range as a fraction of the mean, and it is a specification chosen from the application rather than a property of the machine. The relation \( \Delta E = I\omega^2C_s \) then follows from the kinetic-energy difference, with \( \omega_{\max}+\omega_{\min} \) approximated as \( 2\omega_{\text{mean}} \) — an approximation good to a fraction of a per cent at any realistic \( C_s \).

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A flywheel is sized for \( C_s = 0.02 \). Tightening the requirement to \( C_s = 0.005 \) changes the required inertia by a factor of

\( I = \Delta E/(C_s\omega^2) \), so \( I \propto 1/C_s \): quartering \( C_s \) quadruples \( I \), from 32.9 to 131.5 kg·m² in the worked press. Smoothness is bought linearly in inertia, and inertia costs mass, start-up time and rim stress — which is why the loosest acceptable \( C_s \) is the right specification.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Moving a flywheel from a shaft at 360 rev/min to one at 720 rev/min, for the same \( \Delta E \) and \( C_s \), changes the required inertia by a factor of

\( I \propto 1/\omega^2 \), so doubling the speed quarters the inertia — 32.9 kg·m² becomes 8.22. This is the single most effective flywheel decision available, and it is why the wheel goes on the fastest shaft that carries the fluctuation. The limits are rim stress, which goes as \( \rho v^2 \), and the gyroscopic couple the faster wheel contributes.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

The distinction between a flywheel and a governor is that

A flywheel is an energy store that limits the swing within one revolution and has no view about what the average speed should be. A governor adjusts the energy supplied to hold the mean where it is wanted and cannot respond within a cycle. A machine with a flywheel and no governor runs smoothly at whatever speed the load dictates; one with a governor and no flywheel holds the right average while surging within each revolution.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Punching a 20 mm hole in 10 mm plate of 350 MPa shear strength requires a peak force of about

\( F = \pi d t\tau = \pi(0.020)(0.010)(350\times10^6) = 219.9 \) kN. The sheared area is the circumference times the thickness, not the hole's cross-sectional area — using \( \pi d^2/4 \) instead is the standard error and gives a figure 2.5 times too small here. The work per stroke is roughly half the peak force times the thickness, because the force builds and falls as the slug shears.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A press absorbs 1100 J per stroke in 15% of its cycle. The energy the flywheel must handle is

The motor contributes its mean torque throughout, including during the punch, so the flywheel supplies the rest: \( \Delta E = W(1-0.15) = 935 \) J. Computed the other way, \( (1166.7-175.0)(0.15\times2\pi) = 934.6 \) J — the same number from the area between the pulse and the mean line, which is the check worth doing.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

An unnecessarily large flywheel penalises a machine mainly through

Its own inertia must be accelerated at every start, so start times and starting currents rise with it; its rim stress goes as \( \rho v^2 \) toward a burst speed; and it adds a gyroscopic couple to any machine that changes orientation. Mean power is set by the work per cycle and the rate, and is unaffected — the flywheel neither adds nor removes energy over a complete cycle.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the press that tripped its motor after a die change. A punching press ran for years on a modest motor. A new die was fitted to punch a larger hole in thicker plate, and the press began tripping its motor overload on some strokes and stalling on others. The motor was tested, found sound, and replaced with a larger one, which cured the tripping and introduced a new problem: the drive belts began to fail.

The flywheel had not been reconsidered. Punch work scales as \( \tfrac12(\pi dt\tau)t \) — with the hole diameter and, more strongly, the plate thickness, since thickness enters twice. The original duty of about 1100 J per stroke, with a mean torque of 175 N·m and an energy fluctuation of 935 J, had been matched to a flywheel of roughly 33 kg·m² on the 360 rev/min motor-side shaft for \( C_s = 0.02 \). A larger hole in thicker plate raised \( \Delta E \) proportionally, and since \( C_s = \Delta E/(I\omega^2) \) with \( I \) unchanged, the speed fluctuation rose in step. The shaft was being dragged down far enough during each punch for the motor to see the load as a stall. Fitting a bigger motor treated the symptom: the motor then supplied the peak directly, through the belts, which is exactly the path the flywheel had been installed to avoid, at nearly seven times the mean torque.

Two practices follow. A die change is a change to the turning-moment diagram, so the flywheel calculation is part of the tooling change and not part of the motor's file. And a motor that trips on an intermittent-duty machine is evidence about the energy store before it is evidence about the motor: the useful first check is \( \Delta E \) against \( I\omega^2C_s \), which takes one line.
