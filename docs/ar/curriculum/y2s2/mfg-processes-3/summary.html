MFG 254 · Lesson 01 · Sheet-metal cutting operations

Lesson 01Sheet-metal cutting operations

Key points

  • Sheet cutting separates by fracture, not by chip removal: shearing, blanking and piercing share the mechanics and differ only in which side is scrap.
  • A cut edge carries four zones in order — rollover, burnish, fracture, burr — and is a readable record of the clearance that made it.
  • \( c=A_ct \), with \( A_c \) about 0.045 for soft aluminium, 0.060 for low-carbon steel and 0.075 for hard alloys; on 3 mm steel that is 0.18 mm per side.
  • Too little clearance double-shears and raises force; too much drags, rolls over and burrs.
  • The product side carries the size: blanking die \( =D \) and punch \( =D-2c \); piercing punch \( =D \) and die \( =D+2c \).
  • \( F=StL \) with \( S\approx0.7\,\mathrm{TS} \) — a \( \varnothing60 \) blank in 3 mm steel needs 134.6 kN, plus about 5% stripping, so 141.3 kN or 15.9 US tons.
  • Force scales with the cut perimeter, so a long thin feature can cost more tonnage than a large round one.
  • Angular shear spreads the same work over a longer stroke: \( F_{\rm peak}\approx F\,p_ft/(p_ft+s) \), so 1.5 mm of shear halves 134.6 kN to 67.3 kN. It goes on the die for blanking and the punch for piercing.
  • Staggered rows sit \( s=\sqrt{(D+b)^2-(p/2)^2}=54.56 \) mm apart because they are also offset half a pitch along the feed.
  • That single layout decision takes utilisation from 68.0% to 74.5% — 8.7% less coil, 8.5 g per blank, 849 kg per 100,000 — for one afternoon's work at the drawing stage.

MFG 254 · Lesson 02 · Bending and springback

Lesson 02Bending and springback

Key points

  • The flat blank carries the arc length of the neutral axis, not the sum of the legs: \( \mathrm{BA}=2\pi\frac{\alpha}{360}(R+Kt) \).
  • The neutral axis migrates inward on tight bends: \( K\approx0.33 \) for \( R<2t \) and \( 0.50 \) for \( R\ge2t \). Using the wrong one puts a two-bend part 1.6 mm out while every angle and leg stays correct.
  • Bend deduction \( =2(R+t)\tan(\alpha/2)-\mathrm{BA} \); a 50/80/50 U-channel at 90°, R6, t3 develops to 167.56 mm.
  • Springback is elastic recovery: \( R_i/R_f=4x^3-3x+1 \) with \( x=(R_i/t)(Y/E) \) — the material enters only as \( Y/E \).
  • At \( R/t=10 \) a 90° bend recovers 3.3° in mild steel, 9.1° in high-strength steel and 10.7° in 6061-T6 aluminium, which ranks differently from yield strength alone.
  • Springback grows with \( R/t \) too, which is why tight bottomed bends hold their angle and wide air bends do not.
  • Four compensations: overbend (93.4° for 90° in mild steel), bottoming, coining and stretch forming — plus adaptive brakes, the only approach that copes with coil-to-coil variation.
  • \( F=K_{bf}\mathrm{TS}\,wt^2/D \) gives 169.6 kN per metre at t3 in a 24 mm die — 19.1 US tons per metre, 5.8 per foot.
  • Doubling the thickness quadruples the force at a fixed die opening and only doubles it when the opening is scaled to \( 8t \); tonnage charts are published against die opening for that reason.
  • \( R_{\min}/t=50/r-1 \) from the tensile reduction of area, and a bend line along the rolling direction needs 1.5 to 2 times more radius — a free fix if the layout is decided before the tool is cut.

MFG 254 · Lesson 03 · Deep drawing and stretch forming

Lesson 03Deep drawing and stretch forming

Key points

  • The flange is in circumferential compression and the wall in tension, so wrinkling and tearing are the two failures and the blankholder is the single control that trades between them.
  • Feasibility in one hit: \( \mathrm{DR}=D_b/D_p\le2 \), \( r\le0.5 \), \( t/D_b\ge0.01 \) — a 150 blank to an 80 cup gives 1.875, 0.467 and 0.0133, all passing narrowly.
  • \( \mathrm{LDR}=e^{\eta} \): the ideal \( e=2.72 \) reduced by friction and bending losses to 2.01 at \( \eta=0.7 \), or 2.23 at 0.8.
  • Normal anisotropy \( \bar R \) raises the LDR from about 2.0 to 2.4, because a sheet that resists thinning keeps a stronger wall.
  • Cup height from area constancy alone: \( h=(D_b^2-D_p^2)/4D_p \) — 50.3, 88.5 and 123.4 mm at \( \varnothing \)80, 55 and 42.
  • \( F=\pi D_pt\,\mathrm{TS}(\mathrm{DR}-0.7)=200.8 \) kN and \( F_h=0.015Y\pi[D_b^2-(D_p+2.2t+2R_d)^2]=146.3 \) kN; the press must supply 347 kN, 39.0 US tons.
  • Redraw ratios multiply: 1.875 × 1.455 × 1.310 reaches an overall 3.57 that no single draw could, with gentler stages because each starts from work-hardened material.
  • Ironing thins the wall at constant diameter, \( h'=h\,t/t' \), taking a 50.3 mm wall to 83.9 mm at 1.2 mm while the un-ironed base stays 2 mm.
  • \( p_{\min}=c[(\beta-1)^3+0.5D_p/100t]\mathrm{TS} \) — 0.33, 0.89 and 1.97 MPa at \( \beta= \) 1.5, 1.875 and 2.2. The cube is why the window closes.
  • Die radius \( 4 \) to \( 10t \) and punch nose radius above \( 4t \); too small tears and too large wrinkles, the same trade in another variable.

MFG 254 · Lesson 04 · Roll forming and high-volume sheet lines

Lesson 04Roll forming and high-volume sheet lines

Key points

  • Roll forming makes every bend at once on a strip that never stops, so profile complexity costs stands and capital rather than cycle time.
  • The crossover is computable: \( N^{*}=\Delta C/\Delta c \), here \( 56{,}000/0.783=71{,}489 \) parts for a six-bend 3 m profile.
  • Adding bends lowers the crossover, because they cost time on a brake and only capital on a line.
  • The flower pattern superimposes the section at every stand and encodes the angle schedule (15–30° a stand in mild steel, 3–8° in high-strength), the material path and the driven surfaces.
  • A rising leg forces longitudinal strain on its edge: \( \varepsilon=\sqrt{1+(h/L)^2}-1\approx h^2/2L^2 \).
  • A 40 mm leg over one 400 mm pitch gives 0.499% against a 0.121% yield strain — four times over, so the edge lengthens permanently and buckles into edge wave.
  • Keeping it elastic needs \( L\ge814 \) mm, three stands at a 300 mm pitch; the strain goes as \( h^2 \), so an 80 mm leg would need four times the length.
  • Levelling erases coil set only if \( t/2R \) reaches several times yield: rolls of 248 mm diameter or less for 1.5 mm steel, scaling linearly with thickness.
  • Slitting brings burr direction and camber; camber becomes bow in the finished profile and levelling does not fix it.
  • Sections hold \( \pm0.5 \) mm and \( \pm1^{\circ} \) but bow, camber and twist are hard; end flare is intrinsic; and the process cannot make a section that changes along its length.

  • Break-even and fixed-versus-variable cost (Manufacturing Processes I L11) — the same crossover, applied to a process choice.

MFG 254 · Lesson 05 · Presses, dies, and tooling

Lesson 05Presses, dies, and tooling

Key points

  • A mechanical press converts flywheel torque through a crank, so \( F=T/(R\sin\theta) \) and its rating is only meaningful with a stated distance above bottom dead centre.
  • On a 100-tonne press with a 100 mm stroke rated at 6 mm: 73% of rating at 12 mm, 55% at 25 mm, 47% at mid-stroke.
  • Blanking finishes at the bottom and gets the full rating; drawing needs force high in the stroke and gets about half, which is why drawing belongs on a hydraulic press.
  • Hydraulic presses give full force anywhere with adjustable speed and dwell, at 10–30 strokes a minute against a mechanical press's 60–150.
  • A die set must hold register far tighter than the clearance: \( e\le0.036 \) mm at \( c=0.18 \), and even then the worst case is 1.5:1 side to side.
  • A progressive die yields one part per stroke once full — 100 a minute from eight stations after 4.8 seconds — at the cost of a carrier web; a transfer die recovers the material and adds a mechanism.
  • Presses break three ways: overloaded away from BDC, off-centre loading tipping the slide, and snap-through.
  • Snap-through releases \( E=F^2/2k \) — 250 J at 1,000 kN on a 2,000 kN/mm frame — as 10–15% reverse tonnage; angular shear, staggered punches and slower speeds soften it.
  • Burr height is the wear gauge; a punch with 12 mm of grindable length at 0.3 mm a sharpen has 40 sharpens, roughly eight million hits.
  • The sharpening interval is a computable optimum, \( N^{*}=\sqrt{2C_m/\lambda C_c}=194{,}000 \) hits, where the two cost terms are equal — the same structure as a calibration interval.

MFG 254 · Lesson 06 · Polymer melt processing: extrusion

Lesson 06Polymer melt processing: extrusion

Key points

  • A single screw feeds, melts and pumps with one rotating part, and the melting is mostly mechanical — so melt temperature and output are coupled to screw speed.
  • Screw characteristic \( Q=A-B\Delta P \): drag flow forward, pressure flow back, with \( B \) going as the channel depth cubed.
  • For a 60 mm screw at 60 rev/min, \( A=15.40\ \mathrm{cm^3/s} \) and \( B=1.30\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m^3/(s\,Pa)} \).
  • Die characteristic \( Q=(K/\eta)\Delta P \) with \( K=\pi R^4/8L_d \); the operating point is \( \Delta P^{*}=A/(B+K/\eta) \).
  • A \( \varnothing5 \) die gives 9.26 MPa and 14.20 cm³/s (47.5 kg/h); a \( \varnothing4 \) die gives 20.3 MPa and 12.76 — 2.2 times the pressure for a tenth less output, because the screw is a stiff pump.
  • That stiffness is why head pressure, not throughput, is the early warning of a blinding screen pack or a chilling die.
  • Die swell is viscoelastic recovery: \( B\approx1+(B_0-1)e^{-t/\lambda} \), so 1.38 at a 20 mm land and 1.13 at 100 mm, at 0.72 m/s in the land.
  • The die is never the product size: a 5.0 mm strand at \( B=1.25 \) needs a 4.0 mm die.
  • Downstream, drawdown is an area ratio — \( (5/3)^2=2.78 \) — and the cooling bath is a Fourier-number calculation: 13.5 s, so 13.5 m at 1 m/s.
  • Cable is extruded because a cable is continuous and every insulation joint is an electrical defect; 4.62 mm² of wall at 300 m/min is 77 kg/h, and output and line speed are one variable, so a 1% output drift is a 1% wall error.

MFG 254 · Lesson 07 · Injection molding

Lesson 07Injection molding

Key points

  • The cycle is fill, pack, cool, eject, and cooling dominates: \( t=\frac{h^2}{\pi^2\alpha}\ln[\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{T_m-T_w}{T_e-T_w}] \).
  • Thickness is squared outside the logarithm and temperatures sit inside it, so geometry dominates and process settings barely move the cycle.
  • 3 mm wall: 14.4 s cooling, 23.4 s cycle, 154 parts/h. 1.5 mm wall: 3.6 s, 10.6 s, 340 parts/h — 2.2 times the output from the same tool.
  • Clamp force \( =p_{\rm cav}A_{\rm proj} \): 525 kN, 53.5 tonnes for a 150×100 plate at 35 MPa.
  • The gate obeys the same cooling law and therefore sets the packing window: 1.6 s at 1.0 mm, 6.4 s at 2.0 mm — the smallest feature in the tool governing dimensional control.
  • A cold runner is 21% of every shot; a hot runner removes it at the cost of tooling, on the same crossover as Lesson 4.
  • Uniform shrinkage is a tooling calculation, \( L_{\rm mould}=L_{\rm part}/(1-s)=152.28 \) mm for a 150 mm part at 1.5%.
  • Differential shrinkage is the real problem: a filled grade shrinking 0.3% along flow and 0.9% across gives a 0.9 mm differential on a 150 mm plate, and the part bows.
  • Gate location sets the flow field, the flow sets the orientation, orientation sets the differential and the differential sets the warp — so a centre gate turns a bow into a dish.
  • Defects map to phases: shorts and burns to filling, flash to clamping, sinks and voids to packing, warp to cooling and orientation. A junction 1.3× the wall cools 1.69× slower, which is why ribs are 0.5–0.6 of the wall.

MFG 254 · Lesson 08 · Blow molding, thermoforming, and film

Lesson 08Blow molding, thermoforming, and film

Key points

  • Blow moulding allocates the wall in the parison, not the mould: \( t=t_{\rm parison}/\mathrm{BUR} \), so a 3.0 mm parison at BUR 2.5 gives 1.20 mm.
  • A real bottle's blow-up ratio varies along its height, so a fixed die gap gives a wall varying 3.3:1 — 3.33 mm at a 30 mm neck against 1.00 at a 100 mm body.
  • Parison programming commands the gap as \( t_{\rm target}\times\mathrm{BUR}(z) \), here 0.75 to 2.5 mm; sag and swell shift with melt temperature and rate.
  • Thermoforming thins by areal draw ratio: a 200×150×60 tray gives \( \mathrm{DR}=2.4 \) and a 0.50 mm average wall from 1.2 mm sheet.
  • The average is a budget: a corner drawing at 4.0 finishes 0.30 mm, 40% thin, which is why plug assist exists and why the part is specified on minimum wall.
  • Blown film: \( h=h_{\rm die}/(\mathrm{BUR}\times\mathrm{TUR})=24\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) from a 1.2 mm gap at 2.5 and 20.
  • \( \mathrm{TUR}/\mathrm{BUR} \) sets orientation balance — 8 here, so stiff along the web and easy to tear along it; cast film has better gauge and no transverse orientation at all.
  • Food contact carries two migration limits, 10 mg/dm² and 60 mg/kg, which cross at \( A/m=6\ \mathrm{dm^2/kg} \); the worked bottle sits at 7, so 30 mg is permitted, not 35.
  • Small packs are the hard case, and the limits govern the additive recipe as much as the resin — a food-contact grade is chosen for what has been left out.
  • Barrier is a series-resistance problem: 5 µm of EVOH, 2.5% of a 200 µm wall, cuts oxygen transmission 26-fold.

MFG 254 · Lesson 09 · Packaging and filling operations

Lesson 09Packaging and filling operations

Key points

  • Rigidly coupled machines multiply: \( A_{\rm line}=\prod A_i \), so eight at 98% give 85% and eight at 95% give 66%. Adding a station is never free.
  • Accumulation is sized as rate times duration: 600 containers, 48 m of conveyor at 80 mm pitch, absorbs a 60-second stop on a 600 bpm line.
  • The V-curve rates the filler slowest and everything else faster, by more with distance: 720/660/600/660/700/740/780 bpm.
  • Upstream speed stops the filler being starved; downstream speed stops it being blocked. The V looks symmetric and its halves solve opposite problems.
  • Protecting the filler moves line availability from the product of everyone's toward the filler's own — 85% to 98%, a factor of 1.15 for no extra filler.
  • OEE = availability × performance × quality: 0.90 × 0.95 × 0.995 = 85.1%, so 510 of a nominal 600 bpm.
  • Hygienic design is cleanability without dismantling: drainable surfaces, no dead legs beyond \( L/D=1.5 \), crevice-free joints, and \( R_a\le0.8\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) on contact surfaces.
  • At \( R_a\ 3.2 \) the valleys are four times deeper than the 1 µm organisms they shelter, which is why the finish is a hygiene specification rather than a cosmetic one.
  • CIP needs at least 1.5 m/s — \( \mathrm{Re}=75{,}000 \) in a 50 mm line — because turbulence is the mechanical action; where the velocity is not reached, chemistry cannot compensate.
  • SMED converts internal work to external first, then streamlines: 120 to 35 minutes releases 170 min/day, 102,000 bottles and 17.7% of the day — and makes economic batches 46% smaller, since \( \mathrm{EOQ}\propto\sqrt{S} \).

MFG 254 · Lesson 10 · Additive manufacturing processes

Lesson 10Additive manufacturing processes

Key points

  • Four families differ in how material is placed and bonded: powder bed fusion melts a bed, DED melts on a moving head, extrusion welds a bead, binder jetting prints binder and sinters.
  • The best process for finish is not the best for strength: binder jetting leads on surface, powder bed fusion on strength, extrusion is last on both.
  • Staircase roughness is geometry: \( R_a=h\cos\theta/4 \), so 200/50/20 µm layers give 35/8.8/3.5 µm at a 45° surface.
  • Build time is \( \frac{H}{h}(t_{\rm recoat}+A/v\,\text{hatch}) \) — 14.85 h at 50 µm and 7.42 h at 100.
  • Time goes as \( 1/h \) and roughness as \( h \), so doubling the layer height exactly halves one and doubles the other.
  • Powder bed fusion reaches 95–100% of wrought across the layers and 85–95% along the build; material extrusion reaches 45 MPa and 15 MPa on a 50 MPa polymer — a factor of three.
  • Orientation is a structural decision, and the applied case is a bracket printed flat with its load across the layers.
  • Post-processing is 30–70% of a metal part's cost: stress relief before plate separation, support removal, optional HIP, machining, finishing and internal inspection.
  • Binder jetting compensates sintering by \( L_{\rm green}=L_{\rm final}/(1-s) \) — 1.22× at 18% linear, and 45% by volume.
  • Additive has near-zero tooling and a flat per-part cost, so it crosses over at 46 parts on the worked bracket — and wins overwhelmingly on lead time, where 79 days saved at 8,000 a day is 632,000 and the part's own cost hardly signifies.

MFG 254 · Lesson 11 · Design for manufacturing and assembly

Lesson 11Design for manufacturing and assembly

Key points

  • About three-quarters of manufacturing cost is committed by design release while little has been spent, and change costs escalate roughly 1 / 10 / 100 / 1,000 / 10,000 from concept to post-launch.
  • Every part carries three costs — piece, assembly operation and part-number overhead — and only the first appears on a quotation.
  • Cheapening acts on one term; consolidation acts on \( n \) and therefore on all three.
  • On a 20-part assembly at 69.00, a 10% piece cut saves 6.00 while consolidating to 12 parts at 40% dearer pieces saves 13.20 — 2.2 times as much.
  • At 4.60 a piece the consolidated design loses on piece cost (55.20 against 54.00) and still wins on total (60.60 against 63.00), so a piece-price metric rejects the better design.
  • A part stays separate only if it moves, must be a different material, or must be separate to allow assembly; the survivors define the theoretical minimum and the DFA index \( 3n_{\min}/t \) — 9.4% before, 25% after.
  • Self-locating features remove the alignment sub-operation, and asymmetry that makes wrong assembly impossible removes the defect mode as well.
  • Top-down assembly removes reorientations — three at 6 s is 18 s per unit, permanently — and eliminating four screws saves 0.78 plus four part numbers.
  • Tolerance realism is arithmetic: \( \sigma=0.02 \) mm gives \( C_p=1.67 \) at \( \pm0.1 \) and 0.42 at \( \pm0.025 \), so the tighter tolerance is a routing change, not a tightening.
  • Make it a process — early manufacturing involvement, a producibility gate, total-cost evaluation and the nonconformance record as the feedback channel — and track part count, unique part numbers, assembly time, reorientations and the DFA index.
