Sheet-metal cutting operations

Shearing, blanking, and punching; clearance and its burr signature. Cutting force estimates. Nesting and material yield as quiet profit levers.

§1 · What a punch actually does to sheet

Sheet-metal cutting is not cutting in the machining sense. Nothing is removed as chips: the material is stressed until it fractures along a line the tooling defines. Shearing makes a straight cut, usually to size a strip. Blanking cuts a closed contour and keeps the piece that comes out. Piercing cuts a closed contour and keeps the sheet, throwing away the slug. The mechanics are identical; only the definition of scrap changes, and that difference decides how the tooling is dimensioned.

Follow the punch down and four things happen in order, each leaving its own band on the finished edge. First the material bends into the die opening — rollover, a rounded lip. Then the punch penetrates and the material flows past its edge under compression, producing a smooth, shiny burnished band that is the only part of the edge you would call machined. At some depth the stress reaches the material's limit and cracks start from the punch corner and the die corner; when they meet, the piece separates, leaving a rough, angled fracture zone. Whatever material was dragged rather than cut stands proud as the burr.

An edge is therefore a record of the process that made it, readable without instruments. A wide burnished band with two distinct shiny zones separated by a step means the cracks did not meet and the material was sheared twice. A large rollover and a heavy burr means the opposite. Which of those you get is set almost entirely by one dimension.

§2 · Clearance, and which tool carries the size

Clearance is the gap between punch and die, per side. It is specified as a fraction of the sheet thickness because that is what the crack geometry actually depends on.

Key relation — clearance

\[ c = A_c\,t \]

\( A_c \) is the clearance allowance: roughly 0.045 for soft aluminium, 0.060 for low-carbon steel, 0.075 for stainless and hard alloys — harder and less ductile material needs more.

The clearance's job is to make the two cracks meet. Too little and they miss: the material between them shears a second time, the edge shows a double burnished band, the force rises and the tooling wears faster. Too much and the material is dragged into the gap rather than cut: heavy rollover, a large ragged burr, and a blank that is dished rather than flat. For 3 mm low-carbon steel, \( c = 0.060(3) = 0.18\ \mathrm{mm} \) per side, which is 0.36 mm across a diameter and far larger than most people expect.

That 0.36 mm has to be taken off one tool or added to the other, and the choice is not arbitrary.

Key rule — the product side carries the size

Blanking: the blank is the product, and it takes the size of the die opening. So \( D_{\text{die}} = D \) and \( D_{\text{punch}} = D - 2c \).

Piercing: the hole is the product, and it takes the size of the punch. So \( D_{\text{punch}} = D \) and \( D_{\text{die}} = D + 2c \).

Blanking a \( \varnothing60 \) disc from 3 mm steel therefore needs a 60.00 mm die and a 59.64 mm punch. Piercing a \( \varnothing25 \) hole in the same sheet needs a 25.00 mm punch and a 25.36 mm die. Reverse either rule and every part is 0.36 mm out — a mistake that survives inspection only because it is consistent.

§3 · Force, and how to need less press than the arithmetic says

The force follows from the area actually being sheared, which is the cut perimeter times the thickness.

Key relations — cutting and stripping force

\[ F = S\,t\,L,\qquad S\approx0.7\,\mathrm{TS},\qquad F_{\text{strip}}\approx(0.02\ \text{to}\ 0.10)F \]

\( L \) is the length of the cut, \( t \) the thickness, \( S \) the shear strength. The stripping force pulls the sheet back off the punch after the cut and is carried by the same press stroke, so it belongs in the tonnage sum.

For the \( \varnothing60 \) blank: \( L=\pi(60)=188.5\ \mathrm{mm} \), and with \( \mathrm{TS}=340 \) MPa, \( S=238 \) MPa. Then \( F=238(3)(188.5)=134{,}600\ \mathrm{N} \), which is 134.6 kN, 15.1 US tons, or about 13.7 tonnes force. Adding 5% stripping brings it to 141.3 kN. How the tonnage a press can deliver varies through its stroke is Lesson 5's subject; here the point is only that the number is large and comes almost entirely from the perimeter.

There is a way to need much less of it. Grinding an angular shear onto the tool face means the cut starts at one point and progresses along the contour instead of happening everywhere at once. The total work is unchanged — the same material still has to be separated — but it is spread over a longer punch travel, so the peak force falls.

Key relation — the effect of angular shear

\[ F_{\text{peak}}\approx F\,\frac{p_f t}{p_f t+s} \]

\( s \) is the shear depth ground onto the tool and \( p_f \) is the fraction of the thickness penetrated before fracture, around 0.5 for medium steel. The work \( F\,p_f t \) is conserved; only its distribution over the stroke changes.

With \( t=3 \) mm and \( p_f=0.5 \), a shear of 1.5 mm halves the peak force to 67.3 kN and a shear of 3 mm cuts it to 44.9 kN. The catch is that the tool face is no longer flat, so whatever it pushes on is left distorted — which fixes where the shear goes. In piercing the slug is scrap, so the shear goes on the punch. In blanking the strip is scrap, so the shear goes on the die and the blank comes out flat. Putting it on the wrong member produces a correctly sized, permanently dished product.

§4 · Nesting: the profit nobody watches

In a stamped part the material is commonly half to three-quarters of the cost, and how much of the coil becomes product is decided at the layout stage by a draughtsman, once, for the life of the tool.

Key relations — strip layout

\[ \text{utilisation}=\frac{A_{\text{blank}}}{W\,p/n},\qquad p = D + b,\qquad W = D + 2m\ \ (\text{single row}) \]

\( p \) is the feed pitch, \( b \) the bridge between blanks, \( m \) the web margin at the strip edges, \( n \) the blanks produced per pitch. The bridge and margin are not waste to be minimised freely: they are what holds the strip together through the die, and cutting them too fine tears the carrier.

Take the \( \varnothing60 \) blank with a 3 mm bridge and 3 mm margins. A single row needs a 66 mm strip advancing 63 mm per blank, so each blank consumes \( 66\times63=4{,}158 \) mm² and utilisation is \( 2827/4158=68.0\% \).

Now stagger two rows so the second sits in the gaps of the first. Blanks in adjacent rows are offset half a pitch along the feed, so their centre distance is \( \sqrt{s^2+(p/2)^2} \), and that has to be at least \( D+b \). Solving, \( s=\sqrt{63^2-31.5^2}=54.56\ \mathrm{mm} \) — the rows can be much closer than 63 mm apart because they are also displaced along the strip. The strip becomes \( 60+54.56+6=120.56 \) mm wide and yields two blanks per 63 mm of feed, so each blank consumes 3,798 mm² and utilisation rises to 74.5%.

That is 8.7% less coil per blank. On 3 mm steel it is 8.5 grams a part, 849 kg per hundred thousand, for a change that costs one afternoon at the layout stage and nothing per part thereafter. Three qualifications keep it honest: a wider strip needs a wider press and feed, a staggered layout needs two punches or a two-up die, and any layout that assumes a rectangular blank orientation must be checked against the rolling direction if the part will later be bent — which is the next lesson's problem.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Size the tools, then the press

Problem. A \( \varnothing60 \) mm disc is blanked from 3 mm low-carbon steel (\( \mathrm{TS}=340 \) MPa, clearance allowance 0.060), and a \( \varnothing25 \) mm hole is pierced in the same sheet. Give the punch and die diameters for both operations. Then find the blanking force, the stripping force at 5%, and the total, in kilonewtons and in US tons.

Solution. (1) \( c=A_ct=0.060(3.0)=0.18\ \mathrm{mm} \) per side.

(2) Blanking: the blank takes the size of the die opening, so the die is \( \varnothing60.00 \) and the punch is \( 60.00-2(0.18)=\varnothing59.64\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(3) Piercing: the hole takes the size of the punch, so the punch is \( \varnothing25.00 \) and the die is \( 25.00+2(0.18)=\varnothing25.36\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(4) \( S\approx0.7\,\mathrm{TS}=0.7(340)=238\ \mathrm{MPa} \).

(5) \( L=\pi(60)=188.5\ \mathrm{mm} \), so \( F=StL=238(3)(188.5)=134{,}586\ \mathrm{N}=134.6\ \mathrm{kN} \).

(6) \( F_{\text{strip}}=0.05(134.6)=6.73\ \mathrm{kN} \), giving a total of \( 141.3\ \mathrm{kN} \).

(7) In US customary: \( 134{,}586/4.4482=30{,}256\ \mathrm{lbf}=15.13 \) US tons for the cut, and \( 141{,}315/4.4482/2000=15.89 \) US tons in total.

Answer: blanking die \( \varnothing60.00 \), punch \( \varnothing59.64 \); piercing punch \( \varnothing25.00 \), die \( \varnothing25.36 \); 134.6 kN of cutting force plus 6.7 kN stripping, 141.3 kN (15.9 US tons) in total. Sanity check: the two operations put the clearance on opposite members, and the reason is the same in both cases — the product side carries the nominal size. Reversing it gives parts 0.36 mm out on every piece, consistently enough to pass an unwary first-article check. Note that the force depends on the perimeter, not the area: the \( \varnothing25 \) hole needs \( 238(3)(\pi\times25)=56.1 \) kN, less than half the blank's, despite removing less than a fifth of the area.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Buy the press down with angular shear

Problem. The 134.6 kN blanking operation above is to run on a press rated 100 kN. Taking the penetration fraction at fracture as \( p_f=0.5 \), find the shear depth needed on the tool to bring the peak force under the rating with 20% margin, and the peak force at a shear of 3 mm. State which member the shear is ground onto and why.

Solution. (1) The work of separation, \( F\,p_ft \), is fixed. Angular shear spreads it over a punch travel of \( p_ft+s \), so \( F_{\text{peak}}=F\,p_ft/(p_ft+s) \).

(2) Here \( p_ft=0.5(3)=1.5\ \mathrm{mm} \), so \( F_{\text{peak}}=134.6\times1.5/(1.5+s) \).

(3) A 20% margin on a 100 kN press means a target of 80 kN. Setting \( 134.6(1.5)/(1.5+s)=80 \) gives \( 1.5+s=2.524 \), so \( s=1.02\ \mathrm{mm} \). Round up: a 1.5 mm shear gives \( 134.6(1.5)/3.0=67.3\ \mathrm{kN} \), comfortably inside.

(4) At \( s=3 \) mm: \( 134.6(1.5)/4.5=44.9\ \mathrm{kN} \), a third of the flat-tool force.

(5) This is a blanking operation, so the blank is the product and the strip is scrap. The sheared face distorts whatever it presses on, so the shear goes on the die, leaving the blank flat and dishing the scrap strip. In piercing it would go on the punch, because there the slug is the scrap.

Answer: about 1.0 mm of shear meets the 80 kN target and a standard 1.5 mm gives 67.3 kN; 3 mm gives 44.9 kN. The shear is ground on the die, because the blank must stay flat. Sanity check: nothing about the material or the cut length changed, so the total work is the same in every case — only its distribution over the stroke moved, which is exactly why the press requirement fell and the tool life did not improve with it. Note the failure mode this prevents and the one it introduces: it keeps a 134.6 kN cut off a 100 kN press, and it guarantees a dished part if the shear is ground on the wrong member.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

What a layout decision is worth

Problem. The \( \varnothing60 \) blank is to be nested with a 3 mm bridge and 3 mm web margins. Find the strip width, the material consumed per blank and the utilisation for a single row; then for two staggered rows, deriving the row offset. Give the coil saved per blank as a percentage and as a mass, and the annual saving on 100,000 blanks. Take steel at 7.85 g/cm³.

Solution. (1) Pitch \( p=D+b=60+3=63\ \mathrm{mm} \); blank area \( A=\pi(60)^2/4=2827\ \mathrm{mm}^2 \).

(2) Single row: \( W=D+2m=60+6=66\ \mathrm{mm} \), so each blank consumes \( 66\times63=4158\ \mathrm{mm}^2 \) and utilisation is \( 2827/4158=68.0\% \).

(3) Staggered rows are offset half a pitch along the feed, so the centre distance between blanks in adjacent rows is \( \sqrt{s^2+(p/2)^2} \), which must be at least \( D+b=63 \). Hence \( s=\sqrt{63^2-31.5^2}=\sqrt{3969-992.25}=54.56\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(4) \( W=60+54.56+6=120.56\ \mathrm{mm} \), and two blanks come off per 63 mm of feed, so each consumes \( 120.56\times63/2=3798\ \mathrm{mm}^2 \) and utilisation is \( 2827/3798=74.5\% \).

(5) Coil saved: \( 1-3798/4158=8.67\% \).

(6) Mass: \( 4158\times3=12{,}474\ \mathrm{mm}^3=12.47\ \mathrm{cm}^3 \), so 97.9 g per blank single-row against \( 3798\times3\times7.85\times10^{-3}=89.4 \) g staggered — 8.49 g saved.

(7) On 100,000 blanks: \( 8.49\times10^5\ \mathrm{g}=849\ \mathrm{kg} \).

Answer: single row 66 mm strip, 4,158 mm² and 68.0%; staggered 120.56 mm strip, 3,798 mm² and 74.5%; 8.67% less coil, 8.5 g per blank, 849 kg per 100,000. Sanity check: the rows sit 54.56 mm apart on a part 60 mm across, which looks impossible until you remember they are also displaced 31.5 mm along the feed — the constraint is the centre distance, not the cross-strip gap, and that is the whole trick. Note what the saving is not: it is not free, because the wider strip needs a wider press and feed and the layout needs two punches. Note also that it is decided once, by a draughtsman, and then repeats on every part for the life of the tool, which is why it deserves an afternoon.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

The shiny band on a sheared edge is produced by

That burnished band is the only part of the edge made by anything resembling cutting. Above it sits the rollover where the material first bent into the die, below it the rough fracture zone where cracks from the punch and die corners met, and beyond that the burr. The four zones together are a readable record of the clearance that produced them.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

Clearance that is too small produces

With too little clearance the cracks from the two corners miss each other and the material between them is sheared a second time, leaving two shiny bands with a step between them and raising the force. Heavy burr and rollover are the excessive-clearance signature. Clearance does not change the blank's size — that is set by whichever member is the product side.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

To blank a \( \varnothing60 \) mm disc from 3 mm steel with \( A_c=0.060 \), the tools should be

In blanking the blank is the product and it takes the size of the die opening, so the die is nominal and the punch is smaller by \( 2c=0.36 \) mm. The first option is the piercing rule applied by mistake, and it would make every disc 0.36 mm oversize — consistently enough to survive a careless inspection.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Doubling the diameter of a blanked disc, at constant thickness, changes the cutting force by a factor of

\( F=StL \) and \( L \) is the perimeter, so force scales with diameter, not with area. This is why a long thin slot can need more tonnage than a large round hole, and why adding a decorative outline to a stamping is not free.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

In a blanking operation the angular shear should be ground onto the

A sheared tool face distorts whatever it presses on. In blanking the product is the blank and the scrap is the strip, so the shear goes on the die. In piercing the slug is the scrap, so it goes on the punch. Putting it on the wrong member gives a correctly sized and permanently dished product.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Staggering two rows of \( \varnothing60 \) blanks at a 63 mm pitch lets the rows sit 54.56 mm apart because

The constraint is the centre-to-centre distance, not the cross-strip gap. With a 31.5 mm offset along the feed, a 54.56 mm row spacing still gives \( \sqrt{54.56^2+31.5^2}=63 \) mm between centres. It is why staggering works at all, and why the saving grows with how round the blank is.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

Moving the \( \varnothing60 \) blank from a single row to staggered double rows takes utilisation from 68.0% to 74.5%. The coil saved per blank is

Utilisation rose by 6.5 percentage points, which is a 9.5% relative rise, but what the shop buys is coil: \( 1-3798/4158=8.67\% \) less steel per blank. Reporting the percentage point difference understates it and the utilisation ratio overstates it — the quantity that appears on an invoice is the area consumed.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

The bridge and web margin in a strip layout exist to

They are the carrier: the strip has to survive being fed, located and stripped after each station, and a bridge cut too fine tears. That is why utilisation cannot simply be driven toward 100% by shrinking them, and why the real gains come from the layout geometry instead.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the tool that was ordered from the wrong rule. A fabricator ordered a blanking tool for a \( \varnothing60 \) mm disc in 3 mm steel. The tool arrived with a \( \varnothing60.00 \) punch and a \( \varnothing60.36 \) die. First-article inspection measured the blanks at \( \varnothing60.34 \) against a \( \varnothing60\ \pm0.2 \) drawing, and because every part measured the same the shop concluded the process was capable and released it. The discs were rejected at assembly.

The tool had been dimensioned by the piercing rule. In blanking the blank is the product and it takes the size of the die opening, so the die should have been \( \varnothing60.00 \) and the punch \( \varnothing59.64 \). Sized the other way, every disc came out roughly \( 2c=0.36 \) mm oversize — consistently, which is exactly why it passed a check that looked at spread rather than at position. Metrology & Quality Control makes the same point from the other side: a process can be in perfect control and stably wrong, and a capability index compares the process with the tolerance, not with itself.

Two practices follow. State on the tool drawing which member is nominal and why, in words rather than only in numbers — "die nominal, blank is the product" is one line and it removes the entire failure mode. And treat a first article that is consistently offset as the most suspicious result available: consistency is evidence about the spread and says nothing at all about whether the tooling was built to the right rule.
