Design for manufacturing and assembly

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Give two DFM rules that remove cost without removing function.
  • Why does part-count reduction beat per-part cheapening?

§1 · Cost is committed long before it is spent

By the time a design is released, something like three-quarters of the product's manufacturing cost is committed — fixed by the geometry, the materials, the tolerances and the number of parts — while only a small fraction has actually been spent. Every process in this course confirms it. Lesson 1's nesting decision repeats on every blank for the life of the tool. Lesson 7's wall thickness sets the moulding cycle for every shot. Lesson 2's bend radius decides whether the part can be made from the cheaper grade at all.

That asymmetry is the entire argument for design for manufacturing. The influence is largest at the point where the least is known and the least has been spent, and it decays as drawings become tools. The conventional escalation is a change costing 1 at concept, 10 at detail design, 100 once tooling is cut, 1,000 in production and 10,000 after launch — a rule of thumb rather than a measurement, but the right order of magnitude and the right shape.

What follows is not a plea to think harder. It is a small set of rules whose effect can be computed, and a process for applying them before the leverage is gone.

§2 · Why removing a part beats making it cheaper

Every part in an assembly carries three costs. The piece cost is the obvious one. The assembly operation that installs it is the second. The third is the cost of being a part number at all: purchasing, receiving, inventory, goods-in inspection, engineering drawings, change control. Only the first appears on a quotation, and the other two are the ones that vanish when a part does.

Key comparison — two ways to take cost out

\[ C=n\left(c_{\text{piece}}+c_{\text{assembly}}+c_{\text{part number}}\right) \]

Cheapening acts on \( c_{\text{piece}} \) alone. Consolidating acts on \( n \), and therefore on all three terms at once.

Take a twenty-part assembly at 3.00 a piece, 0.10 of assembly labour per part and 0.35 of part-number overhead: 69.00 in total. Now compare two projects.

Cheapen everything by 10% — a real achievement, requiring twenty supplier negotiations or twenty design changes. The result is \( 20(2.70+0.10+0.35)=63.00 \): a saving of 6.00, or 8.7%.

Consolidate to twelve parts, accepting that the survivors are more complex and cost 40% more each. The result is \( 12(4.20+0.10+0.35)=55.80 \): a saving of 13.20, or 19.1% — more than twice as much, from parts that individually got dearer.

The comparison is worth pushing further, because it exposes a real organisational failure. Suppose the survivors cost 4.60 each, 53% more. The consolidated design's pieces now total 55.20 against the cheapened design's 54.00, so on piece cost it has lost. Its total is 60.60 against 63.00, so on real cost it has won. A purchasing function measured on piece price will reject the better design, correctly by its own metric and wrongly by the company's.

Which parts may be combined is a question with a published answer. A part needs to be separate only if it must move relative to its neighbours, if it must be a different material for a fundamental reason, or if keeping it separate is the only way to assemble anything else. Every other part is a candidate, and counting the ones that pass gives the theoretical minimum from which the DFA index is computed — the theoretical minimum times three seconds, over the actual assembly time. Our baseline, with a theoretical minimum of five parts and 160 s of assembly, scores 9.4%; the consolidated design at 60 s scores 25%.

§3 · Rules that remove cost without removing function

Self-locating features. A chamfer, a locating boss, a step that a part can only sit on one way: these remove the alignment part of an assembly operation and remove the defect mode with it. If six of an eight-second operation is aligning, the feature removes 75% of that operation and the inspection it justified. The strongest version is asymmetry that makes wrong assembly impossible — a part that cannot be fitted backwards needs no instruction, no poka-yoke fixture and no check.

Top-down assembly. Design so every part drops in along one axis, ideally from above with gravity holding it. Each reorientation of a partly built assembly costs several seconds and usually a fixture; removing three at six seconds each takes 18 s out of every unit built, forever.

Eliminate fasteners. A threaded fastener is not one item but five: the screw, the thread feature, the driving operation, the torque specification and the inspection of it. Four screws at 0.12 with six seconds of driving each cost 0.78 an assembly before any of the overhead, and they add four part numbers. A snap fit converts all of that into geometry that is already being moulded.

Tolerance realism. This is the rule Metrology & Quality Control was written to support, and it can be settled arithmetically rather than argued. A turning operation holding \( \sigma=0.02 \) mm meets \( \pm0.1 \) mm at \( C_p=0.2/(6\times0.02)=1.67 \) — comfortable. Asked for \( \pm0.025 \) mm, the same process gives \( C_p=0.42 \), which is not a tighter tolerance but a different process: grinding, another setup, another inspection. The tolerance did not get harder; it changed the routing. The same is true elsewhere in this course — a press brake holds about \( \pm0.5^{\circ} \) (Lesson 2), a roll-formed section about \( \pm0.5 \) mm (Lesson 4), and a powder-bed part about \( \pm0.1 \) mm before machining (Lesson 10). A drawing that asks for better is specifying a secondary operation whether or not it says so.

Design for the process actually chosen. Draft angles and uniform walls if it is moulded (Lesson 7); radii above \( R_{\min} \) and bend lines across the rolling direction if it is formed (Lesson 2); outlines that nest if it is blanked (Lesson 1); loads in the layer plane if it is printed (Lesson 10). None of these removes function. Each of them removes an operation, a defect mode or a material cost that the drawing was going to incur silently.

§4 · Making the conversation a process

All of this fails as goodwill and works as a procedure, because the leverage decays with time and goodwill has no schedule.

Four mechanisms do most of the work. Early manufacturing involvement — a manufacturing engineer in the design reviews from concept, not at release, because at release the answer to every objection is that the tooling is ordered. A producibility checklist applied at a defined gate, so the questions get asked in a fixed order rather than remembered. A costed redesign loop: proposals evaluated on total cost including assembly and part-number overhead, not on piece price, or the arithmetic of \( \S2 \) will send every decision the wrong way. And a feedback channel from the floor with evidence attached — which already exists, because the nonconformance record of Metrology & Quality Control L11 is exactly that channel when the disposition names a design cause rather than a process one.

The measurable outputs are few and worth tracking by name: part count, number of unique part numbers, assembly time, number of reorientations, and the DFA index. All of them are countable from a drawing set before anything is made, which is the point — a design review that produces numbers can be repeated, and one that produces opinions cannot.

It is a reasonable place to end a manufacturing course. Every lesson here has been about a process constraining a design: clearance and nesting in Lesson 1, springback in Lesson 2, the limiting draw ratio in Lesson 3, edge strain in Lesson 4, the tonnage curve in Lesson 5, die swell in Lesson 6, the square law in Lesson 7, blow-up ratio in Lesson 8, the V-curve in Lesson 9 and build orientation in Lesson 10. Design for manufacturing is not an additional subject. It is those constraints applied early enough to be free.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Two projects, one assembly

Problem. An assembly has 20 parts at 3.00 a piece, 0.10 of assembly labour per part and 0.35 of part-number overhead per part. (a) Find the total. (b) Project A cheapens every piece by 10%; find the new total and the saving. (c) Project B consolidates to 12 parts whose pieces cost 40% more each; find the new total and the saving. (d) Show that B still wins if its pieces cost 4.60 each, and say what that implies about how projects are judged.

Solution. (a) Each part carries \( 3.00+0.10+0.35=3.45 \), so \( 20\times3.45=69.00 \).

(b) Pieces fall to 2.70, so \( 20(2.70+0.10+0.35)=20(3.15)=63.00 \) — a saving of 6.00, or 8.7%.

(c) Pieces rise to \( 3.00\times1.40=4.20 \), but there are only twelve: \( 12(4.20+0.10+0.35)=12(4.65)=55.80 \) — a saving of 13.20, or 19.1%, which is 2.2 times A's.

(d1) At 4.60 a piece, B's pieces total \( 12\times4.60=55.20 \), against A's \( 20\times2.70=54.00 \). On piece cost B has lost.

(d2) B's total is \( 12(4.60+0.10+0.35)=12(5.05)=60.60 \), against A's 63.00. On real cost B has won.

(d3) A project judged on piece price would reject B — correctly by that metric and wrongly by the company's. Consolidation must be evaluated on total cost including assembly labour and part-number overhead, or the arithmetic sends every decision the wrong way.

Answer: 69.00 baseline; A saves 6.00 (8.7%), B saves 13.20 (19.1%); at 4.60 a piece B still wins on total cost while losing on piece cost. Sanity check: the reason B wins is structural rather than arithmetic luck — cheapening acts on one of the three cost terms while consolidation acts on all three at once, because the assembly operation and the part number disappear with the part instead of shrinking with it. Note the organisational consequence in (d): the metric a purchasing function is measured on cannot see two-thirds of the saving, which is why DFA proposals need a costing that includes them explicitly.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Settle a tolerance argument with arithmetic

Problem. A turned diameter is produced by a process with \( \sigma=0.02 \) mm. (a) Find \( C_p \) against a \( \pm0.1 \) mm tolerance and against \( \pm0.025 \) mm, and say what each means for the routing. (b) The design office proposes tightening a press-brake angle from \( \pm0.5^{\circ} \) to \( \pm0.1^{\circ} \) and a roll-formed section from \( \pm0.5 \) mm to \( \pm0.1 \) mm; comment on both using this course. (c) State the general principle in one sentence.

Solution. (a1) \( C_p=(\mathrm{USL}-\mathrm{LSL})/6\sigma \). At \( \pm0.1 \): \( 0.20/(6\times0.02)=1.67 \) — comfortably above the usual 1.33 gate, so a single turning operation holds it.

(a2) At \( \pm0.025 \): \( 0.05/0.12=0.42 \) — far below 1.33. The process is not capable, so the feature needs a second operation: grinding, with its own setup, its own inspection and its own cost.

(a3) So the tolerance change is not a tightening. It is a routing change, and it should appear in the cost estimate as one.

(b1) A press brake holds about \( \pm0.5^{\circ} \) (Lesson 2), and springback varies with the material's \( Y/E \) and coil-to-coil, so \( \pm0.1^{\circ} \) needs a different process — bottoming, or an adaptive brake with in-process angle measurement.

(b2) A roll-formed section holds about \( \pm0.5 \) mm (Lesson 4), and the hard errors there are bow, camber and twist along the length rather than the section itself; \( \pm0.1 \) mm implies a straightening or machining operation after forming.

(c) A tolerance the chosen process cannot hold is not a tolerance; it is an unwritten instruction to add an operation.

Answer: \( C_p=1.67 \) and \( 0.42 \); the tighter tolerance changes the routing to turning-plus-grinding; both proposed changes in (b) specify secondary operations; and a tolerance beyond the process is a hidden operation. Sanity check: stating the argument as \( C_p \) converts an opinion into a number both sides can check, which is the whole value of Metrology & Quality Control's machinery in a design review. Note that the answer is never "the tolerance is too tight" in the abstract — it is too tight for the process on the routing, and either the tolerance or the routing has to change, with a cost attached to whichever moves.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Score the assembly before and after

Problem. The 20-part assembly takes 8 s a part to build, and applying the separateness test gives a theoretical minimum of 5 parts. (a) Find the assembly time and the DFA index. (b) After redesign it has 12 parts at 5 s each; find the new time and index. (c) The redesign also removed three reorientations at 6 s each and four screws costing 0.12 with 6 s of driving each at 0.0125 per second; find those savings. (d) Name the three tests that decide whether a part may be combined.

Solution. (a1) Assembly time \( =20\times8=160\ \mathrm{s} \).

(a2) DFA index \( =3n_{\min}/t=3(5)/160=15/160=9.4\% \).

(b1) After: \( 12\times5=60\ \mathrm{s} \).

(b2) Index \( =15/60=25\% \) — nearly three times better, and the improvement comes from both terms, since fewer parts are each faster to fit.

(c1) Reorientations: \( 3\times6=18\ \mathrm{s} \) per unit, removed permanently.

(c2) Fasteners: \( 4(0.12)+4(6)(0.0125)=0.48+0.30=0.78 \) per assembly, plus four part numbers and their overhead.

(d) A part must stay separate only if it moves relative to its neighbours, if it must be a different material for a fundamental reason, or if separating it is the only way to assemble the rest. Everything else is a candidate for combination.

Answer: 160 s and 9.4% before, 60 s and 25% after; 18 s of reorientation and 0.78 of fasteners removed; and the three tests are movement, material and assembly access. Sanity check: the index is deliberately harsh — three seconds a part is close to the best a human hand achieves, so even a good design scores well under 50%, and the number is useful as a comparison between two designs of the same product rather than as an absolute grade. Note that the reorientation and fastener savings are per unit and permanent, which is what distinguishes a design change from a process improvement: nobody has to keep doing anything for them to persist.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the cost-down project that saved nothing. An assembly of twenty parts was targeted for a 10% cost reduction. The project renegotiated or redesigned every piece and achieved it: piece cost fell from 3.00 to 2.70 across the board, a genuine result requiring twenty separate pieces of work. The assembly's total cost fell from 69.00 to 63.00 — 8.7%, against a 10% target — and the shortfall was blamed on the two parts whose suppliers had refused to move.

The target had been set on the wrong quantity. Each part carried 0.10 of assembly labour and 0.35 of part-number overhead as well as its piece cost, so piece cost was only 87% of the total and a 10% cut in it could never deliver 10% overall. Meanwhile a consolidation study done afterwards found the assembly could be built from twelve parts. Even with the survivors 40% dearer each, that design cost 55.80 — a 19.1% saving, more than twice what twenty negotiations had produced, and available from one redesign rather than twenty.

Two practices follow. Set cost-reduction targets against total assembly cost including assembly labour and part-number overhead, because a target set against piece price alone is arithmetically incapable of being met by piece-price work. And run the part-count study first: it is cheaper to do, it is done once rather than per part, and it changes what the piece-cost work should even be aimed at — there is no point negotiating the price of a part the design does not need.
