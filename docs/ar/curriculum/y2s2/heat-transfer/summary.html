HTX 253 — Heat Transfer

HTX 253 · Lesson 01 · Mechanisms and the thermal circuit

Lesson 01Mechanisms and the thermal circuit

Key points

  • Three rate laws do one job: Fourier for conduction, Newton's law of cooling for convection, Stefan–Boltzmann for radiation.
  • Cast each as a resistance and the problem becomes a circuit: \( R_{tot}=\sum_i R_i \) and \( q=\Delta T_{overall}/R_{tot} \).
  • Series resistances add, so the largest single resistance governs — find it before trying to improve anything.
  • Insulating a small-diameter pipe can raise heat loss: added conduction resistance competes with the larger outer area, and below the critical radius the area wins.
  • The critical radius is \( r_c=k/h \) — a real trap on small tubing, irrelevant on large pipe.
  • This resistor-chain bookkeeping carries unchanged through fins, exchangers and radiation for the rest of the course.

HTX 253 · Lesson 02 · Steady conduction and fins

Lesson 02Steady conduction and fins

Key points

  • A fin buys area when the surface coefficient is too low to reject heat any other way — it trades material for exposed surface.
  • The fin equation solves to a cosh profile; for an adiabatic tip, \( \eta_{fin}=\tanh(mL)/mL \) with \( m=\sqrt{hP/kA_c} \).
  • Efficiency compares the fin with an isothermal fin; effectiveness compares it with having no fin at all. They answer different questions.
  • Fins stop paying once \( mL \) is large: \( \tanh \) saturates, so extra length adds material and almost no heat.
  • The worked 200 mm pin fin runs at 59% efficiency with a tip still 41% above ambient — doubling its length to 400 mm bought only 9% more heat for twice the material.
  • Fins pay most where \( h \) is lowest, which is why they dominate natural-convection and gas-side surfaces.

HTX 253 · Lesson 03 · Transient conduction

Lesson 03Transient conduction

Key points

  • Lumped capacitance treats a body as one temperature: \( (T-T_\infty)/(T_i-T_\infty)=e^{-t/\tau} \) with \( \tau=\rho Vc/hA \).
  • The entry ticket is \( Bi=hL_c/k<0.1 \) — internal conduction must be fast compared with surface convection.
  • Check the Biot number before using the shortcut, not after; the worked pin passes in air at \( Bi=0.05 \) and fails once re-quenched in water.
  • The characteristic length is \( L_c=V/A \), the same volume-to-area ratio that governs solidification and fin behaviour.
  • When lumped fails, the body has a real internal gradient: use the semi-infinite solution or one-term series charts instead.
  • The Fourier number \( \alpha t/L_c^2 \) is dimensionless time — the natural clock for every transient problem.

HTX 253 · Lesson 04 · Forced convection: external

Lesson 04Forced convection: external

Key points

  • External forced convection is governed by two boundary layers — velocity and thermal — and their relative thickness is set by the Prandtl number.
  • The grammar of every correlation is \( Nu=f(Re,Pr) \); for a laminar flat plate, \( Nu_L=0.664\,Re_L^{0.5}Pr^{1/3} \).
  • Transition on a flat plate is near \( Re_{crit}\approx5\times10^5 \); this lesson's plate reaches only \( Re_L=62{,}500 \), so it stays laminar throughout.
  • A correlation is valid only inside the range it was fitted over — check \( Re \) and \( Pr \) limits before trusting a number.
  • \( Nu \) is a dimensionless \( h \): it says how much better convection is than pure conduction across the same layer.
  • The flat-plate \( h \) was cross-checked against an independent estimate and agreed to within 1% — always worth a second route.

HTX 253 · Lesson 05 · Forced convection: internal

Lesson 05Forced convection: internal

Key points

  • Internal flow has an entrance region and a fully developed region; "fully developed" means the profile shape stops changing, not that temperature stops changing.
  • The two standard boundary conditions behave differently: constant heat flux gives a linear temperature rise, constant wall temperature an exponential approach.
  • For constant wall temperature, \( (T_s-T_o)/(T_s-T_i)=\exp(-hPL/\dot mc_p) \).
  • That exponential means the last few metres of a heated pipe buy far less temperature rise than the first few — a real limit on "just add length".
  • Dittus–Boelter is the workhorse turbulent correlation for pipe flow.
  • Heat pickup along a run is a mass-flow and surface-area problem before it is a coefficient problem.

HTX 253 · Lesson 06 · Natural convection

Lesson 06Natural convection

Key points

  • Natural convection has no imposed velocity: buoyancy drives the flow, so the driving temperature difference appears on both sides of the problem.
  • Its dimensionless numbers are Grashof and Rayleigh, \( Ra=Gr\cdot Pr=\frac{g\beta\Delta TL^3}{\nu^2}Pr \).
  • Orientation changes everything — vertical plate, horizontal plate facing up, and facing down are different correlations, not variants of one.
  • Natural-convection \( h \) in a gas sits one to two orders of magnitude below even modest forced convection, which is exactly why fins pay off so decisively here.
  • A hotter ambient carries a double penalty: it shrinks \( \Delta T \) and weakens the buoyancy that drives the flow.
  • The worked sealed cabinet loses about 24% of its rejected heat to a Gulf-summer ambient even though \( h \) itself barely moves.

HTX 253 · Lesson 07 · Boiling and condensation

Lesson 07Boiling and condensation

Key points

  • The boiling curve has four regimes — free convection, nucleate, transition and film — and only nucleate boiling is a good place to operate.
  • Past the critical heat flux the surface flux falls before rising again, so a flux-controlled surface jumps straight to film boiling and overheats.
  • That jump is why CHF is a safety limit, not a performance target: Zuber's correlation gives \( q''_{max} \) for the worked case at about 1.1 MW/m² for water at 1 atm.
  • Condensation splits the same way: dropwise is far better, filmwise is what real plants get once surfaces age and wet out.
  • Nusselt's film-condensation results cover vertical plates and horizontal tubes.
  • A design specified near the CHF peak has no margin at all — the worked check flags a 0.85 MW/m² duty as running hotter than sound practice.

HTX 253 · Lesson 08 · Heat exchangers

Lesson 08Heat exchangers

Key points

  • Whatever the architecture — shell-and-tube, plate, double pipe — the thermal problem collapses to one overall coefficient \( U \) and one area.
  • LMTD sizing: \( q=UA\,\Delta T_{lm} \) with \( \Delta T_{lm}=(\Delta T_1-\Delta T_2)/\ln(\Delta T_1/\Delta T_2) \).
  • Use LMTD when both outlet temperatures are known; use effectiveness–NTU when they are not, which is the honest method for rating an existing exchanger.
  • Fouling enters as an extra resistance in \( U \), and it is usually the difference between a clean-duty calculation and installed reality.
  • Counterflow beats parallel flow for the same duty because it holds a larger mean temperature difference.
  • The \( C_r=0 \) case — condensing or evaporating streams — is the special case worth recognising on sight.

HTX 253 · Lesson 08 · Heat exchangers · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Thermal resistance networks (Lesson 1) — U is literally the same series-resistance idea, now with two convection films, a wall, and (in service) two fouling terms.

HTX 253 · Lesson 09 · Radiation: fundamentals and exchange

Lesson 09Radiation: fundamentals and exchange

Key points

  • Radiation needs no medium and scales as \( T^4 \), so it grows fast and is easy to underestimate at moderate temperatures.
  • Real surfaces are handled as gray surfaces with an emissivity; geometry enters through view factors, constrained by reciprocity and the enclosure rule.
  • The two-surface network reduces to the same series-resistance picture as Lesson 1 — surface resistances either side of a space resistance.
  • Radiation shields insert extra resistance pairs; \( N \) shields cut exchange by roughly \( 1/(N+1) \).
  • A linearized radiation coefficient \( h_r \) lets radiation sit alongside convection in one combined coefficient.
  • On the worked bare pipe, radiation carries about 48% of the total loss — a convection-only audit would miss half the heat, and polishing the surface changes the answer far more than insulation thickness would.

HTX 253 · Lesson 10 · Mass transfer analogy

Lesson 10Mass transfer analogy

Key points

  • Mass diffusion obeys Fick's law with exactly the mathematical form of Fourier's law, so every conduction result has a mass-transfer twin.
  • Convective mass transfer has its own coefficient \( h_m \) and its own Sherwood number, playing the roles of \( h \) and \( Nu \).
  • The Lewis number compares thermal and mass diffusivity; for water vapour in air \( Le\approx1 \), which is why the analogy is unusually exact for this one pair.
  • That gives the Lewis relation \( h/h_m\approx\rho c_p \) — a heat-transfer coefficient converts directly into a mass-transfer one.
  • Evaporation cools what is left behind because the latent heat has to come from somewhere; this is the mechanism behind cooling towers and wet-bulb temperature.
  • The worked evaporation estimate reuses this course's own Lesson 4 and Lesson 6 convection numbers — an explicit numerical callback, not a new correlation.

  • Psychrometrics (Thermodynamics II) — the sensible/latent energy split this lesson's evaporative cooling relies on was introduced there for cooling-load estimates.

HTX 253 · Lesson 11 · Thermal design mini-project

Lesson 11Thermal design mini-project

Key points

  • A thermal design brief is answered in two stages: size for the clean duty, then add margin for how the exchanger will actually age.
  • The sizing chain runs duty → LMTD → clean area, with \( U \) assembled from the film coefficients either side plus wall and fouling resistances.
  • A fouling margin can be spent two equivalent ways: extra installed area, or a derated design coefficient — \( A_{design}=A_{clean}(1+m) \) is the same statement as \( U_{design}=U_{clean}/(1+m) \).
  • The capstone's 20% margin lands on an exact \( U_{design}=375\ \mathrm{W/m^2K} \) by either route, which is the arithmetic check that the two views agree.
  • Critique a design against how it fails: fouling, maldistribution, vibration, and the approach temperature that quietly sets the area.
  • Every method in this course meets here — resistance networks, correlations, LMTD and NTU — applied to one piece of hardware.
