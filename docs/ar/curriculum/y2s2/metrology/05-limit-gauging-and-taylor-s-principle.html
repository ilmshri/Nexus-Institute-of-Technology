Limit gauging and Taylor's principle

Limits and fits recalled from the drawing course, then verified the shop-floor way: GO/NO-GO limit gauges designed with gauge-maker's tolerance. Taylor's principle — why the GO gauge must check the whole feature while the NO-GO checks point by point. Where attribute gauging beats measurement, and where it hides trouble.

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:

  • Gauge-maker's tolerance sits inside the work tolerance: who does that protect?

§1 · From a printed limit to a piece of steel

A drawing states a size and two limits. Engineering Drawing & CAD wrote them; this lesson checks them. Take the standard clearance fit \( \varnothing25\,\mathrm{H7/g6} \): the hole runs 25.000 to 25.021 mm, the shaft 24.980 to 24.993 mm, so the assembly clears by between 0.007 and 0.041 mm. Two limits per feature, four numbers, and every one of them has to be verified on parts arriving faster than anyone can measure them.

The oldest answer is still the fastest: build the limits into metal. A limit gauge is a piece of hardened steel made to one limit of the feature, and the inspection is whether it fits. For the hole above, a GO plug gauge is made at 25.000 — the smallest hole allowed — and must enter. A NO-GO plug gauge is made at 25.021, the largest hole allowed, and must not. A hole that takes the first and refuses the second is inside its limits, and no one has read a scale, interpolated a vernier, or written a number down.

Key idea — which limit each gauge embodies

\[ \text{GO} \to \text{MMC},\qquad \text{NO-GO} \to \text{LMC} \]

Maximum material condition is the limit at which the feature contains the most material: the smallest hole, the largest shaft. The GO gauge always embodies MMC, so for a shaft it is a ring gauge at 24.993 that must pass over, and the NO-GO ring at 24.980 must not.

What a limit gauge returns is an attribute — pass or fail — where a micrometer returns a variable, a number. That difference is the whole argument of §4, and it is worth holding in mind from the start: everything the gauge gains in speed it gains by throwing information away.

§2 · Taylor's principle, and the bore that measures perfectly

Making the two gauges to the two limits is not sufficient, and the rule that completes it is Taylor's principle, stated in 1905 and still the reason gauges are shaped the way they are.

Key rule — Taylor's principle

The GO gauge checks the maximum-material limit of all dimensions of the feature simultaneously, over its full length. The NO-GO gauge checks the least-material limit of one dimension at a time, at individual cross-sections.

So a GO plug is a full-length cylinder; a NO-GO plug is short, and ideally a two-point contact rather than a full circle.

The reason is not symmetry, it is function. The MMC limit exists to guarantee assembly, and assembly is a property of the whole feature at once — the mating shaft has to pass along the entire bore, not through each section separately. The LMC limit exists to guarantee that material, wall thickness, or contact area has not been lost, and those are local properties: a hole that is oversize anywhere is defective there, whatever the rest of it does.

Here is what that buys. A bore is bored 25.010 mm in diameter everywhere — comfortably inside 25.000 / 25.021 — but its axis is bowed by 0.008 mm over its length, because the part moved in the fixture. Every two-point measurement anyone can take reads 25.010 and passes. But the largest straight cylinder that can pass through a bore whose axis wanders laterally by \( \delta \) is smaller than the local diameter:

Key relation — what a bowed bore will actually accept

\[ d_{\max}=D-2\delta \]

The mating part must clear the wander on both sides, so the bow costs twice its own value in usable diameter. The same algebra covers a lobed or tapered bore: what assembles is the smallest enclosed straight cylinder, not the smallest measured diameter.

For this bore, \( d_{\max}=25.010-2(0.008)=24.994\ \mathrm{mm} \). The full-form GO plug at 25.000 will not enter, and it is right not to: a shaft at its own MMC of 24.993 would be left with 0.001 mm of clearance where the fit was designed for at least 0.007. A two-point instrument cannot see this at any number of sections, because the defect is not in any section — it is in the relationship between them. That is Taylor's principle earning its keep, and it is also why shortening a worn GO plug into a stubby pin, which shops do, quietly destroys the only check that was catching this class of fault.

The converse matters too. If the NO-GO plug were also full-form it would jam in a bore that was locally oversize but slightly bent, and the inspector would read that jam as a pass. Making it short and two-point is what lets it find a single oversize section anywhere along the length.

§3 · The gauge is a manufactured part too

A gauge made to 25.000 mm is a drawing instruction, not an object. The object is made to some tolerance of its own, called the gauge-maker's tolerance, and it wears in service. Both have to be paid for out of somebody's budget, and the convention decides whose.

Key relations — gauge tolerance and wear allowance

\[ t_g=0.10\,T,\qquad w=0.05\,T,\qquad \text{guaranteed band}=T-(t_g+w)-t_g=0.75\,T \]

Both are disposed inside the work tolerance zone \( T \). The wear allowance is applied to the GO gauge only, because the GO gauge enters every part made and the NO-GO gauge, by design, enters almost none.

Work the hole through. \( T=0.021 \) mm, so \( t_g=0.0021 \) and \( w=0.00105 \) mm. The GO plug is made 25.00105 to 25.00315: even at its smallest it is above the smallest permitted hole, so no GO plug can ever admit an undersize hole, and as it wears down it has 1.05 µm of life before it reaches 25.000 and must be withdrawn. The NO-GO plug is made 25.0189 to 25.0210: even at its largest it is no bigger than the largest permitted hole, so no NO-GO plug can ever fail to catch an oversize one.

Now read the diagram again. A hole is certain to be accepted only between 25.00315 and 25.0189 — 15.75 µm of the 21 the shop was given, exactly 75%. The other quarter has been transferred, silently, from the manufacturer to the customer. That is the answer to the question of who unilateral disposition protects: the customer, completely, and at the shop's expense. The alternative — straddling the gauge tolerance across the work limit, which some standards permit for general work — hands the tolerance back and admits parts up to \( t_g/2 \) outside the limit. Neither is wrong; they are different answers to who carries the risk, and the drawing or the contract should say which is in force.

Two consequences follow for practice. The gauge belongs on the calibration schedule of Lesson 3 like any other instrument, and its interval is set by how many parts pass through it, not by the calendar. And the wear allowance is a life budget, not a licence: it tells you the gauge has 1.05 µm to spend, which is worth knowing only if someone is measuring how much is left.

§4 · What gauging wins, and what it hides

Attribute gauging is not a poor relation of measurement. It is faster than any instrument, needs no interpretation, cannot be misread, works at the machine in the operator's hands, and makes 100% inspection affordable where measuring every part would not be. And for a fit, a full-form GO gauge is a more faithful test of function than a set of numbers, because it is a physical model of the mating part rather than an inference about it.

What it hides is everything between the limits. Consider a bore process on target, centred at 25.0105 mm with a standard deviation of 3.5 µm, so that the limits sit three standard deviations out. Its natural reject rate is 0.27%: about one part in 370. Let the mean drift by one standard deviation and the rate becomes 2.28%, one in 44. Let it drift by two and it is 15.87%, about one in six. The gauge reports 99.7%, then 97.7%, then 84.1% pass — and reports nothing at all in between, because a verdict has no units. The first clear signal that the process moved is scrap arriving in quantity, by which time everything made since the drift began is suspect.

A measurement would have said it sooner and said it quantitatively. Ten measured parts locate the mean to a standard error of \( 3.5/\sqrt{10}=1.11\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), so a 3.5 µm shift stands out from a sample of ten taken before a single part failed anything. The gauge cannot do this at any sample size, because it never produces a number to average.

There is a second, quieter hazard. A gauge shifts its own accept limit as it wears, and it does so in the direction of leniency — a worn GO plug enters holes it should refuse. Nothing in the gauging result reveals this; the parts simply keep passing. The instrument that is checking everything is the one thing nothing is checking, unless someone has scheduled it.

The working rule that comes out of all this is short. Gauge for the verdict, measure for the process. Use limit gauges where the question is whether this part ships, and measurements where the question is where the process is going — which, over a shift, is nearly always the more valuable question.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Design the plug gauge set, twice, in two unit systems

Problem. A hole is specified 25.000 / 25.021 mm. Using a gauge-maker's tolerance of 10% of the work tolerance and a wear allowance of 5%, both disposed inside the work tolerance zone, give the limits of the GO and NO-GO plug gauges. State the band of hole sizes that is certain to be accepted whichever gauges the shop is issued, and what fraction of the work tolerance that is. Then repeat the design for a hole specified 1.0000 / 1.0008 in.

Solution. (1) The work tolerance is \( T = 25.021-25.000 = 0.021\ \mathrm{mm} \), so \( t_g = 0.10T = 0.0021 \) mm and \( w = 0.05T = 0.00105 \) mm.

(2) The GO plug embodies MMC, the smallest hole, 25.000. Its wear allowance is placed into the hole's material side so the gauge starts oversize and wears toward the limit: low limit \( 25.000+0.00105 = 25.00105 \), high limit \( 25.00105+0.0021 = 25.00315 \). It is withdrawn from service when it wears back to 25.000.

(3) The NO-GO plug embodies LMC, the largest hole, 25.021, and takes no wear allowance because it does not enter parts. Its tolerance is disposed inward: 25.0189 to 25.0210.

(4) A hole is certainly accepted if it admits the largest GO plug and refuses the smallest NO-GO plug, so between 25.00315 and 25.0189: a band of \( 25.0189-25.00315 = 0.01575\ \mathrm{mm} = 15.75\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(5) \( 0.01575/0.021 = 0.75 \), so 75% of the work tolerance survives. The 25% lost is \( t_g + w \) at the GO end and \( t_g \) at the NO-GO end — \( 10+5+10 \) per cent.

(6) In inches: \( T = 0.0008 \) in, \( t_g = 0.00008 \), \( w = 0.00004 \). GO plug 1.00004 / 1.00012 in; NO-GO plug 1.00072 / 1.00080 in; guaranteed band \( 1.00072-1.00012 = 0.00060 \) in, again exactly 75%.

Answer: GO 25.00105 / 25.00315 mm, NO-GO 25.0189 / 25.0210 mm, guaranteed band 15.75 µm = 75% of \( T \); in inches GO 1.00004 / 1.00012, NO-GO 1.00072 / 1.00080, band 0.00060 in = 75%. Sanity check: the 75% is a property of the two percentages, not of the size or the unit — which is why the inch design lands on the same fraction. Note the direction of every disposition: each gauge limit was moved so that a gauge at the worst end of its own tolerance still cannot accept a bad hole. Reverse either one and the guarantee inverts — the gauge set would then be capable of passing a hole outside its limits, which is the one thing a limit gauge exists to prevent.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

The bore that measures perfectly and will not assemble

Problem. A bore for the \( \varnothing25\,\mathrm{H7/g6} \) fit measures 25.010 mm at every section, but its axis is bowed 0.008 mm off straight. Find the largest straight cylinder that will pass through it. Give the verdict from a full-form GO plug gauge at 25.000 and from a two-point bore micrometer, and find the clearance left on a shaft at its own MMC. Express the bow in inches and in shop "tenths".

Solution. (1) The mating cylinder must clear the wander on both sides, so \( d_{\max}=D-2\delta = 25.010-2(0.008) = 24.994\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(2) The GO plug is 25.000 mm and \( 24.994 < 25.000 \), so it will not enter: the bore is rejected.

(3) The bore micrometer reads 25.010 at every section, and \( 25.000 < 25.010 < 25.021 \), so on that evidence the bore passes — at any number of sections, because the defect is between sections, not in one.

(4) The shaft at MMC is its largest permitted size, 24.993 mm. Clearance is therefore \( 24.994-24.993 = 0.001\ \mathrm{mm} \), against a design minimum of \( 25.000-24.993 = 0.007\ \mathrm{mm} \) — a seventh of what the fit called for.

(5) \( 0.008\ \mathrm{mm}\div25.4 = 0.000315\ \mathrm{in} \), that is 3.15 tenths.

Answer: 24.994 mm effective; GO plug rejects, two-point micrometer passes; 0.001 mm clearance instead of 0.007; the bow is 0.000315 in (3.15 tenths). Sanity check: the two verdicts disagree and the gauge is the one that is right, which is the whole content of Taylor's principle — the GO gauge is a physical model of the mating shaft, and the micrometer is an inference about sections. Note the factor of two: a 0.008 mm bow costs 0.016 mm of usable diameter, so a bow well inside the tolerance can eat the fit entirely. Note also what a shortened, worn-down GO plug would have done here: a stubby pin passes a bent bore easily, and the shop would have lost the only check that saw the fault.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

How far can a process drift before the gauge notices?

Problem. A bore process is normally distributed with a standard deviation of 3.5 µm and is centred in the 25.000 / 25.021 mm tolerance. Find its reject rate when centred, after the mean drifts by one standard deviation, and after two. State what a GO/NO-GO gauge reports at each stage, and find the standard error of the mean of ten measured parts.

Solution. (1) The centre is \( (25.000+25.021)/2 = 25.0105 \) mm and the half-width is 0.0105 mm, which is \( 0.0105/0.0035 = 3.0 \) standard deviations. Centred, the reject rate is \( 2\,\Phi(-3) = 2(0.00135) = 0.0027 \), that is 0.27%, one part in 370.

(2) Drifted by \( 1\sigma \), the mean is 25.014. The upper tail is \( z=(25.021-25.014)/0.0035 = 2.0 \rightarrow 0.0228 \); the lower tail is at \( z=4.0 \), worth 0.00003. Total 2.28%, one in 44.

(3) Drifted by \( 2\sigma \), the mean is 25.0175. The upper tail is \( z=1.0 \rightarrow 0.1587 \); the lower is negligible. Total 15.87%, about one in six.

(4) The gauge reports 99.73%, then 97.72%, then 84.13% pass. It reports nothing else, because a pass/fail verdict carries no units and no position within the limits.

(5) \( \mathrm{SE} = \sigma/\sqrt{n} = 0.0035/\sqrt{10} = 0.00111\ \mathrm{mm} = 1.11\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

Answer: 0.27%, 2.28%, 15.87%; the gauge shows 99.73 / 97.72 / 84.13 per cent pass and nothing more; ten measured parts locate the mean to ±1.11 µm. Sanity check: the drift that eventually produced one reject in six was 7 µm, and ten measured parts would have resolved it to about 1 µm — visible long before any part failed. That is the trade the gauge makes: it is faster per part and blind between the limits, so it answers "does this ship?" and cannot answer "where is the process going?". Note also that the reject rate rose almost sixty-fold for a drift of two standard deviations, so the transition from "everything passes" to "a bin of scrap" is abrupt, which is exactly what makes gauge-only inspection feel like a sudden failure rather than a slow one.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

A GO plug gauge for a hole specified 25.000 / 25.021 mm is made nominally to

The GO gauge always embodies maximum material condition, and for a hole that is the smallest permitted size — most material left. It must enter every acceptable hole, so it has to be as small as the smallest one allowed. For a shaft, MMC is the largest size, so the GO ring gauge is made to the shaft's upper limit instead.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

Under Taylor's principle the NO-GO gauge should be

The least-material limit protects local properties — wall thickness, contact area — so a hole that is oversize anywhere is defective there. A short, two-point NO-GO finds a single oversize section. A full-form NO-GO would jam in a bore that was locally oversize but slightly bent, and the inspector would read the jam as a pass.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

The GO gauge must check the whole feature at once because the limit it embodies protects

MMC exists so that the mating part goes together, and the mating part has to pass along the entire feature rather than through each section separately. That is why the GO gauge is a full-form physical model of the mating part. LMC, by contrast, protects local material, which is why its gauge is local.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

A bore measures 25.010 mm at every section but its axis is bowed 0.008 mm. The largest straight cylinder that will pass through it is

\( d_{\max}=D-2\delta = 25.010-0.016 = 24.994\ \mathrm{mm} \). The mating part must clear the wander on both sides, so a bow costs twice its own value in usable diameter. The common slip is subtracting \( \delta \) once and getting 25.002, which would wrongly pass the bore against a 25.000 GO plug.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Gauge-maker's tolerance disposed entirely inside the work tolerance zone protects

Placing the gauge's own tolerance inside the work zone means that a gauge at the worst end of its tolerance still cannot admit a bad part — the guarantee is complete, and the shop pays for it in lost tolerance. Straddling the work limit instead hands the tolerance back and admits parts up to half the gauge tolerance outside the limit. Both are used; the drawing or the contract should say which.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A wear allowance is applied to

The GO gauge enters every part made and wears accordingly; the NO-GO gauge, by design, enters almost none. The allowance is placed so the GO gauge starts on the safe side and wears toward the work limit, at which point it is withdrawn. It is a service-life budget, not permission to keep using a gauge past the limit.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

With gauge tolerance at 10% of the work tolerance and a 5% wear allowance, both disposed inward, the band of sizes certain to be accepted is

The GO end consumes \( t_g + w = 15\% \) and the NO-GO end consumes \( t_g = 10\% \), so \( 25\% \) is lost and \( 0.75\,T \) survives. On the 21 µm hole that is 15.75 µm. The fraction depends only on the two percentages, so it is the same in millimetres or inches and at any nominal size.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A process gauged GO/NO-GO passes essentially everything for weeks, then rejects about one part in six overnight. The most likely explanation is

A two-standard-deviation drift takes the reject rate from 0.27% to 15.87% while the gauge reports 99.7% then 84.1% pass — and reports nothing in between. The abruptness is an artefact of attribute inspection, not of the process, which moved gradually. Ten measured parts would have located the mean to about 1 µm and shown the drift before anything failed.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the gauges that were replaced with a better instrument. A machine shop making \( \varnothing25\,\mathrm{H7} \) bores retired its plug gauge sets in favour of a digital bore micrometer, on the reasonable-sounding argument that a number beats a pass. Bore inspection records improved immediately: every part measured between 25.005 and 25.015, well inside 25.000 / 25.021, and the records showed it. Assembly rejects rose over the following weeks, with shafts that would not enter bores the paperwork said were correct.

The bores were bowed. A part that shifts in the fixture during boring produces an axis that wanders while the local diameter stays constant, and a two-point instrument is blind to it by construction — it measures across a section and the defect is between sections. A bore reading 25.010 everywhere with a 0.008 mm bow admits a straight cylinder of only \( 25.010-2(0.008)=24.994\ \mathrm{mm} \). Against a shaft at its MMC of 24.993 that leaves 0.001 mm where the fit was designed for at least 0.007, so the parts assembled by force or not at all. The full-form GO plug had been rejecting these bores all along, and its verdict was discarded as less informative than a number.

Two practices follow. The GO gauge is not a crude substitute for measurement — it is a physical model of the mating part, and for anything that has to assemble it tests a property no set of sectional measurements contains. Where variable data is genuinely wanted for process control, the answer is to measure and gauge, not to swap one for the other. And when an inspection change is followed by more downstream failures, the change is the first suspect, however much better the new records look: the records improved here precisely because the new method could not see the defect.
