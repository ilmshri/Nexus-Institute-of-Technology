Surface texture measurement

المحاضرة

§1 · What is on the surface, and what the instrument does to it

Drag a fine stylus across a machined surface and it traces a profile \( z(x) \). That profile is not one thing. It is a superposition of three, separated by wavelength: form, the intended shape and its long-wavelength errors; waviness, the medium-wavelength undulation left by machine vibration, spindle error or workpiece deflection; and roughness, the short-wavelength marks of the cutting or abrasive process itself.

Nothing in the raw trace announces which is which, so roughness is defined by the filter that extracts it. Two wavelengths do the separating. The long cutoff \( \lambda_c \) divides roughness from waviness; the short cutoff \( \lambda_s \) divides roughness from noise. One sampling length equals \( \lambda_c \), and the standard evaluation length is five of them, so a stated roughness is an average over five sampling lengths.

The instrument filters mechanically before it filters electronically. A stylus tip is a cone ending in a radius, typically 2 µm, and it cannot enter a valley sharper than itself — it bridges across, and the profile it records is smoother than the surface. For a sinusoidal profile of amplitude \( a \) and wavelength \( \lambda \), the radius of curvature at the valley floor is \( \lambda^2/4\pi^2a \), so the tip follows only while that exceeds the tip radius \( r \).

Key relation — the shortest wavelength a stylus can follow

\[ \lambda_{\min}=2\pi\sqrt{a\,r} \]

With \( a=0.5\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) and a 2 µm tip, \( \lambda_{\min}=6.28\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). A 10 µm feature is traced faithfully; a 4 µm one is bridged and under-reported.

This is worth stating plainly because it is the honest version of a claim instruments make about themselves: a 0.01 µm vertical resolution says nothing about whether the tip reached the bottom of the valley it was resolving. Optical instruments trade this problem for different ones — they have no tip radius, but steep flanks scatter light away and are reported as missing data.

§2 · Ra, Rz, Rq, and what each of them discards

From the filtered profile, taken about its mean line, the standard parameters follow.

Key relations — the amplitude parameters

\[ R_a=\frac{1}{L}\int_0^L|z|\,dx,\qquad R_q=\sqrt{\frac{1}{L}\int_0^L z^2\,dx},\qquad R_z=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\bigl(z_{p,i}-z_{v,i}\bigr) \]

\( R_a \) is the mean magnitude of the deviation, \( R_q \) its root mean square, and \( R_z \) the mean of the five largest peak-to-valley heights, one from each sampling length. \( R_t \) is the single largest peak-to-valley height over the whole evaluation length.

\( R_a \) dominates drawings because it is stable: it averages over the whole trace, so a stray scratch barely moves it, and repeat measurements agree. That stability is also its defect. It is blind to shape, and shape is what most surfaces are for.

The diagram makes the point with two profiles of identical \( R_a=0.80\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). The first is a sinusoid, which needs amplitude \( a=\pi R_a/2=1.257\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) and so stands 2.51 µm crest to trough. The second is plateau-honed: flat over 90% of its length with narrow valleys of depth \( d \). Its mean line sits \( 0.1d \) below the plateau, so \( R_a=0.9(0.1d)+0.1(0.9d)=0.18d \), giving \( d=4.44\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) — 1.77 times the total height of the sinusoid, at the same \( R_a \).

The cheapest discriminator is the ratio \( R_q/R_a \), because \( R_q \) weights large deviations by their square and \( R_a \) does not. A sinusoid gives \( \pi/2\sqrt2=1.111 \); a random surface with a normal height distribution gives \( \sqrt{\pi/2}=1.253 \); the plateau profile above gives 1.667. A measured ratio well above 1.25 says the height distribution has a tail — deep valleys, or high peaks, and the sign of the skew says which. When that distinction actually governs function, the standard offers parameters built for it (\( R_k \), \( R_{pk} \), \( R_{vk} \), from the bearing-area curve), and a drawing that cares should call for them rather than tightening \( R_a \) and hoping.

§3 · Why the cutoff changes the answer

Because roughness is whatever survives the filter, changing \( \lambda_c \) changes the measured value on an unchanged surface. This is not instrument error. It is the definition working.

Take a profile that is the sum of two sinusoids: a machining roughness of 0.5 µm amplitude at 0.1 mm wavelength, and a waviness of 2.0 µm amplitude at 1.6 mm. Measured with \( \lambda_c=0.8\ \mathrm{mm} \), the 1.6 mm component is longer than the cutoff and is removed as waviness, leaving \( R_a=2(0.5)/\pi=0.318\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). Measured with \( \lambda_c=2.5\ \mathrm{mm} \), the 1.6 mm component is shorter than the cutoff, survives as roughness, and \( R_a=1.294\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). The same surface, the same instrument, the same operator: 4.06 times the answer.

Key rule — a roughness figure without its cutoff is not a specification

\[ R_a \text{ alone is incomplete};\qquad R_a\ \text{with}\ \lambda_c\ \text{is a number} \]

The standard cutoff is chosen from the roughness itself — 0.8 mm for \( R_a \) between 0.1 and 2 µm, 2.5 mm above that, 0.25 mm below − so a first trace is used to pick the cutoff for the second.

That selection rule sounds circular and is not: it is a two-pass procedure, and it exists so that the filter is matched to the process rather than to the inspector. The practical consequences are direct. A supplier and a customer measuring the same part with different cutoffs will disagree by a factor, and neither will be wrong. A drawing calling for \( R_a\ 0.8 \) with no cutoff has under-specified the part. And a surface whose real content sits near the cutoff — a milled face with feed marks at roughly \( \lambda_c \) — is the case where the disagreement is worst, because the filter is cutting exactly where the energy is.

The same logic governs the short end. \( \lambda_s \) exists so that electrical noise and the stylus's own tip effects are not reported as roughness, and it is the reason a measured profile should never be read as the true surface at scales near the tip radius.

§4 · Matching a finish to what the surface has to do

A finish specification is a functional requirement, not a quality aspiration, and smoother is frequently wrong. Four cases cover most of what an engineer specifies.

A face that has to seal against a soft gasket wants texture, not polish. A spiral-wound or compressed-fibre gasket seats by biting into the flange face, and a lapped flange gives it nothing to grip; the usual call is a controlled serrated or phonographic finish in the \( R_a\ 3.2\text{--}6.3\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) range. A face that has to seal against a rigid counterface — a mechanical seal, a metal-to-metal joint — is the opposite: the leak path is the roughness itself, so it is lapped to \( R_a\ 0.1\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) or better, and flatness is checked optically rather than by stylus. A surface that is going to be painted or bonded wants tooth for the adhesive to key into, so a blasted or coarsely machined \( R_a\ 3.2\text{--}12.5\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) is specified deliberately. A lubricated sliding surface is the interesting one, because it wants both: a smooth load-carrying plateau and valleys deep enough to hold oil — which is the plateau-honed profile of §2, and exactly what \( R_a \) alone cannot specify.

Key relation — the film-thickness ratio

\[ \Lambda=\frac{h_{\min}}{\sqrt{R_{q,1}^2+R_{q,2}^2}} \]

Roughnesses of two contacting surfaces combine in quadrature. \( \Lambda>3 \) is full-film separation, \( 1<\Lambda<3 \) is mixed lubrication with intermittent asperity contact, and \( \Lambda<1 \) is boundary contact. The film thickness \( h_{\min} \) comes from the bearing analysis; the roughnesses come from this lesson.

A journal at \( R_q=0.25\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) running in a shell at \( R_q=0.40 \) has a composite roughness of \( \sqrt{0.25^2+0.40^2}=0.472\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). With a minimum film of 1.8 µm, \( \Lambda=3.82 \) — the surfaces never touch. Refinish the journal to \( R_q=0.80\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) and the composite becomes 0.894, so \( \Lambda=2.01 \): the film is unchanged, the clearance is unchanged, the drawing dimensions are unchanged, and the bearing is now in mixed lubrication, where it will wear. The only thing that moved was a finish, and the number that governed it was \( R_q \) rather than \( R_a \).

Two habits follow from all of this. State the cutoff with the roughness, always, or the specification does not close. And say what the surface is for — a finish specified for a seal face, a gasket seat and a paint key are three different requirements that a single tightened \( R_a \) serves badly.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Two surfaces, one Ra

Problem. Surface A is sinusoidal with \( R_a=0.80\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). Surface B is plateau-honed: flat over 90% of its length, with valleys of depth \( d \) over the remaining 10%, and it too measures \( R_a=0.80\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). Find the total height \( R_t \) of each and the ratio \( R_q/R_a \) of each, and say which parameter would have distinguished them on a drawing.

Solution. (1) For a sinusoid of amplitude \( a \), \( R_a = 2a/\pi \), so \( a = \pi(0.80)/2 = 1.257\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) and \( R_t = 2a = 2.513\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(2) \( R_q = a/\sqrt2 = 0.889\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), so \( R_q/R_a = \pi/(2\sqrt2) = 1.111 \).

(3) For surface B the mean line must pass through the profile's centroid. With the plateau at height 0 and the valleys at \( -d \), the mean sits at \( -0.1d \), so the plateau is \( +0.1d \) above it and the valley floor \( -0.9d \) below.

(4) \( R_a = 0.9(0.1d) + 0.1(0.9d) = 0.18d \). Setting this to 0.80 gives \( d = 4.444\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), which is \( R_t \) — 1.77 times surface A's.

(5) \( R_q^2 = 0.9(0.1d)^2 + 0.1(0.9d)^2 = 0.09d^2 \), so \( R_q = 0.3d = 1.333\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) and \( R_q/R_a = 0.3d/0.18d = 1.667 \).

(6) \( R_a \) is identical, so it distinguishes nothing. \( R_t \) or \( R_z \) separates them by 1.77 times, and \( R_q/R_a \) separates them as 1.111 against 1.667.

Answer: A: \( R_t=2.51\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), \( R_q/R_a=1.111 \). B: \( R_t=4.44\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), \( R_q/R_a=1.667 \). \( R_a \) cannot tell them apart; a peak-to-valley parameter or the \( R_q/R_a \) ratio can. Sanity check: a random surface with a normal height distribution gives \( R_q/R_a=\sqrt{\pi/2}=1.253 \), so both results sit either side of that benchmark in the right direction — the sinusoid has no tail and reads low, the valley-dominated profile has a long one and reads high. Note the practical inversion: surface B is the rougher by every extreme measure and is the better bearing surface, because it carries load on 90% of its area and stores oil in the rest.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

The same surface, two cutoffs

Problem. A profile is the sum of two sinusoids: 0.5 µm amplitude at 0.1 mm wavelength, and 2.0 µm amplitude at 1.6 mm. Give \( R_q \) as measured with \( \lambda_c=0.8\ \mathrm{mm} \) and with \( \lambda_c=2.5\ \mathrm{mm} \), and the ratio between them. State which reading a drawing calling for "\( R_a\ 0.4 \)" would have been judged against.

Solution. (1) A long cutoff \( \lambda_c \) passes wavelengths shorter than itself as roughness and rejects longer ones as waviness.

(2) At \( \lambda_c=0.8 \) mm: the 0.1 mm component passes, the 1.6 mm component is rejected because \( 1.6 > 0.8 \). Only the roughness survives, and for a single sinusoid \( R_q = a/\sqrt2 = 0.5/\sqrt2 = 0.354\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(3) At \( \lambda_c=2.5 \) mm: both components pass, since \( 1.6 < 2.5 \). Sinusoids of different wavelength are orthogonal over a common length, so their mean squares add: \( R_q = \sqrt{a_1^2/2 + a_2^2/2} = \sqrt{0.125+2.000} = \sqrt{2.125} = 1.458\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(4) The ratio is \( 1.458/0.354 = 4.12 \). (The corresponding \( R_a \) values are 0.318 and 1.294 µm, a factor of 4.06 — the same story.)

(5) The 0.8 mm cutoff is the standard choice for \( R_a \) between 0.1 and 2 µm, so a drawing calling for \( R_a\ 0.4 \) with no cutoff stated would be judged against 0.318 and the part would pass — while an inspector who chose 2.5 mm would read 1.294 and reject it.

Answer: \( R_q = 0.354\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) at \( \lambda_c=0.8 \) mm and \( 1.458\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) at 2.5 mm, a factor of 4.12; the part passes on one cutoff and fails on the other. Sanity check: nothing about the surface changed, so this is the definition of roughness showing itself, not a measurement error — roughness is what survives the filter. Note that the waviness carries almost all the height here \( (2.0 \) against \( 0.5\ \mu\mathrm{m}) \), which is why including it swamps the result; a surface whose waviness were small would show little cutoff sensitivity. This is the whole reason a specification without \( \lambda_c \) is incomplete.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

A refinished journal, and a US customary check

Problem. A journal finished to \( R_q=0.25\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) runs in a shell at \( R_q=0.40\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) with a minimum oil film of 1.8 µm. Find the film-thickness ratio and classify the lubrication regime. The journal is later reground to \( R_q=0.80\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) with everything else unchanged; repeat. Express both journal finishes in micro-inches.

Solution. (1) The two roughnesses combine in quadrature: \( \sigma = \sqrt{0.25^2+0.40^2} = \sqrt{0.0625+0.1600} = \sqrt{0.2225} = 0.472\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(2) \( \Lambda = h_{\min}/\sigma = 1.8/0.472 = 3.82 \). Since \( \Lambda > 3 \), the surfaces are fully separated by the film and do not touch.

(3) After regrinding, \( \sigma = \sqrt{0.80^2+0.40^2} = \sqrt{0.80} = 0.894\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(4) \( \Lambda = 1.8/0.894 = 2.01 \), which is between 1 and 3: mixed lubrication, with asperities touching through the film.

(5) \( 0.25\ \mu\mathrm{m} = 250/25.4 = 9.8\ \mu\mathrm{in} \) and \( 0.80\ \mu\mathrm{m} = 800/25.4 = 31.5\ \mu\mathrm{in} \).

Answer: \( \Lambda = 3.82 \) (full film) before, \( \Lambda = 2.01 \) (mixed) after; the journal went from 9.8 to 31.5 micro-inches. Sanity check: the clearance, the oil, the load and every dimension on the drawing were unchanged — only a finish moved, and the bearing crossed out of full-film operation into a regime where it wears. That is why a journal drawing states a finish at all, and why the parameter it should state is \( R_q \): the quadrature sum is a sum of squares, so it is \( R_q \) that combines correctly and \( R_a \) that does not. Note the rougher surface dominates the composite — at 0.80 against 0.40, the shell contributes only a tenth of \( \sigma^2 \), so polishing the shell would have bought almost nothing.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

Roughness is separated from waviness by

\( \lambda_c \) is the wavelength at which the filter divides them: shorter content is reported as roughness, longer as waviness. One sampling length equals \( \lambda_c \) and the evaluation length is five of them. The tip radius and \( \lambda_s \) act at the short end, keeping noise and tip artefacts out.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

The same surface measured at \( \lambda_c = 0.8 \) mm and at \( \lambda_c = 2.5 \) mm gives \( R_a \) values differing by a factor of four. This means

Both are correct measurements of different quantities, because roughness is defined as what survives the filter. Here a 1.6 mm undulation is waviness at the 0.8 mm cutoff and roughness at the 2.5 mm cutoff. It is why a roughness value quoted without its cutoff is not a specification, and why supplier and customer can disagree by a factor with neither at fault.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

Two surfaces both measure \( R_a = 0.80\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). One is sinusoidal, the other plateau-honed with deep valleys. Their \( R_q/R_a \) ratios are

\( R_q \) weights deviations by their square, so a profile with deep valleys raises \( R_q \) without raising \( R_a \) proportionately: 1.111 for the sinusoid, 1.667 for the plateau profile. A random surface with a normal height distribution sits at \( \sqrt{\pi/2}=1.253 \), so the ratio is a quick test for whether the height distribution has a tail.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

A 2 µm stylus tip traversing a profile of 0.5 µm amplitude will bridge rather than follow features shorter than about

\( \lambda_{\min}=2\pi\sqrt{ar}=2\pi\sqrt{(0.5)(2)}=6.28\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). Below that the valley floor is sharper than the tip and the recorded profile is smoother than the real surface. A quoted vertical resolution of 0.01 µm says nothing about this — the limit is geometric, not electrical.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A flange face that must seal against a spiral-wound gasket should be specified

A soft gasket seals by biting into the face, so it needs something to grip; a lapped flange gives it nothing and can leak. The controlled serrated finish is a functional requirement in its own right. The opposite case — a rigid counterface such as a mechanical seal, where the roughness is the leak path — is lapped to 0.1 µm or better.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Two surfaces with \( R_q \) of 0.25 and 0.40 µm have a composite roughness of

They combine in quadrature: \( \sqrt{0.25^2+0.40^2}=\sqrt{0.2225}=0.472\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), not by addition. Because it is a sum of squares, the rougher surface dominates — and because it is a sum of squares, the parameter that combines correctly is \( R_q \) rather than \( R_a \).

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A bearing operating at \( \Lambda = 2.0 \) is in

\( \Lambda>3 \) is full film, \( 1<\Lambda<3 \) is mixed, \( \Lambda<1 \) is boundary. At 2.0 the film carries most of the load but asperities touch, and the surface wears. In the worked example the bearing arrived there purely by being refinished coarser — the film, clearance and load were unchanged.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

The main reason \( R_a \) dominates drawings despite being blind to profile shape is

Averaging magnitudes over the evaluation length makes \( R_a \) stable and repeatable, which is a real virtue in a shop specification. The cost is that it discards shape entirely. Every instrument computes \( R_q \) and \( R_z \) as well, \( R_t \) is larger by construction, and \( R_a \) needs a cutoff exactly like the others.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the polished journal that wore out. A pump shaft came back from an overhaul with its journals reground and then polished, on the instruction "improve the finish". The shop measured them, recorded \( R_a\ 0.35\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) against a drawing calling for 0.4 maximum, and released the shaft. The bearings wore visibly within a few hundred hours.

The measurement was in specification and the surface was wrong. Polishing had removed the honing valleys and left a smooth, near-sinusoidal profile: same \( R_a \), \( R_q \) risen from 0.25 to about 0.4 µm as the profile lost its plateau character, and — the part that mattered — no valleys left to hold oil where the film thins at start-up and shutdown. On top of that the drawing specified only \( R_a \) with no cutoff and no shape parameter, so the finish that had worked for years was never actually specified; it had been produced by the original honing process and preserved by habit.

Two practices follow. Where the surface has a job beyond looking finished, specify the parameter that governs that job — \( R_q \) for a lubricated contact, a peak-to-valley or bearing-area parameter where oil retention or contact area is the point — and state the cutoff so the number closes. And treat "improve the finish" as an unsafe instruction on a functional surface: it is a direction without a target, and on a plateau-honed journal the improvement runs the wrong way.
