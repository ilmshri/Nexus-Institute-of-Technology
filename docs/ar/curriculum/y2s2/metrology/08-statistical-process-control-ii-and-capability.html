المحاضرة

§1 · When the data are counts, not measurements

Lesson 7's charts plot a measurement. Plenty of characteristics do not produce one — a part passes a limit gauge or it does not, a weld shows defects or it does not — and those need charts built on the distribution the counting itself follows.

Key relations — the attribute charts

\[ \bar p\pm3\sqrt{\frac{\bar p(1-\bar p)}{n}}\qquad\text{(fraction nonconforming, binomial)} \]

\[ \bar c\pm3\sqrt{\bar c}\qquad\text{(defects per unit, Poisson)} \]

The \( p \) chart counts nonconforming units out of \( n \); the \( c \) chart counts defects, of which one unit may carry several. Their limits are the three-sigma convention applied to the right distribution, not a new idea.

Take \( \bar p=0.03 \) with samples of 200. The standard error is \( \sqrt{0.03(0.97)/200}=0.01206 \), so the upper limit is \( 0.03+0.0362=0.0662 \) and the lower limit computes to \( -0.0062 \) — negative, so it is truncated to zero. That truncation is not a formality. It means the chart cannot detect an improvement: no sample, however clean, will ever fall below a limit of zero. The chart is one-sided by arithmetic.

Getting a usable lower limit means making \( \bar p>3\sqrt{\bar p(1-\bar p)/n} \), which rearranges to \( n>9(1-\bar p)/\bar p \) — here \( 9(0.97)/0.03=291 \), so samples of 292 or more. At \( \bar p=0.003 \) it would be 2,991. This is the same point Lesson 5 made about limit gauges, in a new form: a pass/fail observation carries far less information than a measurement, so attribute charts need very large samples to say anything a variables chart says from five parts. Where a measurement is available, chart the measurement.

§2 · \( C_p \): could the process fit?

Stability, which Lesson 7 established, is not the same question as adequacy. A process can be perfectly stable and still too variable for the tolerance it has been given. The index that asks this is \( C_p \).

Key relation — potential capability

\[ C_p=\frac{\mathrm{USL}-\mathrm{LSL}}{6\hat\sigma},\qquad \hat\sigma=\frac{\bar R}{d_2} \]

A ratio of two widths: what is allowed, over what the process naturally produces. \( C_p=1 \) means the natural spread exactly fills the tolerance, which even perfectly centred leaves 0.27% outside — the same three-sigma tail as a control limit.

The bore process of Lesson 7 had \( \hat\sigma=1.83\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) against a 21 µm tolerance, so \( C_p=21/(6\times1.827)=1.92 \). Its spread occupies just over half the tolerance. Note carefully what has and has not been established by that number: \( C_p \) contains no information at all about where the process is sitting. It is a statement about the machine, not about the parts. For that reason it is sometimes called potential capability — what the process could achieve if someone centred it.

§3 · \( C_{pk} \): is it fitting?

The index that accounts for position takes the worse of the two sides.

Key relations — actual capability and the centring loss

\[ C_{pk}=\min\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{USL}-\mu}{3\hat\sigma},\ \frac{\mu-\mathrm{LSL}}{3\hat\sigma}\right),\qquad k=1-\frac{C_{pk}}{C_p} \]

\( C_{pk}\le C_p \) always, with equality only when the process is centred. The gap between them is pure centring: \( k \) is the fraction of the half-tolerance the mean has given away.

The diagram's process is the standard diagnostic case, and it answers the question directly. \( C_p=1.80 \) and \( C_{pk}=0.70 \) give \( k=1-0.70/1.80=0.611 \). Since \( C_p=1.80 \) means the tolerance is \( 10.8\hat\sigma \) wide, its half-width is \( 5.4\hat\sigma \), and the mean is \( 0.611\times5.4=3.3\hat\sigma \) off centre. Check it against the definition: the room remaining above is \( 5.4-3.3=2.1\hat\sigma \), and \( 2.1/3=0.70 \). The process is not too variable; it is badly aimed, and the fix is a setting, not a machine.

The defect rate follows from the near side alone. \( z=3C_{pk}=2.1 \), so the upper tail is \( 1-\Phi(2.1)=0.01786 \), about 17,900 parts per million. The lower tail sits \( 8.7\hat\sigma \) away and contributes nothing measurable. Every one of those 17,900 is recoverable by moving the mean.

Which answers the other standing question — what does \( C_{pk}=1.33 \) actually promise? \( z=3(1.33)=3.99 \), so the near tail is \( 1-\Phi(3.99)=3.30\times10^{-5} \): 33 parts per million on the near side. The familiar figure of 63 ppm is the two-sided total, and it is only correct if the process is centred, that is if \( C_p=C_{pk}=1.33 \). A process with \( C_p=3 \) and \( C_{pk}=1.33 \) has 33 ppm and nothing on the far side; a centred one has 33 on each. \( C_{pk} \) alone bounds the near tail and says nothing about the total, which is exactly why both indices are quoted together and neither is quoted alone.

The same arithmetic explains the most-quoted number in quality. A process with \( C_p=2 \) whose mean is allowed to wander \( 1.5\hat\sigma \) has \( C_{pk}=1.5 \), so \( z=4.5 \) and the tail is \( 3.40\times10^{-6} \) — 3.4 parts per million. The 1.5\( \sigma \) drift is a convention about long-term behaviour, not a law, and the 3.4 ppm is a consequence of assuming it.

§4 · Four ways a capability index lies

Capability indices are the most abused numbers in manufacturing, and every abuse is avoidable with one question each.

The process is not stable. \( \hat\sigma=\bar R/d_2 \) estimates the spread of a distribution, which presupposes there is one. On an out-of-control process the parameter being estimated changes while you estimate it, and the resulting index describes nothing. Stability first, capability second: that ordering is not a preference, it is a precondition, and computing \( C_{pk} \) before checking the charts is the single commonest error in the field.

The measuring system is inside the number. Independent variances add, so what a study observes is \( \sigma^2_{\text{obs}}=\sigma^2_{\text{process}}+\sigma^2_{\text{measurement}} \). A process at \( \sigma=1.5\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) measured with a gauge whose own spread is 1.0 µm reads \( \sqrt{1.5^2+1.0^2}=1.80\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), taking \( C_p \) from 2.33 to 1.94 — a 17% loss produced entirely by the instrument. How much of an observed spread belongs to the measuring system, and how to find out, is taken up later in this course.

The estimate is far softer than it looks. \( C_{pk} \) is computed from a sample and inherits its uncertainty. The standard large-sample interval is

Key relation — the confidence interval on \( C_{pk} \)

\[ \hat C_{pk}\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{9n}+\frac{\hat C_{pk}^2}{2(n-1)}} \]

At \( \hat C_{pk}=1.33 \) from 30 parts the 95% interval is 0.97 to 1.69. From 100 parts it is 1.13 to 1.53. A single quoted index is a point estimate, and a small one is barely evidence.

A supplier reporting "\( C_{pk}=1.33 \), \( n=30 \)" has demonstrated that the true value is somewhere between not-capable and very capable. That is worth knowing before a study is accepted, and it is why capability studies specify a sample size rather than leaving it to whoever runs them.

The distribution is not normal. Every parts-per-million figure above comes from the normal tail. A skewed process — a form or position characteristic bounded at zero, a plated thickness — can show a respectable \( C_{pk} \) and a defect rate several times what the index implies, because the real tail is heavier than the model's. The index is still useful as a comparative measure; the ppm conversion is not.

Used properly, the study is a gate rather than a report. Before production starts, run the process, prove it is stable, then show the index clears the threshold the contract sets — commonly 1.33, and 1.67 where failure is expensive or unsafe. Doing it in that order is what makes capability a decision instead of an autopsy.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Read a process from its two indices

Problem. A study reports \( C_p=1.80 \) and \( C_{pk}=0.70 \). Find the centring loss \( k \), how far the mean sits from the centre of the tolerance in process standard deviations, and the fraction of production outside the specification. Say what should be done, and what \( C_{pk} \) would become if it were done.

Solution. (1) \( k=1-C_{pk}/C_p=1-0.70/1.80=0.611 \).

(2) \( C_p=1.80 \) means the tolerance is \( 6(1.80)=10.8\hat\sigma \) wide, so its half-width is \( 5.4\hat\sigma \). The mean is displaced by \( k \) of that: \( 0.611\times5.4=3.3\hat\sigma \).

(3) Check against the definition: the room left on the near side is \( 5.4-3.3=2.1\hat\sigma \), and \( 2.1/3=0.70=C_{pk} \). Consistent.

(4) Near tail: \( z=3C_{pk}=2.1 \), so \( 1-\Phi(2.1)=0.01786 \). Far tail: \( z=5.4+3.3=8.7 \), which contributes about \( 10^{-18} \). Total 1.79%, or 17,900 ppm.

(5) The spread is fine and the aim is not, so the action is to move the mean to the centre of the tolerance — a setting change, not a machine problem. Centred, \( C_{pk}=C_p=1.80 \), and the defect rate falls to \( 2(1-\Phi(5.4))=6.7\times10^{-8} \), about 0.07 ppm.

Answer: \( k=0.611 \), the mean is \( 3.3\hat\sigma \) high, 1.79% (17,900 ppm) is outside; recentring alone takes \( C_{pk} \) to 1.80 and the defect rate to well under one ppm. Sanity check: the two indices together carry information neither has alone — \( C_p \) says the machine is capable, \( C_{pk} \) says the parts are not, and only the pair identifies centring as the cause. Note the leverage: a quarter of a million defective parts per fifteen million disappear for the cost of a tool offset, whereas improving \( C_p \) from 1.80 would have meant a new process. This is why a report quoting \( C_{pk} \) alone is incomplete.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

A capability study in inches, and how much to believe it

Problem. A shaft is specified 1.2490 / 1.2510 in. A stable process gives \( \mu=1.2504 \) in and \( \hat\sigma=0.00025 \) in. Find \( C_p \), both one-sided indices, \( C_{pk} \), and the defect rate in parts per million. Then, given that the study used 30 parts, give the 95% confidence interval on the reported \( C_{pk} \) and say what it means for an acceptance threshold of 1.33.

Solution. (1) \( C_p=(1.2510-1.2490)/(6\times0.00025)=0.0020/0.0015=1.333 \).

(2) Upper side: \( (1.2510-1.2504)/(3\times0.00025)=0.0006/0.00075=0.800 \).

(3) Lower side: \( (1.2504-1.2490)/0.00075=0.0014/0.00075=1.867 \).

(4) \( C_{pk}=\min(0.800,1.867)=0.800 \).

(5) Upper tail \( z=3(0.800)=2.4 \), giving \( 1-\Phi(2.4)=0.008198 \); lower tail \( z=5.6 \), giving \( 1.1\times10^{-8} \). Total 8,198 ppm, about 0.82%.

(6) With \( n=30 \) and \( \hat C_{pk}=0.800 \): \( \sqrt{1/(9\times30)+0.64/58}=\sqrt{0.003704+0.011034}=0.1214 \), so the interval is \( 0.800\pm1.96(0.1214)=0.562 \) to 1.038.

(7) The whole interval lies below 1.33, so the process fails the threshold and the sample size is not the reason — even the optimistic end of the interval is short.

Answer: \( C_p=1.333 \), \( C_{pu}=0.800 \), \( C_{pl}=1.867 \), \( C_{pk}=0.800 \), 8,198 ppm; the 95% interval is 0.56 to 1.04, entirely below the 1.33 gate. Sanity check: \( C_p \) and \( C_{pk} \) differ by a factor of 1.67, and the two one-sided indices show immediately why — the mean sits 0.0004 in above the centre of a 0.0020 in band, so 60% of the upper half-tolerance is gone. Note that the arithmetic never touched a unit conversion: the indices are ratios of lengths and are the same number in inches or millimetres. Note also what the interval does and does not settle — here it rules the process out, whereas a reported 1.33 from the same 30 parts would have ranged from 0.97 to 1.69 and settled nothing.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Two things that are not the process

Problem. A tolerance of 21 µm is checked on a process whose true standard deviation is 1.5 µm, using a gauge whose own measurement standard deviation is 1.0 µm. Find the observed \( C_p \), the true \( C_p \), and the fraction of the index lost to the instrument. Separately, a \( p \) chart runs at \( \bar p=0.03 \) with samples of 200: give its limits and state what the chart cannot do, and find the smallest sample size that fixes it.

Solution. (1) Independent variances add: \( \sigma_{\text{obs}}=\sqrt{1.5^2+1.0^2}=\sqrt{3.25}=1.803\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(2) \( C_{p,\text{obs}}=21/(6\times1.803)=1.941 \).

(3) \( C_{p,\text{true}}=21/(6\times1.5)=2.333 \).

(4) Loss \( =1-1.941/2.333=0.168 \), so 17% of the index was consumed by the measuring system, not by the process.

(5) \( p \) chart: \( \sqrt{0.03(0.97)/200}=0.01206 \), so \( \mathrm{UCL}=0.03+0.0362=0.0662 \) and the lower limit computes to \( -0.0062 \), which is truncated to 0.

(6) With a lower limit of zero no sample can ever fall below it, so the chart cannot detect an improvement — only a deterioration.

(7) A positive lower limit needs \( \bar p>3\sqrt{\bar p(1-\bar p)/n} \), that is \( n>9(1-\bar p)/\bar p=9(0.97)/0.03=291 \). Samples of 292 or more.

Answer: observed \( C_p=1.94 \) against a true 2.33, a 17% loss to the gauge; the \( p \) chart runs 0 to 0.0662 and is blind to improvement below \( n=292 \). Sanity check: both results are the same lesson in different clothing — the number a study reports is a property of the measurement scheme as well as of the process. The gauge cost more index than most process improvements would have gained, and the \( p \) chart's blindness is arithmetic rather than bad luck. Note that a variables chart on the same characteristic would have signalled from five parts, which is the argument for measuring wherever a measurement exists.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A supplier reports \( C_{pk}=1.33 \) from a study of 30 parts. The 95% confidence interval is roughly

\( \hat C_{pk}\pm1.96\sqrt{1/(9n)+\hat C_{pk}^2/(2(n-1))} \) gives \( 1.33\pm0.36 \). The reported value is a point estimate from a sample and inherits its uncertainty; from 30 parts it spans not-capable to very capable. From 100 parts it is still 1.13 to 1.53, which is why studies specify a sample size.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

The widely quoted "3.4 parts per million" follows from

\( C_p=2 \) with the mean allowed to wander \( 1.5\hat\sigma \) leaves \( C_{pk}=1.5 \), so \( z=4.5 \) and the tail is \( 3.40\times10^{-6} \). The \( 1.5\sigma \) shift is a convention about long-term behaviour rather than a law, so the 3.4 ppm is a consequence of that assumption and not a property of any particular process.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the capability study that was run on the wrong week. A supplier had to demonstrate \( C_{pk}\ge1.33 \) on a bore before a production order was released. The study was run on 30 consecutive parts from a single setup, returned \( C_{pk}=1.38 \), and the order was placed. Rejects in the first production month ran near 2%.

Three things had gone wrong, and each was checkable in advance. No control chart accompanied the study, so nobody had shown the process was stable; 30 consecutive parts from one setup capture the variation within a setup and almost none of the variation between setups, which is where this process moved. The sample size made the number soft in any case: at \( \hat C_{pk}=1.38 \) with \( n=30 \) the 95% interval runs from about 1.01 to 1.75, so 1.38 was never evidence of clearing 1.33. And the gauge's own variation sat inside \( \hat\sigma \) unexamined, which if anything made the reported figure conservative — the one error that happened to work in the supplier's favour and still told nobody anything true.

Two practices follow. A capability study is not a number, it is a number plus the conditions that produced it: the control charts showing stability, the sampling plan showing what sources of variation were allowed to act, the sample size, and the state of the measuring system. And an acceptance threshold should be applied to the lower confidence limit rather than to the point estimate, which is what turns "we measured 1.38" into "we have demonstrated 1.33".
