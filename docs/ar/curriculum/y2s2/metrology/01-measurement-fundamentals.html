Measurement fundamentals

Accuracy, precision, resolution, and bias untangled for good. Traceability chains to national standards. The vocabulary that makes calibration certificates readable.

Measurement, resolution, precision, accuracy lectureDAVID SKLENICKA

المحاضرة

§1 · Four words that are not synonyms

A measurement is a claim about the world, and like any claim it can be wrong in more than one way. Quality work depends on separating the ways, because each has a different cure and confusing them wastes money on the wrong one.

Resolution is the smallest change an instrument can display — 1 µm on a digital micrometer, 0.02 mm on a vernier caliper. It is a property of the display, and it is the weakest of the four: an instrument can resolve to a micrometre and be wrong by fifty.

Precision, or repeatability, is how closely repeated measurements of the same thing agree with each other. It is measured as a standard deviation and improved by technique, fixturing and averaging.

Bias is the systematic difference between the average of many measurements and the true value. It does not shrink with repetition, and no amount of care in the measuring reduces it.

Accuracy is closeness to the true value, and it needs both: small bias and small scatter. The figure shows the case that catches people out — ten readings of a certified 25.0000 mm block, scattering by only about a micrometre, all of them about five micrometres too big. The instrument is precise and inaccurate, and the operator who checks repeatability and stops there will believe it is fine.

§2 · What the readings actually say

Take the ten readings, in micrometres above the certified value: 4, 6, 5, 7, 4, 6, 5, 6, 4, 5.

Key relations — separating scatter from offset

\[ \bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum x_i,\qquad s=\sqrt{\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2}{n-1}},\qquad \text{bias}=\bar{x}-x_{\text{ref}},\qquad u(\bar{x})=\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]

\( s \) is the spread of a single reading; \( s/\sqrt{n} \) is the uncertainty of their mean. Bias is the distance from that mean to the reference and is not divided by anything.

The mean is 5.2 µm, so the instrument reads 25.0052 mm against a block certified at 25.0000. The sample standard deviation is 1.03 µm. Averaging ten readings tightens the mean to \( 1.03/\sqrt{10}=0.33\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) — genuinely worth having — while the 5.2 µm bias is untouched. That asymmetry is the whole practical point: repetition buys precision and never buys accuracy.

The block itself carries an uncertainty, quoted on its certificate as \( U = 0.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) at \( k=2 \). Compare that with the 5.2 µm bias and the verdict is unambiguous: the reference is twenty-six times better than the error being measured, so the error belongs to the micrometer. Had the two been comparable, the honest conclusion would have been that the check could not resolve the question.

§3 · Traceability: why anyone should believe the block

The block's certificate is only worth what stands behind it. Traceability is that chain: an unbroken sequence of calibrations, each with its own stated uncertainty, linking a working instrument to a national measurement institute and through it to the SI definition of the metre.

Each link degrades slightly — uncertainty accumulates downward, never shrinks — which is why a working gauge is calibrated against a reference gauge several times better than itself, rather than against another working gauge. The usual guidance is a ratio of at least 4:1 between the tolerance being checked and the uncertainty of the check, and the 26:1 above is comfortable.

A calibration certificate is readable once the vocabulary is: the measurand is what was measured, the reference standard is what it was measured against and where its traceability comes from, the result is a value with an uncertainty and a coverage factor, the conditions are the temperature and humidity it holds at, and the statement of conformity is a judgement against a specification, not a measurement. A certificate with a result and no uncertainty is not a calibration certificate.

§4 · Temperature, the error nobody writes on the drawing

Dimensional metrology is defined at 20°C. That is not a nicety; it is part of what a length means, because every material changes size with temperature.

Key relation — thermal error

\[ \Delta L=L\,\alpha\,\Delta T \]

\( \alpha \approx 11.5\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}} \) for steel and \( 23.1\times10^{-6} \) for aluminium.

A 200 mm steel part measured in a 25°C workshop instead of a 20°C room is \( 200(11.5\times10^{-6})(5) = 11.5\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) longer than its defined size. On an IT7 tolerance of about 46 µm that is a quarter of the band, spent before any instrument is picked up. The same part in aluminium moves 23.1 µm.

Two consequences follow. First, the difference between materials matters as much as the absolute movement: measuring an aluminium part with a steel gauge at 25°C leaves \( 23.1-11.5 = 11.6\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) of differential expansion that no amount of instrument quality removes. Second, soak time is a real requirement rather than a ritual — a part carried from a machine to an inspection room is not at room temperature when it arrives, and measuring it immediately measures its history rather than its size.

What this lesson assumes

  • Mean and standard deviation (Engineering Statistics L2) — the two numbers that separate scatter from offset.
  • The standard error of a mean (Engineering Statistics L5) — \( s/\sqrt{n} \), and what it does and does not improve.
  • Thermal expansion (Materials Science I L6) — \( \Delta L=L\alpha\Delta T \), applied here at micrometre scale.
  • Tolerances and IT grades (Engineering Drawing & CAD L6) — the bands these errors are being spent against.
  • SI units (Engineering Mathematics I L1) — the definition the traceability chain terminates at.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Separating precision from accuracy

Problem. A micrometer is used ten times on a gauge block certified at 25.0000 mm with \( U = 0.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) (\( k=2 \)). The readings, in micrometres above the certified value, are 4, 6, 5, 7, 4, 6, 5, 6, 4, 5. Find the mean reading, the repeatability standard deviation, the bias, and the uncertainty of the mean. State whether the instrument is precise, accurate, both or neither.

Solution. (1) \( \bar{x} = (4+6+5+7+4+6+5+6+4+5)/10 = 52/10 = 5.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), so the mean reading is 25.0052 mm.

(2) Deviations from 5.2: −1.2, 0.8, −0.2, 1.8, −1.2, 0.8, −0.2, 0.8, −1.2, −0.2. Their squares sum to 9.60, so \( s = \sqrt{9.60/9} = \sqrt{1.0667} = 1.03\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(3) Bias \( = 5.2 - 0 = +5.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \): the instrument reads high.

(4) \( u(\bar{x}) = s/\sqrt{n} = 1.03/\sqrt{10} = 0.33\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

Answer: mean 25.0052 mm, \( s = 1.03\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), bias \( +5.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), \( u(\bar{x}) = 0.33\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). The instrument is precise but not accurate. Sanity check: the bias is 5.2 µm against a reference uncertainty of 0.2 µm — a ratio of 26, so the error is unambiguously the micrometer's and not the block's. Note what averaging did and did not do: it reduced the uncertainty of the mean to 0.33 µm, better than a single reading by a factor of \( \sqrt{10} \), while leaving the 5.2 µm error exactly where it was. Precision is earned by repetition; accuracy is earned by calibration.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Thermal error against the tolerance band (with a US customary check)

Problem. A 200 mm shaft is measured in a workshop at 25°C rather than the reference 20°C. Find the thermal error for steel (\( \alpha = 11.5\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}} \)) and for aluminium (\( 23.1\times10^{-6} \)), express the steel error as a fraction of an IT7 tolerance of 46 µm, and give the steel error in thousandths of an inch. Then state the error left if an aluminium part is measured with a steel gauge at that temperature.

Solution. (1) Steel: \( \Delta L = L\alpha\Delta T = 200(11.5\times10^{-6})(5) = 0.0115\ \mathrm{mm} = 11.5\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(2) Aluminium: \( 200(23.1\times10^{-6})(5) = 0.0231\ \mathrm{mm} = 23.1\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(3) \( 11.5/46 = 0.25 \) — a quarter of the tolerance band.

(4) \( 11.5\ \mu\mathrm{m} = 0.0115\ \mathrm{mm} \div 25.4 = 0.00045\ \mathrm{in} \), that is 0.45 thou.

(5) An aluminium part on a steel gauge, both at 25°C, leaves the difference: \( 23.1 - 11.5 = 11.6\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

Answer: 11.5 µm steel and 23.1 µm aluminium; the steel error is 25% of an IT7 band, or 0.45 thou; measuring aluminium with a steel gauge leaves 11.6 µm. Sanity check: the differential case is the one worth remembering, because it survives every improvement to the instrument — a perfect gauge of the wrong material still reports 11.6 µm of temperature. It also explains why gauge blocks are specified in the same material as the work where precision matters, and why the reference temperature is written into the definition of a dimension rather than left to the inspector. A quarter of the tolerance band spent before measuring is the practical reason inspection rooms are air-conditioned.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Is the check good enough to make the call?

Problem. A feature has a tolerance band of 46 µm. Three candidate checks are offered: (a) the micrometer above, uncertainty 1.03 µm per reading; (b) a vernier caliper with 20 µm resolution and a repeatability of 15 µm; (c) a comparator reading against the gauge block, uncertainty 2.0 µm. Apply the 4:1 rule — the tolerance should be at least four times the measurement uncertainty — and say which checks are fit for the job.

Solution. The rule asks for \( T/u \ge 4 \), i.e. \( u \le 46/4 = 11.5\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(a) \( 46/1.03 = 44.7 \) ✓ comfortably fit — provided the bias is corrected, since the 5.2 µm offset is not part of \( s \).

(b) \( 46/15 = 3.1 \) ✗ below 4: the caliper's scatter alone eats a third of the band, and its 20 µm resolution is itself nearly half the tolerance.

(c) \( 46/2.0 = 23 \) ✓ fit.

Answer: (a) and (c) are fit for purpose, (b) is not. Sanity check: case (a) carries the lesson's trap. Judged on repeatability alone the micrometer looks like the best of the three, and used uncorrected it would report every part 5.2 µm oversize — more than the entire uncertainty budget the rule allows. The 4:1 rule is a statement about uncertainty, and an uncorrected bias is not uncertainty, it is error. A gauge that has been characterised and not corrected is worse than one that has not been characterised, because the number is now trusted.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

An instrument's resolution is

Resolution is a property of the display and nothing more. A micrometer resolving to 1 µm can read 5 µm high all day, as the worked example does. Closeness to truth is accuracy, the spread of repeats is precision, and all three are independent — which is why a specification quoting only resolution tells you almost nothing about whether an instrument can do a job.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

Ten repeated readings are averaged instead of one being taken. This improves

\( u(\bar{x}) = s/\sqrt{n} \), so ten readings tighten the mean from 1.03 to 0.33 µm. Bias is systematic: every one of the ten readings carries the same 5.2 µm offset, so their average carries it too. More readings of a wrong ruler are still wrong, and this is the single most useful thing to understand about repetition.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

Traceability means that a measurement

It is the chain, and the uncertainty stated at every link. Each step adds uncertainty rather than removing it, which is why a working gauge is calibrated against a reference several times better than itself. A record in a quality system is evidence of traceability, not traceability; a technician's certification is about competence, which is a different requirement.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

A calibration certificate gives a result but states no uncertainty. It is

A measurement result without an uncertainty is not a result, because nothing can be judged against it: you cannot apply a 4:1 rule, cannot decide conformity near a limit, and cannot pass traceability down another link. Accreditation is what makes a stated uncertainty credible; it does not substitute for stating one.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Dimensional metrology defines length at

20°C is part of what a dimension means, not a convenience. A 200 mm steel part at 25°C is 11.5 µm longer than its defined size — a quarter of an IT7 band — and an aluminium one is 23.1 µm longer. Without a defined reference temperature, a drawing dimension would not be a single number.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

An aluminium part is measured with a steel gauge, both at 25°C. The residual error is

Both expand, so part of the error cancels — but not all of it, because the coefficients differ: \( (23.1-11.5)\times10^{-6}(200)(5) = 11.6\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). This differential term survives any improvement to the instrument, which is why gauges are specified in the same material as the work when precision matters, and why "both are at the same temperature" is not the reassurance it sounds like.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

The 4:1 rule requires that the tolerance being checked is at least four times the

It is a ratio of tolerance to uncertainty, so the whole measurement process is in scope, not just the display. It also does not license an uncorrected bias: a bias is a known error and belongs corrected, not absorbed into an uncertainty budget. The worked micrometer passes the rule at 44:1 on scatter and would still report every part 5.2 µm oversize.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A part is carried straight from a machining centre to the inspection room and measured immediately. The measurement

The room being at 20°C says nothing about the part, which arrives carrying the heat of the cut. Soak time is a real requirement, not a ritual: until the part reaches the reference temperature, \( \Delta L=L\alpha\Delta T \) is measuring how recently it was machined. A calibrated instrument reports that thermal state faithfully, which is precisely the problem.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the shaft batch that failed at the customer and passed at the works. A batch of 200 mm shafts was inspected against a 46 µm diameter tolerance, passed, shipped, and rejected on receipt as oversize. Both parties re-measured, both were confident, and the readings differed by about 5 µm consistently — not randomly, which is the detail that solved it.

A consistent difference is a bias, and bias belongs to an instrument rather than to a process. Checking the works micrometer against a gauge block certified at 25.0000 mm with \( U = 0.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) gave ten readings averaging 25.0052 mm: the instrument read 5.2 µm high, with a repeatability of only 1.03 µm. Its scatter was excellent, which is why nobody had suspected it — repeated measurements agreed beautifully with each other and consistently with nothing else. Against a 46 µm band, a 5.2 µm offset consumes 11% of the tolerance and moves the whole batch toward one limit.

Two practices follow. Repeatability checks are not calibration: agreeing with yourself is the one thing a biased instrument does perfectly, so an instrument must periodically be compared with a traceable reference and not merely with itself. And when two parties disagree consistently rather than randomly, the argument is about a gauge, not about a process — the fastest route to the answer is for both to measure the same certified artefact, which takes minutes and settles what weeks of re-inspection will not.
