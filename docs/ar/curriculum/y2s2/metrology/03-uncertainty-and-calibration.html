Uncertainty and calibration

Uncertainty budgets built from type A and B components. Calibration intervals and out-of-tolerance investigations. A micrometer calibration worked end to end.

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Type A versus Type B uncertainty: what distinguishes them?
  • Out-of-tolerance found at calibration: what must the investigation cover?

§1 · A result without an uncertainty is not a result

Lesson 1 ended on that claim; this lesson makes it operational. An uncertainty is the width of the interval that can reasonably be attributed to the measurand — not a mistake, not a tolerance, and not a guess. It is computed, and the computation is a budget: list everything that could move the answer, put a standard deviation on each, and combine them.

The vocabulary trips people at the first step. Type A and Type B classify how a component was evaluated, not what kind of error it is. Type A means evaluated by statistics on repeated observations; Type B means evaluated by any other means — a calibration certificate, a manufacturer's specification, the resolution of a display, or documented judgement. A systematic effect can be Type A and a random one Type B. The distinction that people import from elsewhere, random versus systematic, is a different axis entirely and does not appear in the arithmetic.

§2 · Building the budget

Each component enters as a standard uncertainty, which is a standard deviation. Getting it there depends on what you know about the component.

Key relations — converting to standard uncertainty

\[ u = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\ \text{(mean of \(n\) repeats)},\qquad u = \frac{U}{k}\ \text{(certificate)},\qquad u = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}\ \text{(rectangular, half-width \(a\))} \]

The rectangular divisor applies when a value is known only to lie within limits with no reason to prefer the middle — the usual case for a display resolution or a temperature band.

Take the micrometer of Lesson 1, calibrated against the 25 mm block. Four components:

  • Repeatability (Type A): ten readings gave \( s = 1.03\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), and the result is their mean, so \( u = 1.03/\sqrt{10} = 0.327\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).
  • Display resolution (Type B): 1 µm steps, so the true value lies within \( \pm0.5\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) of what is shown; rectangular, \( u = 0.5/\sqrt{3} = 0.289\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).
  • Temperature (Type B): the room is held to \( \pm1\ \mathrm{K} \), and \( 25\ \mathrm{mm}\times11.5\times10^{-6}\times1\ \mathrm{K} = 0.288\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) of half-width; rectangular, \( u = 0.166\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).
  • Reference block (Type B): certificate says \( U = 0.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) at \( k=2 \), so \( u = 0.10\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).
Key relation — combining and expanding

\[ u_c=\sqrt{\textstyle\sum u_i^2},\qquad U=k\,u_c\ \ (k=2 \approx 95\%) \]

Independent components add in quadrature, so the largest dominates and the small ones almost vanish.

\( u_c=\sqrt{0.327^2+0.289^2+0.166^2+0.100^2}=0.477\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), and \( U = 0.95\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). The calibration therefore reports a correction of \( -5.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) with an uncertainty of \( 0.95\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) at \( k=2 \).

§3 · Reading the budget, which is the point of writing it

The single number \( U \) is what goes on the certificate; the ranking is what the budget is actually for. Because components add in quadrature, their variance shares are what matter: repeatability 47%, resolution 37%, temperature 12%, block 4%.

That ordering is a spending plan. Buying a block certified ten times better would improve \( u_c \) by less than a tenth of a per cent, because 4% of the variance cannot be worth much. Taking more readings attacks the biggest term — but quadrupling them to 40 only reduces \( U \) from 0.95 to 0.77 µm, a 19% gain for four times the work, because \( s/\sqrt{n} \) improves as a square root and the display's resolution is waiting underneath at 0.289 µm. Beyond that point the only remaining moves are a finer display or a different instrument.

The budget also settles whether the bias is real. The measured offset is 5.2 µm against an expanded uncertainty of 0.95 µm — a factor of 5.5 — so it is not an artefact of the calibration, and it should be corrected rather than ignored. Had the bias come out at, say, 0.6 µm, the honest statement would have been that no bias was detected at this uncertainty.

§4 · From certificate to decision: intervals, guard bands, and the bad day

Calibration is not an event but a cycle. The interval is set by how the instrument actually behaves — its drift history, how hard it is used, and what it decides — not by a number copied from a supplier's leaflet. Instruments that pass comfortably every time earn a longer interval; one that drifts earns a shorter one, and the evidence for either is the calibration history.

Using the certificate to accept a part introduces a second decision. A measured value sitting exactly on the tolerance limit is a coin toss, because the uncertainty straddles it. A guard band resolves this by accepting only within limits pulled in by \( U \): on a 21 µm tolerance with \( U = 0.95 \), accepting only within \( 21 - 2(0.95) = 19.1\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) of band gives a confident pass, at the cost of rejecting some good parts. Which risk to carry is a commercial decision, and it belongs written down in a decision rule rather than settled per-inspector.

Finally, the case everyone hopes to avoid. When an instrument is found out of tolerance at calibration, the problem is not the instrument — it is every measurement made with it since the last good calibration, all of which are now suspect. That is why calibration records identify which instrument measured what, and why the interval matters commercially: it bounds how much work an out-of-tolerance finding puts back in question. A shop that cannot answer "what did this gauge measure since March" has a records problem that a calibration contract does not solve.

What this lesson assumes

  • Bias, precision and the standard error (Lesson 1) — the ten readings reused here as the Type A component.
  • Thermal error (Lesson 1) — \( \Delta L=L\alpha\Delta T \), entering the budget as a temperature term.
  • Instrument resolution (Lesson 2) — which becomes a rectangular contribution rather than a rounding nuisance.
  • Variance of a sum (Engineering Statistics L4) — why independent contributions add in quadrature and not linearly.
  • Normal and uniform distributions (Engineering Statistics L3) — the source of the \( k=2 \) and \( \sqrt{3} \) divisors.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

A micrometer calibration budget, end to end

Problem. Build the uncertainty budget for the Lesson 1 calibration. The components are: repeatability, \( s = 1.03\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) over ten readings, the result being their mean; display resolution 1 µm; room temperature held to \( \pm1\ \mathrm{K} \) on a 25 mm steel block (\( \alpha = 11.5\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}} \)); and a reference block certified at \( U = 0.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), \( k=2 \). Find each standard uncertainty, the combined and expanded uncertainty, and state the reported result given a measured bias of \( +5.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

Solution. (1) Repeatability, Type A: \( u_1 = s/\sqrt{n} = 1.03/\sqrt{10} = 0.327\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(2) Resolution, Type B, rectangular with half-width 0.5 µm: \( u_2 = 0.5/\sqrt{3} = 0.289\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(3) Temperature, Type B: half-width \( 25(11.5\times10^{-6})(1) = 0.2875\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), rectangular, so \( u_3 = 0.2875/\sqrt{3} = 0.166\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(4) Reference block, Type B: \( u_4 = U/k = 0.2/2 = 0.100\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(5) \( u_c = \sqrt{0.327^2+0.289^2+0.166^2+0.100^2} = \sqrt{0.1069+0.0834+0.0276+0.0100} = \sqrt{0.2278} = 0.477\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(6) \( U = 2u_c = 0.95\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

Answer: the micrometer requires a correction of \( -5.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), with an expanded uncertainty of \( 0.95\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) at \( k=2 \). Sanity check: the bias is 5.5 times the expanded uncertainty, so it is unambiguously real and belongs corrected rather than noted. Note also that the four components span a factor of only 3.3 between largest and smallest, yet in quadrature the smallest contributes 4% of the variance against the largest's 47% — quadrature punishes small terms hard, which is why chasing them is almost always wasted effort.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

What should we spend money on?

Problem. Using the budget above, compute each component's share of the total variance. Then evaluate two proposals: (a) buy a reference block certified ten times better, and (b) increase the repeat count from 10 to 40. Give the new \( U \) in each case and say which is worth doing.

Solution. (1) Variance shares, \( u_i^2/u_c^2 \) with \( u_c^2 = 0.2278 \):

repeatability \( 0.1069/0.2278 = 46.9\% \); resolution \( 0.0834/0.2278 = 36.6\% \); temperature \( 0.0276/0.2278 = 12.1\% \); block \( 0.0100/0.2278 = 4.4\% \).

(2) Proposal (a): a block ten times better gives \( u_4 = 0.010 \), so \( u_c = \sqrt{0.2278-0.0100+0.0001} = \sqrt{0.2179} = 0.4668 \) and \( U = 0.93\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) — a 2% improvement for a large cost.

(3) Proposal (b): \( u_1 = 1.03/\sqrt{40} = 0.163 \), so \( u_c = \sqrt{0.0266+0.0834+0.0276+0.0100} = \sqrt{0.1476} = 0.384 \) and \( U = 0.77\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) — a 19% improvement for four times the measuring effort.

Answer: shares 46.9 / 36.6 / 12.1 / 4.4 per cent; the better block buys 2% and the extra readings buy 19%. Take the readings if either. Sanity check: the diminishing return in (b) is the instructive part. Quadrupling \( n \) halves only the repeatability term, and once it has fallen to 0.163 the display resolution at 0.289 is the largest contributor — a term that no number of repeats can touch. A budget is worth rebuilding after any improvement, because the thing to fix next is rarely the thing you just fixed.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Guard banding a conformity decision (with a US customary check)

Problem. A 25 mm feature carries a 21 µm tolerance and is measured with the calibrated micrometer, \( U = 0.95\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). (a) Find the guard-banded acceptance band if both limits are pulled in by \( U \). (b) A part measures 0.4 µm inside the upper limit — state the correct decision. (c) Give the guard-banded band in thousandths of an inch. (d) Say what changes if the same feature is inspected with the Lesson 2 caliper.

Solution. (a) Pulling each limit in by \( U \) removes \( 2U \) from the band: \( 21 - 2(0.95) = 19.1\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(b) The part sits 0.4 µm inside the limit, which is less than \( U = 0.95 \). It is inside the tolerance as measured but inside the guard band's rejected zone: conformity cannot be demonstrated, so under a guarded-acceptance rule it is not accepted.

(c) \( 19.1\ \mu\mathrm{m} = 0.0191\ \mathrm{mm} \div 25.4 = 0.00075\ \mathrm{in} \), that is 0.75 thou, against a 0.83 thou tolerance.

(d) The caliper's worst-case Abbe error alone is 69.8 µm — more than three times the whole tolerance. There is no guard band to compute: \( T - 2U \) would be negative, meaning no measured value could demonstrate conformity at all.

Answer: a 19.1 µm acceptance band (0.75 thou); the part at 0.4 µm inside is not accepted; with the caliper the decision is impossible. Sanity check: part (d) is the useful one. A negative guard band is the arithmetic telling you the measurement cannot support the decision being asked of it — the same verdict the 4:1 rule gave in Lesson 1, arrived at from the other direction. Note also that guard banding protects the customer by rejecting some good parts; pushing the band outward instead protects the supplier and ships some bad ones. Neither is neutral, which is why the choice belongs in a written decision rule rather than in an inspector's judgement.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

Type A and Type B uncertainty components are distinguished by

Type A means evaluated by statistics on repeated observations; Type B means evaluated by any other means — a certificate, a specification, a display resolution, or documented judgement. Random versus systematic is a different axis: a systematic effect can be evaluated statistically and a random one from a specification. Nothing in the combination arithmetic depends on the classification at all.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

A display resolves to 1 µm. Its contribution to the uncertainty budget is

The true value lies within half a count either side of what is shown, with no reason to prefer the middle, so it is a rectangular distribution of half-width 0.5 µm and its standard uncertainty is \( a/\sqrt3 = 0.289\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). Using the half-width directly overstates it by 73%; using the full count overstates it by more than three times.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A certificate states \( U = 0.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) at \( k = 2 \). The standard uncertainty to enter in a budget is

\( u = U/k = 0.2/2 = 0.1\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). The coverage factor has to be undone before combining, because budgets combine standard uncertainties and are expanded only once at the end. Entering the expanded value directly double-counts the coverage factor and inflates the final \( U \).

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Independent uncertainty components are combined

Variances add for independent contributions, so the standard uncertainties add in quadrature. The practical consequence is that quadrature punishes small terms severely: in the worked budget the smallest component is only 3.3 times smaller than the largest, yet contributes 4% of the variance against 47%. Taking the largest alone would understate \( u_c \) by about a third here.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

In the worked budget, repeatability contributes 47% of the variance and the reference block 4%. Buying a block ten times better would

Removing almost all of a 4% share leaves \( u_c=\sqrt{0.2278-0.0100} = 0.467 \) against 0.477, so \( U \) falls from 0.95 to 0.93 µm — about 2%. It is not literally zero, but it is money spent on the smallest term in the budget. That ranking is the main reason to write a budget rather than merely quote its total.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Quadrupling the number of repeat readings from 10 to 40 reduces the worked \( U \) from 0.95 to 0.77 µm. The reason the gain is only 19% is that

Four times the readings halve the repeatability term, from 0.327 to 0.163 — but the display resolution sits underneath at 0.289 and is untouched by repeating. Once the biggest term has been beaten below another, further effort on it buys almost nothing. Rebuild the budget after any improvement: what to fix next is rarely what you just fixed.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A measured value sits 0.4 µm inside a tolerance limit, with \( U = 0.95\ \mu\mathrm{m} \). Under a guarded-acceptance rule the part is

The uncertainty straddles the limit, so the measurement cannot demonstrate conformity. Guarded acceptance pulls the acceptance limits in by \( U \) — a 21 µm tolerance becomes a 19.1 µm acceptance band — and protects the customer at the cost of rejecting some genuinely good parts. Re-measuring until a favourable reading appears is not a decision rule; it is selecting from noise.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

An instrument is found out of tolerance at its calibration. The most serious consequence is that

The instrument can be adjusted or replaced in an afternoon; the work it has already passed cannot. Everything measured since the last good calibration is now in question, which is why records must link instruments to the parts they measured, and why the calibration interval is a commercial decision as well as a technical one — it bounds how much work an out-of-tolerance finding puts back on the table.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the better gauge block that changed nothing. An inspection department was asked to tighten its measurement uncertainty and responded by replacing its reference gauge blocks with a grade certified an order of magnitude better. The purchase was substantial, the certificates were impressive, and the reported uncertainty on the department's calibrations barely moved.

The budget said why, and the budget had never been written. Of the four components in a typical micrometer calibration — repeatability 0.327, resolution 0.289, temperature 0.166 and the block 0.100 µm — the block contributed 4.4% of the variance. Removing almost all of it took \( u_c \) from 0.477 to 0.467 µm and \( U \) from 0.95 to 0.93: a 2% improvement. Meanwhile repeatability at 46.9% and display resolution at 36.6% together held five-sixths of the variance, and neither had been touched. Quadrature is unforgiving that way — a component three times smaller than the leader carries a ninth of its weight.

Two practices follow. Write the budget before spending: it is an afternoon's arithmetic and it ranks every possible improvement by what it would actually buy, which no supplier's datasheet does. And rebuild it after each change, because the ranking moves — in this budget, taking four times as many readings would have been the right first move, and would then have left display resolution as the largest term and a finer instrument as the only remaining option.
