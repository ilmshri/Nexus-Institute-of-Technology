Spur gear design: bending

Lewis bending analysis with velocity and geometry factors. Module and face width chosen against tooth breakage. Where root cracks start and why.

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Which two velocity-dependent factors modify Lewis bending stress?
  • Why do gear root cracks start on the tension side?

§1 · A gear tooth is a cantilever that nobody built carefully

Strip the mesh of everything decorative and one tooth is a short, stubby cantilever loaded near its tip. Wilfred Lewis published that idealisation in 1892 and it still sets the module of most industrial gearing, because the assumption it makes is the right one to make first: the tooth breaks at its root, in bending, and the root is where a designer has any leverage at all.

The idealisation is deliberately crude. It puts the whole tangential load on a single tooth tip, ignores the radial component that compresses the root, ignores the fact that two tooth pairs share the load through part of the mesh cycle, and — most importantly — ignores the stress concentration at the root fillet. Every one of those omissions is conservative except the last, which is why Lewis alone is a sizing tool and not a verdict.

What the idealisation gets right is the scaling. The tooth's section modulus grows with the square of its size while the load it must carry falls as the gear gets bigger, and that combination is what makes module such a powerful design variable.

§2 · The Lewis equation and its form factor

Treat the tooth as a cantilever of face width \( b \), thickness \( t \) at the critical root section, and height \( h \) from the load point to that section. Bending gives \( \sigma = 6W_t h/(bt^2) \). Lewis's insight was that for a given tooth form, \( h \) and \( t \) both scale with the module, so the whole geometry collapses into one dimensionless number:

\[ \sigma = \frac{W_t}{b\,m\,Y} \]

\( Y \) is the Lewis form factor. It depends only on the number of teeth and the pressure angle, not on size — 0.245 at 14 teeth, 0.322 at 20, 0.359 at 30, approaching about 0.485 for a rack. Fewer teeth means a more sharply undercut, weaker root, which is the design reason to avoid very small pinions quite apart from interference.

The tangential load comes from the power, not from a free body: \( W_t = P/V \) with \( V = \pi d n/60 \) the pitch-line velocity and \( d = mN \). Note what that does — for fixed power, a bigger gear runs faster at the pitch line and therefore carries less tangential load. Module helps twice over.

§3 · The velocity factor, and what it is honestly doing

Real teeth are not perfect involutes. Manufacturing error, tooth deflection under load, and the resulting velocity fluctuation at each mesh throw a dynamic increment on top of the steady load, and it grows with speed. The classical Barth factor

\[ K_v = \frac{6.1 + V}{6.1} \qquad (V \text{ in m/s, cut teeth}) \]

is an empirical fudge, and worth naming as one. It is not derived; it is a curve fitted to gears of a particular quality class, and better-cut gears get a gentler constant. Use it to size, then check against a real rating standard before cutting metal. What it captures correctly is the direction: the faster the pitch line runs, the more of the load is dynamic rather than transmitted.

Key relation — Lewis bending with dynamic effect

\[ \sigma = \frac{W_t K_v}{b\,m\,Y}, \qquad W_t = \frac{P}{V}, \qquad V = \frac{\pi m N n}{60} \]

Hold \( b = 10m \) and the module appears cubed in the denominator: once through \( b \), once through \( m \), and once more through \( W_t \), which falls as the gear grows. Only \( K_v \) pushes back, because the bigger gear runs faster.

§4 · The design working method

One: fix the transmitted power, the pinion speed, and the tooth count from the ratio you need. Two: pick a trial module and compute in this order — \( d = mN \), then \( V \), then \( W_t = P/V \), then \( K_v \). Three: choose the face width as a multiple of module; 8 to 16 is the usual band, and the reason it is a band rather than a number is load distribution: a wide, narrow-shafted gear does not carry load evenly across its face, so the extra width is not fully earned. Four: compute \( \sigma \) and compare against the allowable bending stress for the material and life required. Five: if it fails, step the module up before reaching for face width — the module is the cubic lever and the face width is only linear.

That last instruction is the whole practical content of the lesson. A designer who answers a failed bending check by widening the gear is buying a linear improvement at a cost in shaft length, bearing span, and load distribution; a designer who steps the module buys roughly a cubic one and a stiffer tooth into the bargain.

الأسس

What this lesson assumes

  • Gear tooth geometry (Kinematics & Dynamics of Machinery L5) — module, pitch diameter, pressure angle, and why the involute is the profile used.
  • Bending of beams (Strength of Materials L4) — \( \sigma = Mc/I \) and the idea of a section modulus.
  • Stress concentration (Strength of Materials L11) — why a fillet radius, not a sharp corner, decides where a crack starts.
  • Fatigue and the S–N method (Machine Design I L3) — a tooth is loaded once per revolution, so the allowable stress here is a fatigue allowable, not yield.

Glossary

TermEquationWhen to use
Module\( m = d/N \)The size variable of a gear tooth; the single strongest lever on bending stress.
Tangential load\( W_t = P/V \)The load that bends the tooth; falls as the gear grows, for fixed power.
Pitch-line velocity\( V = \pi d n/60 \)Sets both the tangential load and the dynamic factor.
Lewis form factor\( Y \)Depends on tooth count and pressure angle only; 0.322 at 20 teeth, 20° full depth.
Lewis bending stress\( \sigma = W_t K_v/(bmY) \)Sizing the module against tooth breakage at the root.
Velocity (dynamic) factor\( K_v = (6.1+V)/6.1 \)Empirical uplift for mesh dynamics; state the quality class it came from.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Sizing a pinion against tooth breakage

Problem. A spur pinion transmits \( P = 15\ \mathrm{kW} \) at \( n = 1450\ \mathrm{rev/min} \). It has \( N = 20 \) teeth, 20° full-depth form (\( Y = 0.322 \)), and the face width is held at \( b = 10m \). Cut teeth, so use the Barth factor. Find: for a trial module \( m = 4\ \mathrm{mm} \), the pitch diameter, pitch-line velocity, tangential load, velocity factor, and the Lewis bending stress.

Solution. (1) Pitch diameter: \( d = mN = 4 \times 20 = 80\ \mathrm{mm} \). (2) Pitch-line velocity: \( V = \pi d n/60 = \pi(0.080)(1450)/60 = 6.074\ \mathrm{m/s} \). (3) Tangential load: \( W_t = P/V = 15\,000/6.074 = 2470\ \mathrm{N} \). (4) Velocity factor: \( K_v = (6.1+6.074)/6.1 = 1.996 \). (5) Face width \( b = 10m = 40\ \mathrm{mm} \), so \( \sigma = W_tK_v/(bmY) = (2470)(1.996)/(0.040 \times 0.004 \times 0.322) = 95.7\ \mathrm{MPa} \).

Against a 140 MPa allowable this passes with a margin of 1.46. Note the order of the calculation: geometry, then velocity, then load. The load is an output of the size choice, not an input to it, which is what makes gear sizing iterative.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

The module step that decides the design

Problem. For the same drive, a colleague proposes \( m = 3\ \mathrm{mm} \) to save space, keeping \( b = 10m \). Find: the resulting bending stress, whether it meets the 140 MPa allowable, and the ratio of this stress to the \( m = 4 \) result. Compare that ratio with the cube of the module ratio and explain the difference.

Solution. \( d = 60\ \mathrm{mm} \), \( V = \pi(0.060)(1450)/60 = 4.555\ \mathrm{m/s} \), \( W_t = 15\,000/4.555 = 3293\ \mathrm{N} \), \( K_v = (6.1+4.555)/6.1 = 1.747 \), \( b = 30\ \mathrm{mm} \). Then \( \sigma = (3293)(1.747)/(0.030 \times 0.003 \times 0.322) = 198.5\ \mathrm{MPa} \) — over the 140 MPa allowable, so the proposal fails.

The ratio is \( 198.5/95.67 = 2.07 \), against \( (4/3)^3 = 2.37 \) for a pure cubic law. The shortfall is the velocity factor: the larger gear runs at 6.07 m/s rather than 4.56, so its \( K_v \) is 1.996 rather than 1.747 and it gives back some of what the geometry won. Module is very nearly a cubic lever, but not quite, and the correction always works against the bigger gear.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Rescuing the small module with face width

Problem. Suppose the \( m = 3\ \mathrm{mm} \) pinion must be kept for envelope reasons. Find: the face width that would bring its bending stress down to the 140 MPa allowable, and the resulting \( b/m \) ratio. Comment on whether this is a sound fix.

Solution. Bending stress is inversely proportional to face width, so \( b = W_tK_v/(\sigma m Y) \). With \( W_t = 3293\ \mathrm{N} \), \( K_v = 1.747 \), \( \sigma = 140\ \mathrm{MPa} \), \( m = 0.003\ \mathrm{m} \), \( Y = 0.322 \):

\[ b = \frac{(3293)(1.747)}{(140\times10^6)(0.003)(0.322)} = 0.0425\ \mathrm{m} = 42.5\ \mathrm{mm} \]

That is \( b/m = 42.5/3 = 14.2 \), inside the 8–16 band but at its upper end. The arithmetic works; the engineering is weaker. Face width buys stress only in proportion, and it buys it on the assumption that load is uniform across the face — which a 14:1 face-width ratio on a shaft that deflects under the same load will not deliver. The honest comparison is that \( m = 4 \) at \( b = 40\ \mathrm{mm} \) is both narrower and less stressed than \( m = 3 \) at \( b = 42.5\ \mathrm{mm} \).

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

Holding the face-width ratio \( b/m \) constant, how does Lewis bending stress scale with module for a fixed transmitted power and speed?

Module enters three times: through \( b = (b/m)\cdot m \), through \( m \) itself, and through \( W_t = P/V \), since \( V \propto m \) makes the tangential load fall as the gear grows. That is \( 1/m^3 \), tempered slightly by \( K_v \) rising with velocity. In the worked case one step from 3 to 4 mm divided the stress by 2.07 against a pure-cubic 2.37.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

Doubling only the face width \( b \), with module and speed unchanged, changes the Lewis bending stress by what factor?

\( \sigma = W_tK_v/(bmY) \) and nothing else in it depends on \( b \), so stress is simply inversely proportional to face width: doubling \( b \) halves \( \sigma \). This is exactly why face width is the weak lever — and why it is tempting, since it looks like the cheapest change on a drawing.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

The Lewis form factor \( Y \) for a 20° full-depth tooth is 0.245 at 14 teeth and 0.359 at 30 teeth. What does that trend say?

\( Y \) depends only on tooth count and pressure angle, never on size. A low tooth count gives a more undercut, thinner root and therefore a smaller \( Y \), which raises the stress for the same load. Small pinions are already the interference-prone member; the form factor penalises them again on strength. The two distractors that tie \( Y \) to module or to load both misread it as something other than a pure shape number.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

For fixed transmitted power, increasing the module increases the pitch-line velocity. What is the net effect on the tangential load \( W_t \)?

Power is the product of force and velocity at the pitch line, so at fixed power a faster pitch line means a smaller tangential force. Since \( V = \pi mNn/60 \) grows in direct proportion to module, \( W_t \) falls in inverse proportion. This is the third of the three routes by which module reduces bending stress, and the one designers most often forget when they reason from torque alone.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A spur tooth fails in bending. Where does the crack almost always originate?

Bending puts the loaded flank's root in tension, and the fillet there is both the highest-stressed section and a geometric stress raiser. Fatigue cracks initiate in tension, so the tension-side fillet is the site. The pitch line is where surface failure — pitting — concentrates, which is a different failure mode with a different equation, and the subject of the next lesson.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Which of these does the plain Lewis equation not account for?

Lewis treats the tooth as a smooth cantilever and takes no account of the fillet as a stress raiser — which is precisely the location where the crack starts. Modern ratings restore it as a geometry factor \( J \) that folds the concentration and load sharing into \( Y \). The other three are all present: \( W_t \) is the tangential load, \( b \) is the face width, and tooth count enters through \( Y \).

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

The Barth velocity factor \( K_v = (6.1+V)/6.1 \) is best described as:

It is a curve fitted to measured behaviour of gears cut to a particular quality, and better-cut gearing uses a gentler constant. Naming it as empirical matters: it tells you the number is a sizing aid whose validity ends where its quality class does, and that a real rating check against a standard is still owed before metal is cut.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A bending check fails by a small margin. Which change reduces the stress most for the least penalty elsewhere?

Module is close to a cubic lever; face width is strictly linear and is paid for in shaft length, bearing span, and worse load distribution across the face. Shaft diameter does not appear in the Lewis equation at all — it matters for deflection and therefore load distribution, but it does not reduce tooth bending stress directly. Raising the speed lowers \( W_t \) but raises \( K_v \) and is rarely a free variable.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the gearbox that was quieter after it broke. A drive on a materials-handling line sheds two teeth from its input pinion after eighteen months. The replacement is ordered to the same drawing, and fails again in under a year. Only on the second failure does anyone plot the fracture faces: both start at the root fillet on the loaded flank, both show beach marks, and both are fatigue — not the overload the first report assumed.

The design file, when it is finally read, sizes the pinion on the motor's nameplate power. The line, however, starts against a loaded belt, and the starting torque is roughly twice nameplate for about a second. Lewis bending stress is linear in load, so that is a factor of two on the stress for a few hundred cycles per start — and the pinion is started perhaps a dozen times a shift. The original calculation was arithmetically correct and answered the wrong question: it sized for the load that the drive runs at, not the load that it starts at. The rebuild steps the module up one size, keeps the same centre distance by reducing tooth counts on both members, and records the start condition as a named load case. The characteristic detail is that the new gearbox is also audibly quieter, because a stiffer tooth deflects less under the same load and meshes closer to its intended involute.
