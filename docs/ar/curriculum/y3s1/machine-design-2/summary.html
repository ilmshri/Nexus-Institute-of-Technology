MDN 301 · Lesson 01 · Spur gear design: bending

Lesson 01Spur gear design: bending

Key points

  • A gear tooth is idealised as a cantilever loaded at the tip and broken at the root: \( \sigma = W_tK_v/(bmY) \).
  • The Lewis form factor \( Y \) depends only on tooth count and pressure angle — 0.245 at 14 teeth, 0.322 at 20, 0.359 at 30 — never on size.
  • The tangential load is an output of the size choice, not an input: \( W_t = P/V \) with \( V = \pi mNn/60 \), so a bigger gear carries less tooth load at the same power.
  • Module therefore appears three times in the denominator and is close to a cubic lever; face width appears once and is strictly linear.
  • Worked case: 15 kW at 1450 rev/min through a 20-tooth pinion at \( b = 10m \) gives 198.5 MPa at \( m = 3 \) — failing a 140 MPa allowable — and 95.7 MPa at \( m = 4 \), passing with a margin of 1.46.
  • The stress ratio between those two is 2.07 rather than the pure-cubic 2.37, because the larger gear runs faster at the pitch line and carries a higher velocity factor.
  • \( K_v = (6.1+V)/6.1 \) is an empirical fit for one quality class, not a derived dynamic amplification; size with it, then rate against a standard.
  • Lewis ignores the root fillet stress concentration, which is exactly where the fatigue crack starts — so it sizes, it does not certify.
  • When a bending check fails, step the module before widening the face: the first is cubic and stiffens the tooth, the second is linear and costs shaft length and load distribution.

MDN 301 · Lesson 01 · Spur gear design: bending · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Gear tooth geometry (Kinematics & Dynamics of Machinery L5) — module, pitch diameter, pressure angle, and why the involute is the profile used.
  • Bending of beams (Strength of Materials L4) — \( \sigma = Mc/I \) and the idea of a section modulus.
  • Stress concentration (Strength of Materials L11) — why a fillet radius, not a sharp corner, decides where a crack starts.
  • Fatigue and the S–N method (Machine Design I L3) — a tooth is loaded once per revolution, so the allowable stress here is a fatigue allowable, not yield.

Glossary

TermEquationWhen to use
Module\( m = d/N \)The size variable of a gear tooth; the single strongest lever on bending stress.
Tangential load\( W_t = P/V \)The load that bends the tooth; falls as the gear grows, for fixed power.
Pitch-line velocity\( V = \pi d n/60 \)Sets both the tangential load and the dynamic factor.
Lewis form factor\( Y \)Depends on tooth count and pressure angle only; 0.322 at 20 teeth, 20° full depth.
Lewis bending stress\( \sigma = W_t K_v/(bmY) \)Sizing the module against tooth breakage at the root.
Velocity (dynamic) factor\( K_v = (6.1+V)/6.1 \)Empirical uplift for mesh dynamics; state the quality class it came from.
Face-width ratio\( b/m \)Usually 8–16; wider gains less than proportionally because load spreads unevenly.

MDN 301 · Lesson 02 · Spur gear design: surface durability

Lesson 02Spur gear design: surface durability

Key points

  • Gears have two failure modes with two equations and two allowables: tooth breakage in bending, and pitting from repeated Hertzian contact. Neither sizes a gear alone.
  • Pitting is surface fatigue, concentrated in a band near the pitch line and usually just below it on the dedendum, where sliding reverses and the film is thinnest.
  • At the pitch point the flanks are replaced by cylinders of radius \( (d/2)\sin\phi \), giving \( \sigma_H = \sqrt{W'E^*/(\pi R)} \) with \( 1/R = 1/r_1 + 1/r_2 \) and \( 1/E^* = \sum (1-\nu^2)/E \).
  • \( R \) is dominated by the smaller member, which is the geometric reason the pinion always pits first — 10.26 mm against radii of 13.68 and 41.04 in the worked case.
  • The two modes are exactly coupled: \( \sigma_H = K\sqrt{\sigma_b} \), with \( K = 69.5\ \mathrm{MPa}^{1/2} \) here, depending only on material, tooth count, ratio, pressure angle and form factor.
  • So halving bending stress buys only \( \sqrt2 \) on contact, and contact stress responds to load only as \( \sqrt{W} \). Geometry is a weak instrument against pitting.
  • Compare modes as utilisations, never as stresses: 95.7 MPa against a 140 allowable passes at 0.68, while 680 MPa against a 620 allowable fails at 1.10 — the larger number is the safer one.
  • For this drive bending reaches its allowable at \( m = 3.44 \) and contact at \( m = 4.31 \); the gear is sized on the later crossing, so the previous lesson's \( m = 4 \) is not adequate and \( m = 5 \) is the smallest that passes both.
  • The cheaper route is usually metallurgy: carburising and grinding roughly doubles the contact allowable, taking the \( m = 4 \) pair to 0.52 utilisation with no change in envelope — then re-check bending, because the root's allowable changed too.

MDN 301 · Lesson 02 · Spur gear design: surface durability · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

  • Lewis bending (this course, L1) — \( \sigma_b = W_tK_v/(bmY) \) and the worked 15 kW drive it sized.
  • Gear tooth geometry (Kinematics & Dynamics of Machinery L5) — pressure angle, pitch circle, and the involute's radius of curvature.
  • Fatigue as a surface phenomenon (Machine Design I L3) — cyclic stress, initiation sites, and why a finite-life allowable is not a yield strength.
  • Elastic constants (Strength of Materials L1) — Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio as independent material numbers.

Glossary

TermEquationWhen to use
Hertzian contact stress\( \sigma_H = \sqrt{W'E^*/(\pi R)} \)Peak pressure in line contact; the pitting driver.
Relative radius\( 1/R = 1/r_1 + 1/r_2 \)Dominated by the smaller member — the pinion pits first.
Contact modulus\( 1/E^* = \sum (1-\nu^2)/E \)One number for both materials; ~114 GPa steel on steel.
Load per unit face\( W' = W_tK_v/(b\cos\phi) \)The normal load, not the tangential one, spread across the face.
Mode coupling\( \sigma_H = K\sqrt{\sigma_b} \)Exact for a given tooth system; halving bending buys only \( \sqrt{2} \) on contact.
Utilisation\( \sigma/\sigma_{allow} \)The only fair comparison between two modes with different allowables.
Pitting—Surface fatigue near the pitch line; ends gears far more often than breakage.
