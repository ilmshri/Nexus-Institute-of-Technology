Spur gear design: surface durability

Contact stress by Hertzian logic; pitting as the surface fatigue mode. Bending versus pitting as competing failure budgets. The design meaning of gear micropitting found in an oil sample.

المحاضرة

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Bending versus pitting: which failure budget wears out and which breaks?
  • What does micropitting in an oil report imply about film thickness?

§1 · The other way a tooth dies

The previous lesson sized a pinion so its teeth would not snap off. That check is necessary and it is not sufficient, because the commoner end for an industrial gear is not a broken tooth at all. It is a flank that slowly turns to pitted, spalled, grey-looking metal until the profile no longer resembles an involute, the mesh gets noisy, and someone condemns the gearbox on vibration long before anything actually breaks.

That is surface fatigue — pitting. The mechanism is not bending but repeated Hertzian contact: two curved surfaces pressed together, a very small contact patch, and a subsurface shear stress that cycles once per mesh. Cracks nucleate a little below the surface, run back to it, and lift out a flake. The damage concentrates in a band near the pitch line, typically just below it on the dedendum flank, where the sliding direction reverses and the lubricant film is at its thinnest.

Two failure modes, two different equations, two different allowables. Neither one sizes a gear on its own.

§2 · Hertz, in the form gears actually use

At the pitch point the two flanks can be replaced by two cylinders whose radii are the radii of curvature of the involutes there: \( r_1 = (d_1/2)\sin\phi \) and \( r_2 = (d_2/2)\sin\phi \). For two parallel cylinders pressed together with a load \( W' \) per unit length, the peak contact pressure is

\[ \sigma_H = \sqrt{\frac{W' E^*}{\pi R}}, \qquad \frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{r_1}+\frac{1}{r_2}, \qquad \frac{1}{E^*} = \frac{1-\nu_1^2}{E_1}+\frac{1-\nu_2^2}{E_2} \]

\( R \) is the relative radius — dominated by the smaller member, which is why the pinion is always the one that pits first. \( E^* \) is the contact modulus, a single number standing in for both materials; for steel on steel it is about 114 GPa. The load per unit length is the normal load spread over the face: \( W' = W_tK_v/(b\cos\phi) \).

Note the square root. It is the whole difference between this lesson and the last one.

§3 · Why contact stress is so stubborn

Put the two expressions side by side. Bending was \( \sigma_b = W_tK_v/(bmY) \). Substituting \( d_1 = mN_1 \) into the Hertz expression and collecting terms, everything that varies with the design collapses into the same group \( W_tK_v/(bm) \), and what is left is a constant of the tooth system:

Key relation — the two modes are not independent

\[ \sigma_H = K\sqrt{\sigma_b}, \qquad K = \sqrt{\frac{2E^*Y\,(1+1/i)}{\pi N_1 \sin\phi\cos\phi}} \]

\( K \) depends only on material, tooth count, ratio, pressure angle and form factor — not on module, face width, load or speed. For this drive \( K = 69.5\ \mathrm{MPa}^{1/2} \), and the identity is exact, not approximate.

The consequence is blunt. Any change that halves the bending stress reduces the contact stress only by \( \sqrt{2} \), a factor of 1.41. Quartering the bending stress — a serious redesign — halves the contact stress. Geometry is a weak instrument against pitting, and every step you take to fix bending buys you only half as much, in log terms, against the surface.

This is why the two utilisation curves in the figure have different slopes, why they cross their allowable line at different modules, and why the gear must be sized on whichever crossing comes later.

§4 · What you actually change

Run both checks, always, and let the losing one size the gear. When bending governs, grow the tooth: it is a cubic lever and it works. When contact governs — which it usually does on larger, slower, well-loaded industrial drives — growing the gear is a poor trade, and the real instrument is metallurgy. Through-hardened steel might allow about 620 MPa of contact stress; a carburised and ground surface allows roughly twice that, at no change in size, because surface fatigue strength scales with surface hardness while the tooth's bending strength barely notices.

The working method. One: size on bending, as before, to get a starting module. Two: compute \( r_1, r_2, R \), then \( W' \), then \( \sigma_H \). Three: divide each stress by its own allowable and compare the two utilisations, not the two stresses — they are in different units of danger. Four: if contact governs, price both routes: the module that passes, against the heat treatment that passes. Five: if you harden, re-check bending, because case hardening changes the root's residual stress and its allowable too.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Contact stress on the pinion sized in L1

Problem. Take the previous lesson's design: \( m = 4\ \mathrm{mm} \), \( N_1 = 20 \), ratio \( i = 3 \), \( b = 40\ \mathrm{mm} \), \( \phi = 20^\circ \), \( W_t = 2470\ \mathrm{N} \), \( K_v = 1.996 \), steel on steel with \( E = 207\ \mathrm{GPa} \), \( \nu = 0.3 \). Find: \( E^* \), the two radii of curvature, the relative radius, the load per unit face width, and the contact stress.

Solution. (1) Contact modulus: \( 1/E^* = 2(1-0.3^2)/207 = 0.008792\ \mathrm{GPa^{-1}} \), so \( E^* = 113.7\ \mathrm{GPa} \). (2) \( d_1 = 80\ \mathrm{mm} \), \( d_2 = 240\ \mathrm{mm} \), so \( r_1 = 40\sin20^\circ = 13.68\ \mathrm{mm} \) and \( r_2 = 120\sin20^\circ = 41.04\ \mathrm{mm} \). (3) \( 1/R = 1/13.68 + 1/41.04 \Rightarrow R = 10.26\ \mathrm{mm} \). (4) Normal dynamic load \( W_n = W_tK_v/\cos\phi = (2470)(1.996)/0.9397 = 5245\ \mathrm{N} \), so \( W' = 5245/0.040 = 131.1\ \mathrm{kN/m} \). (5) \( \sigma_H = \sqrt{W'E^*/(\pi R)} = \sqrt{(131\,124)(113.7\times10^9)/(\pi \times 0.010261)} = 680\ \mathrm{MPa} \).

Note that \( R \) came out closer to \( r_1 \) than to \( r_2 \): the small member dominates the relative radius, which is the geometric reason the pinion always pits before the wheel.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Which mode actually sizes this gear

Problem. The bending allowable is 140 MPa and the contact allowable for the through-hardened steel is 620 MPa. The bending stress at \( m = 4 \) was 95.7 MPa and the contact stress is 680 MPa. Find: both utilisations, state which mode governs, and find the module at which each mode just reaches its allowable.

Solution. Bending utilisation \( = 95.7/140 = 0.68 \) — passes. Contact utilisation \( = 680/620 = 1.10 \) — fails. Contact governs, and the design accepted in the previous lesson is not adequate.

Sweeping the module: bending reaches utilisation 1.0 at \( m = 3.44\ \mathrm{mm} \), contact at \( m = 4.31\ \mathrm{mm} \). The gear must be sized on the later crossing, so the next standard module up, \( m = 5 \), is the smallest that satisfies both (utilisations 0.39 and 0.83).

The general lesson is in the two crossings being different at all: comparing raw stresses would have been meaningless, because 95.7 and 680 are measured against different allowables. Utilisation is the only fair comparison.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Buying the margin with heat treatment instead of size

Problem. Rather than growing the gearbox to \( m = 5 \), the alternative is to carburise and grind the \( m = 4 \) pair, raising the contact allowable to about 1300 MPa. Find: the resulting contact utilisation, the factor by which the bending stress would have had to fall to achieve the same contact relief by geometry alone, and one check that must be repeated afterwards.

Solution. (1) Contact utilisation becomes \( 680/1300 = 0.52 \) — comfortably passing, with no change in size.

(2) By geometry alone, reaching \( \sigma_H = 680/(1300/620) = 324\ \mathrm{MPa} \) means reducing contact stress by a factor \( 680/324 = 2.10 \). Since \( \sigma_H = K\sqrt{\sigma_b} \), the bending stress would have to fall by \( 2.10^2 = 4.4 \) times. That is the asymmetry in one number: a factor of two on the surface costs a factor of four on the tooth.

(3) Re-check bending. Case hardening changes the root's allowable and its residual stress state, usually favourably, but a case that is too deep or ends badly at the root fillet can move the crack initiation site rather than remove it. The check that was passing at 0.68 is not automatically still valid against a different material condition.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

For a given tooth system, halving the bending stress reduces the contact stress by what factor?

Both stresses trace back to the same group \( W_tK_v/(bm) \), but bending is linear in it and contact goes as its square root, giving the exact relation \( \sigma_H = K\sqrt{\sigma_b} \) with \( K \) independent of module, face width, load and speed. So a factor of 2 on bending is only \( \sqrt{2} \) on contact — the reason geometry is a weak instrument against pitting.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

Why does the pinion pit before the wheel, other things being equal?

\( 1/R = 1/r_1 + 1/r_2 \) is a reciprocal sum, so the smaller radius dominates it and \( R \) sits closer to \( r_1 \) than to \( r_2 \) — in the worked case 10.26 mm against radii of 13.68 and 41.04. A smaller relative radius means a smaller contact patch and a higher peak pressure. The tangential load is identical on both members, and the pinion also accumulates more stress cycles per unit time, which compounds the same conclusion.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

The contact modulus \( E^* \) for steel on steel is about 114 GPa, roughly half of steel's Young's modulus. Why is it lower than \( E \)?

\( E^* \) is a series combination of the two bodies' compliances, each already softened by the \( (1-\nu^2) \) plane-strain factor. Two identical steel bodies each contribute the same compliance, so \( E^* \) lands near half of \( E \). It is a geometric-elastic bookkeeping term, not a safety factor and not a lubrication term.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Contact stress varies with transmitted load how?

\( \sigma_H = \sqrt{W'E^*/(\pi R)} \), so contact stress goes as the square root of load. A 20% overload raises contact stress by only about 10% — which sounds reassuring and is not, because it also means shedding load is an inefficient way to cure pitting. The same square root that protects you from overload frustrates you in redesign.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Where on the tooth flank does pitting typically concentrate?

The pitch line is where rolling is pure and sliding reverses direction, so the lubricant film is thinnest there and the contact pressure band is at its most damaging; damage usually appears just below it on the dedendum. The root fillet is where bending cracks start, a different mode entirely — distinguishing the two on a failed tooth is the first thing a failure analysis does.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A gearbox is found to be contact-limited. Which remedy gives the most relief for the least change in envelope?

Surface fatigue strength scales with surface hardness, so case hardening can roughly double the contact allowable with no change in size at all. Face width and module both work only through the square root and cost envelope; a 20% face increase buys under 10% on contact stress. Reducing the pressure angle actually makes contact worse, because it shrinks the radii of curvature at the pitch point.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

Why must the two modes be compared as utilisations rather than as stresses?

In the worked case bending is 95.7 MPa against a 140 MPa allowable and contact is 680 MPa against 620 MPa. The contact number is seven times larger and yet it is the one that fails — because surface allowables are far higher than bending allowables. Dividing each by its own allowable is what makes them commensurable, and it is the step most often skipped.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

As gears are made larger and slower for the same transmitted power, which failure mode tends to become governing?

Since \( \sigma_H = K\sqrt{\sigma_b} \), a change that drops bending utilisation by a factor of four drops contact utilisation only by two. Growing a gear therefore retires the bending problem much faster than the pitting problem, and on large slow drives the surface is nearly always what sets the size. That is exactly the crossing pattern in the figure: 3.44 mm for bending, 4.31 mm for contact.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the gearbox that was replaced for the wrong reason. A reduction gearbox on a conveyor drive is condemned after four years on rising vibration. The teardown report records "gear wear" and the unit is replaced like-for-like. The replacement is condemned on the same symptom in three and a half years.

The second teardown is photographed properly, and the flanks tell a specific story: pitting in a narrow band just below the pitch line on the pinion dedendum, the wheel almost unmarked, and every tooth root clean and uncracked. That is surface fatigue on the smaller member, which is where the relative radius is least — the textbook signature, not general wear. Recalculating the original selection shows a bending utilisation near 0.6 and a contact utilisation just above 1.0: the drive had been chosen on a torque rating that reflected tooth strength, and the surface had never been checked separately.

The fix costs nothing in envelope. The same gear geometry is re-specified in a case-hardened and ground grade, roughly doubling the contact allowable, and the drive is still in service. The instructive part is not the metallurgy but the diagnosis: a report that says "wear" closes an investigation, while a report that says "pitting on the pinion dedendum, roots clean" names the failure mode, and naming the mode is what points at the one design number nobody had computed.
