ELE 156 — Electrical Fundamentals for Mechanical Engineers

ELE 156 · Lesson 01 · Charge, current, voltage, and power

Lesson 01Charge, current, voltage, and power

Key points

  • Charge is the quantity, current its flow rate, voltage the potential difference driving it, and power the rate energy is delivered.
  • The water analogy is a scaffold for getting started and is then dropped — voltage is not literally pressure, and pushing it too far misleads.
  • Ohm's law \( V=IR \) links the three, and dissipation follows as \( P=I^2R \).
  • The \( I^2 \) is why current, not voltage, sizes cables and why a loose terminal overheats: resistance rises exactly where the heat then appears.
  • A multimeter measures voltage across and current through — the connection differs, and reversing them is how meters and fuses die.
  • Work the method: identify the quantity, choose the connection, then read — folklore about "testing for power" is not a method.

ELE 156 · Lesson 02 · DC circuit analysis

Lesson 02DC circuit analysis

Key points

  • Most DC networks collapse by series-parallel reduction before any formal analysis is needed.
  • Kirchhoff's current law is charge conservation at a node; the voltage law is energy conservation around a loop.
  • Between them they solve any network the reduction cannot, and they are the basis of every circuit method that follows.
  • Voltage dividers set a reference; current dividers split a branch — recognising them saves most of the algebra.
  • A real source has internal resistance, so terminal voltage falls under load — the reason a battery reads fine open-circuit and sags when it drives something.
  • That sag is what kills battery-fed control circuits at the worst moment, under inrush.

ELE 156 · Lesson 03 · Capacitance and inductance

Lesson 03Capacitance and inductance

Key points

  • Capacitors store energy in an electric field, inductors in a magnetic field — neither dissipates it.
  • Their defining relations are dual: \( I=C\,dV/dt \) and \( V=L\,dI/dt \).
  • Because current cannot change instantly through an inductor, interrupting it forces a large \( dI/dt \) and therefore a large voltage.
  • That is why inductive loads arc their switches and why contactor coils get flyback diodes or snubbers.
  • First-order circuits settle on a time constant — \( \tau=RC \) or \( L/R \) — and are practically complete after about five of them.
  • Time constants explain both control-relay delays and the inrush behaviour of real loads.

ELE 156 · Lesson 04 · AC fundamentals and phasors

Lesson 04AC fundamentals and phasors

Key points

  • AC quantities are sinusoids described by amplitude, frequency and phase.
  • RMS is the equivalent DC value for heating: \( V_{rms}=V_{max}/\sqrt2 \), and every nameplate voltage is an RMS value.
  • Phasors turn calculus into algebra by representing a sinusoid as a rotating vector at one fixed frequency.
  • Impedance extends resistance to AC: resistors dissipate, inductors lag the current, capacitors lead it.
  • Those phase shifts are the whole reason reactive power exists in the next lesson.
  • At 50 Hz a cycle lasts 20 ms — the clock behind relay timing, drive switching, and every protection setting in the plant.

ELE 156 · Lesson 05 · AC power and power factor

Lesson 05AC power and power factor

Key points

  • Real power \( P \) does work, reactive power \( Q \) sustains the magnetic fields, and apparent power \( S \) is what the supply must actually carry.
  • They form a right triangle: \( S^2=P^2+Q^2 \), with \( PF=P/S=\cos\varphi \).
  • Reactive power does no work but still causes current, so it heats cables and consumes transformer and switchgear capacity.
  • That is why utilities bill for poor power factor — the customer is using capacity they are not paying for in kWh.
  • Capacitors supply leading reactive power to cancel the lagging demand of motors, and are sized from the required \( \Delta Q \).
  • Correcting power factor releases real capacity in existing cables and transformers, often deferring a costly upgrade.

ELE 156 · Lesson 06 · Three-phase systems

Lesson 06Three-phase systems

Key points

  • Three-phase supplies three sinusoids 120° apart, connected as wye (star) or delta.
  • In wye, \( V_{line}=\sqrt3V_{phase} \) and line current equals phase current; in delta the relationships swap.
  • Three-phase power is \( \sqrt3V_LI_L\cos\varphi \) and, unlike single phase, arrives at a constant rate rather than pulsating.
  • Constant power delivery is why three-phase motors do not vibrate at twice line frequency the way single-phase machines do.
  • It also carries more power per kilogram of copper, which is the economic reason distribution is three-phase.
  • Industrial plants are three-phase places: the motors, the drives and the distribution all assume it.

ELE 156 · Lesson 07 · Transformers

Lesson 07Transformers

Key points

  • An ideal transformer trades voltage for current at constant apparent power: \( V_1/V_2=N_1/N_2 \), \( I_1/I_2=N_2/N_1 \), \( S_1\approx S_2 \).
  • It transforms AC only — there is no transformer action without a changing flux.
  • Real losses split into copper losses, which vary with load squared, and core losses, which are essentially constant whenever it is energised.
  • That split is why a lightly loaded transformer can be inefficient and why leaving one energised has a standing cost.
  • The nameplate carries kVA rating, voltage ratio, impedance percentage and vector group — impedance sets both voltage regulation and fault current.
  • On a plant one-line diagram, transformers are where voltage levels and available fault current both change.

ELE 156 · Lesson 08 · The induction motor

Lesson 08The induction motor

Key points

  • Three-phase stator currents create a rotating magnetic field turning at synchronous speed \( N_s=120f/p \).
  • The rotor must lag that field to induce current and produce torque, so slip \( s=(N_s-N)/N_s \) is always positive under load.
  • A loaded induction motor can never reach synchronous speed — at zero slip there is no induction and no torque.
  • The torque-speed curve runs from a modest starting torque, through breakdown torque, to a steep, stable operating region near synchronous speed.
  • Rated operation sits on that steep falling side, which is why speed barely changes as load varies.
  • Nameplate literacy is the practical skill: rated power, current, service factor, insulation class and efficiency all constrain how the machine may be applied.

ELE 156 · Lesson 09 · Synchronous machines and standby generation

Lesson 09Synchronous machines and standby generation

Key points

  • A synchronous machine locks to the supply frequency, so its speed is fixed by \( f \) and pole count with no slip at all.
  • Its field is separately excited, which means reactive power can be controlled independently of real power.
  • Over-excitation makes it supply reactive power, so a synchronous machine can correct plant power factor as well as generate.
  • Standby diesel sets are the common industrial encounter: sized for starting kVA, not just running kW.
  • Paralleling requires matching voltage, frequency, phase sequence and phase angle — closing out of synchronism is violently destructive.
  • Those four conditions are non-negotiable and are what synchronising equipment exists to enforce.

ELE 156 · Lesson 10 · Motor starting, protection, and VFDs

Lesson 10Motor starting, protection, and VFDs

Key points

  • Direct-on-line starting is simplest and draws six to eight times full-load current, which is what limits its use on weak supplies.
  • Star-delta starting cuts starting current and torque to about a third — acceptable only if the load can accelerate on reduced torque.
  • Overload relays protect the motor thermally, and trip class sets how long they tolerate starting current.
  • Short-circuit protection and overload protection are different jobs with different devices; one does not substitute for the other.
  • A VFD varies frequency and voltage together to hold flux constant, giving speed control with full torque and a soft start.
  • Drives bring their own problems: fast switching edges cause shaft currents and bearing damage unless the motor is insulated, bonded or filtered.

ELE 156 · Lesson 11 · Electrical safety and measurements

Lesson 11Electrical safety and measurements

Key points

  • Two hazards, not one: shock — current through the body — and arc flash, a radiant and pressure event that needs no contact at all.
  • Current, not voltage, injures; the path through the body and the duration decide the outcome.
  • Lockout-tagout means isolate, lock, tag, and then prove dead with a tester checked against a known source before and after.
  • Testing the tester is the step that turns a procedure into an actual verification.
  • Insulation resistance testing applies a high DC voltage to find degradation before it becomes a fault — trend it rather than reading it once.
  • A clamp meter reads current without breaking the circuit, which is the mechanical engineer's most useful electrical measurement.
  • Know the scope boundary: a mechanical engineer must read the machine's electrical behaviour competently and hand the work itself to a qualified electrical person.
