MFG 105 — Manufacturing Processes I — Casting & Bulk Forming

MFG 105 · Lesson 01 · The manufacturing plant: process taxonomy and material flow

Lesson 01The manufacturing plant: process taxonomy and material flow

Key points

  • Manufacturing reduces to six families: casting, forming, machining, joining, powder processing and finishing.
  • Primary processes create the shape; secondary processes refine it. Almost every real part is a chain, not a single step.
  • Reading that chain — melt, cast, heat treat, machine, join, finish — tells you where cost, lead time and quality risk actually sit.
  • Material handling and inspection add no value at all yet own most of the lead time.
  • The taxonomy is a diagnostic map: it lets an engineer walk any unfamiliar plant and place what they see.
  • It also frames every later selection decision, because the families have fundamentally different tolerance, volume and cost behaviour.

MFG 105 · Lesson 02 · Metal casting I — solidification, gating, and riser design

Lesson 02Metal casting I — solidification, gating, and riser design

Key points

  • Casting is a controlled thermal collapse: pour, fill, freeze, and manage the shrinkage that follows.
  • The gating system is Bernoulli with consequences — a badly designed sprue aspirates air and entrains oxide into the casting.
  • Chvorinov's rule runs the foundry: solidification time \( t=C(V/A)^n \) with \( n\approx2 \).
  • It is the volume-to-area ratio that matters, not size — a thick section freezes last wherever it sits.
  • Metal shrinks three times: liquid contraction, solidification contraction, and solid contraction. Only the first two are fed by a riser.
  • A riser therefore must freeze after the section it feeds, which is a direct application of the same \( V/A \) rule.

MFG 105 · Lesson 02 · Metal casting I — solidification, gating, and riser design · fundamentals

Assumed knowledge

What this lesson assumes

  • Latent heat of solidification (Thermodynamics I) — the energy the mould must remove. Chvorinov's rule is presented here as an empirical result with its volume-to-area reasoning given in full; Heat Transfer supplies the conduction derivation in Year 2.

MFG 105 · Lesson 03 · Metal casting II — practice, quality, and defects

Lesson 03Metal casting II — practice, quality, and defects

Key points

  • Pattern allowances build in what the casting will lose: shrinkage, machining stock, draft and distortion.
  • Moulding sand is a system — silica, clay binder and water — balancing strength, permeability and collapsibility.
  • Fluidity limits how far metal runs before freezing; too little gives misruns, or cold shuts where two fronts meet without fusing.
  • Gas porosity follows Sievert's law: dissolved gas goes as the square root of partial pressure, so degassing and covering the melt work.
  • Shrinkage porosity is a feeding failure, not a gas problem, and the two look different under magnification.
  • Diagnosing a defect means identifying which mechanism produced it, because the remedies are completely different.

MFG 105 · Lesson 04 · Continuous casting of steel

Lesson 04Continuous casting of steel

Key points

  • Continuous casting runs ladle → tundish → water-cooled mould → spray zone → withdrawal, producing strand instead of discrete castings.
  • A solid shell forms in the mould and thickens as the square root of time — the same parabolic law as any diffusion-controlled growth.
  • Metallurgical length is where the strand is finally solid through, and it sets how long the machine must be.
  • Casting speed and metallurgical length trade directly: faster casting pushes the liquid core further down the machine.
  • Breakout — the shell tearing and releasing liquid steel — is the failure this whole design guards against.
  • Mould powder lubricates, insulates and absorbs inclusions, which is why it is a process variable rather than a consumable detail.

MFG 105 · Lesson 05 · Casting processes survey: sand to die casting

Lesson 05Casting processes survey: sand to die casting

Key points

  • Casting processes sort along one axis: expendable mould versus permanent mould.
  • Expendable moulds allow complex shapes and undercuts; permanent moulds give better tolerance, finish and rate.
  • Shell moulding buys finish and dimensional control over green sand; investment casting buys the finest detail at the highest cost.
  • Die casting is fast and precise but limited to lower-melting alloys, because die life collapses with temperature.
  • Trapped air in high-pressure die casting causes porosity that rules the part out of heat treatment and welding.
  • Choose by volume, tolerance, alloy melting point and section complexity — in that order, not by unit price alone.

MFG 105 · Lesson 06 · Fundamentals of metal forming and workability

Lesson 06Fundamentals of metal forming and workability

Key points

  • Forming is governed by flow stress, and the flow curve \( \sigma=K\varepsilon^n \) describes how the metal hardens as it deforms.
  • The strain-hardening exponent \( n \) measures how much stretch a sheet can take before it necks.
  • Hot, warm and cold working are defined by homologous temperature, not by whether the metal glows.
  • Above roughly half the absolute melting temperature, recrystallization keeps pace with deformation so the metal does not harden.
  • Cold working keeps climbing the flow curve, giving strength and finish but needing far more force.
  • Hot working is strain-rate sensitive; friction and lubrication set both the force required and how uniformly the material flows.

MFG 105 · Lesson 07 · Rolling of metals

Lesson 07Rolling of metals

Key points

  • Rolling begins with the bite condition: the rolls only draw the stock in if friction is sufficient, giving a maximum draft \( d_{max}=\mu^2R \).
  • Check the bite before anything else — a pass that cannot be gripped is not a pass.
  • Contact length follows from roll radius and draft, and force, torque and power follow from it.
  • Larger rolls grip more but need more force, which is why backup rolls exist in four-high stands.
  • Hot and cold rolling make different products, not the same product at different settings: hot gives scale and loose tolerance, cold gives finish, strength and tight gauge.
  • Defects map to causes — wavy edges and centre buckle come from roll deflection and non-uniform elongation across the width.

MFG 105 · Lesson 08 · Forging

Lesson 08Forging

Key points

  • Forging spans open-die, impression-die and flashless, in increasing order of tooling cost and dimensional control.
  • Upsetting analysis gives the force including friction, which causes barrelling and drives force up sharply as the billet gets thinner.
  • The force curve rises steeply at the end of the stroke, which is why press capacity is set by the last few millimetres.
  • Flash is not waste: its thin, cooled land restricts outflow and forces the metal to fill the die cavity.
  • Grain flow follows the shape, giving a forged part directional strength a machined-from-solid part cannot have.
  • That aligned flow is the real reason critical parts — crankshafts, hooks, connecting rods — are forged.

MFG 105 · Lesson 09 · Extrusion and wire drawing

Lesson 09Extrusion and wire drawing

Key points

  • Extrusion and drawing both push metal through a die, in opposite stress states: extrusion pushes in compression, drawing pulls in tension.
  • That difference caps drawing — the exit stress must stay below the material's yield strength or the wire simply breaks.
  • Extrusion has no such limit, so it achieves far larger reductions in a single pass.
  • Extrusion ratio sets the strain, and empirical strain formulas account for redundant work and friction.
  • Drawing therefore proceeds in many small passes through a tandem line, with annealing when the metal work-hardens too far.
  • The survival check — computed draw stress against yield — is the design step that decides pass schedule.

MFG 105 · Lesson 10 · Powder metallurgy

Lesson 10Powder metallurgy

Key points

  • Powder metallurgy is three steps: powder production, compaction and sintering.
  • Sintering bonds particles by diffusion below the melting point — the part never becomes liquid.
  • Density is the controlling quality variable: strength, ductility and fatigue life all track it.
  • Residual porosity is unavoidable, so a PM part is typically around 96% dense and never quite matches wrought properties.
  • PM wins on high-volume near-net shapes, on controlled porosity for self-lubricating bearings and filters, and on materials that cannot be melted together.
  • It is a volume process: the tooling cost only makes sense across large quantities.

MFG 105 · Lesson 11 · Process selection and cost fundamentals

Lesson 11Process selection and cost fundamentals

Key points

  • Process selection is a cost structure question: fixed cost (tooling) plus variable cost (per part).
  • A low-fixed, high-variable process wins at low volume; a high-fixed, low-variable one wins once volume is large enough.
  • Break-even volume is where the two total-cost lines cross, and it is the number that decides the process.
  • The worked comparison — $2,000 + $8/part against $20,000 + $2/part — crosses at 3,000 parts.
  • Below that the cheap tooling wins outright; above it, decisively the other way.
  • Break-even is necessary but not sufficient: tolerance capability, lead time, material compatibility and supply risk can all override it.
