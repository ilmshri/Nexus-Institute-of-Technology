THM 155 — Thermodynamics I

THM 155 · Lesson 01 · Systems, properties, and state

Lesson 01Systems, properties, and state

Key points

  • Choosing the system and drawing its boundary is the first and most consequential step — every term in every later balance depends on it.
  • A closed system passes energy but not mass; an open system (control volume) passes both.
  • Intensive properties (temperature, pressure) do not scale with size; extensive ones (volume, energy) do. Dividing an extensive property by mass makes it specific, and intensive.
  • A property has a value at a state and no memory of how it got there — that is what distinguishes it from work and heat.
  • The state postulate: two independent intensive properties fix the state of a simple compressible substance.
  • Fix units and sign conventions once and never renegotiate them — most first-law errors are sign errors, not physics errors.

THM 155 · Lesson 02 · Pure substance properties and steam tables

Lesson 02Pure substance properties and steam tables

Key points

  • A pure substance lives in three regions: compressed liquid, the saturated mixture under the dome, and superheated vapour.
  • Inside the dome, pressure and temperature are not independent — they are locked together, so a third property is needed to fix the state.
  • Quality \( x \) supplies it, and every property interpolates linearly: \( y=y_f+x(y_g-y_f) \).
  • Outside the dome, pressure and temperature are independent again and the superheat or compressed-liquid tables apply.
  • Turbines want superheat because expansion into the wet region erodes blades; condensate pumps want subcooling to avoid cavitation.
  • Steam-table fluency is a working method: identify the region first, then choose the table, then interpolate — in that order.

THM 155 · Lesson 03 · Work and heat

Lesson 03Work and heat

Key points

  • Boundary work is \( W=\int P\,dV \), the area under the process curve on a P–v diagram.
  • That area depends on the path, which is why work is a path function and not a property.
  • Two processes between identical end states can deliver different work — the clearest demonstration that "the state" does not determine "the journey".
  • Heat is energy in transit across a boundary because of a temperature difference; a body contains internal energy, never "heat".
  • Both work and heat are therefore \( \delta \) quantities, not \( d \) quantities — an inexact differential with no state function behind it.
  • Read work off the diagram before computing it: the sign and rough magnitude should be obvious from the picture.

THM 155 · Lesson 04 · First law: closed systems

Lesson 04First law: closed systems

Key points

  • The closed-system energy balance is one statement: \( Q-W=\Delta U \), with kinetic and potential terms added only when they matter.
  • Use internal energy for a constant-volume process and enthalpy for a constant-pressure one — enthalpy already contains the \( PV \) work.
  • That is why \( c_v \) governs the rigid tank and \( c_p \) the piston-cylinder.
  • Heat the same substance equally in both and the rigid tank ends hotter, by exactly the ratio \( c_p/c_v \).
  • The reason is physical, not algebraic: the piston-cylinder spends part of the heat doing work against its surroundings.
  • Work the method in a fixed order — system, process, property table, balance — and the arithmetic stops being the hard part.

THM 155 · Lesson 05 · First law: control volumes

Lesson 05First law: control volumes

Key points

  • The steady-flow energy equation carries mass across the boundary, so flow work appears and enthalpy becomes the natural variable.
  • Steady flow means nothing inside the control volume changes with time — mass in equals mass out, and stored energy is constant.
  • Five devices reduce to one line each: nozzle and diffuser (kinetic energy), turbine and compressor (work), throttle (isenthalpic), heat exchanger (heat), pump (work on a liquid).
  • Reading a device means deciding which terms are zero before computing anything.
  • A throttle conserves enthalpy while destroying availability — a device that looks free on the first law and is not.
  • This one equation carries the rest of the degree: every cycle, exchanger and compressor in later courses is an application of it.

THM 155 · Lesson 06 · Transient flow processes

Lesson 06Transient flow processes

Key points

  • Steady flow fails whenever the contents of the control volume are themselves changing — filling, emptying, starting up, blowing down.
  • The transient balance keeps the storage term that steady flow discards, so the answer depends on initial and final states as well as on what crossed the boundary.
  • Charging an evacuated, insulated, rigid tank has a striking result: \( T_2=kT_{line} \), so the gas ends up hotter than the line supplying it.
  • Nothing was heated — flow work done pushing gas in becomes internal energy inside the tank.
  • This is why receivers and cylinders warm on filling, a real and often-missed effect.
  • The working method is to recognise honestly when steady flow no longer applies, which is the whole skill of this lesson.

THM 155 · Lesson 07 · Second law and machines

Lesson 07Second law and machines

Key points

  • The Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements say the same thing twice: no engine converts all heat to work, and no refrigerator moves heat uphill unaided.
  • Thermal efficiency is what you want over what you pay for; a COP is the same ratio for a device whose output is heat moved, so it exceeds 1 without contradiction.
  • The Carnot limit \( \eta=1-T_C/T_H \) uses absolute temperatures and depends on nothing but the two reservoirs.
  • It is a ceiling no design, material or vendor claim can pass — the fastest way to test an extraordinary efficiency claim.
  • It also shows where efficiency comes from: raise \( T_H \) or lower \( T_C \); nothing else in the machine matters as much.
  • Refrigerators and heat pumps are the same reversed cycle, differing only in which side of it you are paid for.

THM 155 · Lesson 08 · Entropy

Lesson 08Entropy

Key points

  • Entropy is a property, so it has a value at a state and a change between states independent of path.
  • The balance is \( \Delta S=\int\delta Q/T+S_{gen} \) with \( S_{gen}\ge0 \), zero only for a reversible process.
  • A system's entropy can fall — the entropy of system plus surroundings cannot.
  • \( S_{gen} \) is the bookkeeping of irreversibility: friction, unrestrained expansion, mixing, and heat crossing a finite temperature difference all generate it.
  • On a T–s diagram, area under a reversible path is heat, and an isentropic process is a vertical line.
  • That vertical line is the reference every real device is measured against in the next lesson.

THM 155 · Lesson 09 · Isentropic efficiency

Lesson 09Isentropic efficiency

Key points

  • Isentropic efficiency compares a real device with the isentropic one operating between the same pressures.
  • The ratio inverts between work-producing and work-absorbing devices: actual over ideal for a turbine, ideal over actual for a compressor or pump.
  • The "lost" work is not lost from the universe — it reappears as internal energy in the stream, which is why a real turbine's exit is hotter than the ideal one's.
  • On an h–s diagram the real state sits to the right of the ideal, and the horizontal gap is the entropy generated.
  • This single number converts textbook cycles into machines that can be specified and bought.
  • It is also the number to demand from a vendor: a cycle efficiency quoted without component efficiencies is unverifiable.

THM 155 · Lesson 10 · Exergy first contact

Lesson 10Exergy first contact

Key points

  • Exergy is the maximum useful work obtainable as a system comes to equilibrium with its surroundings — the dead state.
  • Energy is conserved; exergy is destroyed. That difference is why a first-law audit can look balanced while the plant wastes opportunity.
  • Destruction is quantified by \( X_{destroyed}=T_0S_{gen} \), tying this directly to Lesson 8's entropy generation.
  • Throttling is the clean example: enthalpy unchanged, exergy gone.
  • Heat crossing a large temperature difference is thermally comfortable and exergetically expensive.
  • This is a first contact, not the full method — Thermodynamics II applies it to real utility systems.

THM 155 · Lesson 11 · Gas power cycle sketches

Lesson 11Gas power cycle sketches

Key points

  • Otto, Diesel and Brayton are the three idealized gas cycles, distinguished by how heat is added: constant volume, constant pressure, and constant pressure in steady flow.
  • On the Otto P–v loop the enclosed area is the net work per cycle, and compression ratio is what sets efficiency.
  • Mean effective pressure converts that area into a single equivalent pressure — the fair way to compare engines of different displacement.
  • Back-work ratio \( BWR=w_{compressor}/w_{turbine} \) is small for a steam plant and large for a gas turbine.
  • That large back-work ratio is why gas-turbine component efficiencies matter so much more than a steam plant's.
  • These are literacy sketches, deliberately ideal; Thermodynamics II supplies the real cycles with losses attached.
