MTH 101 — Engineering Mathematics I

Lesson 01 · Functions, units, and engineering magnitudes

  • Every physical quantity carries a unit, and units obey algebra exactly as numbers do.
  • Dimensional homogeneity: both sides of a physical equation must carry identical dimensions, and the arguments of \( \sin \), \( \ln \) and \( e^x \) must be dimensionless. Checking this kills roughly half of all calculation errors before they are made.
  • A function is a machine's behaviour written down — the pump curve \( H(Q) \), the torque curve \( T(\omega) \), the cooling curve \( T(t) \). The domain is physical, not decorative: beyond runout flow the curve is forbidden, not merely undefined.
  • Estimate before you calculate. If a suitcase-sized transfer pump computes to 300 kW, the arithmetic is wrong — and you know it before re-checking.
  • Working method: substitute units rather than numbers and confirm both sides match; convert every input to base SI before any arithmetic; convert the answer back to trade units and test it against an order-of-magnitude estimate.
  • Two traps with names: the gauge trap (gauge and absolute pressure differ by one atmosphere) and the prefix trap (kPa meeting Pa, m³/h meeting m³/s in the same line).

Lesson 02 · Limits and continuity

  • A limit describes what a quantity approaches, which is not always what it equals at the point itself.
  • Most plant quantities are continuous, but not all — some genuinely jump, and pretending otherwise hides real behaviour.
  • One-sided limits are the honest description of a switching event: a relay closing, a valve seating, a pump cutting in.
  • The ratio \( \Delta y/\Delta x \) as \( \Delta x \to 0 \) is why limits matter at all — it is the derivative in embryo.
  • Evaluation order: substitute first; if that gives \( 0/0 \), factor, rationalise or expand and try again; for behaviour at large argument, compare the dominant terms.

Lesson 03 · The derivative as a rate

  • The derivative is the instantaneous rate of change — the average rate over an interval that has been allowed to shrink to nothing.
  • Read the sign and the magnitude before the formula: sign gives direction of drift, magnitude gives urgency.
  • From a model: differentiate the expression. From data: take finite differences — but note that shrinking the interval amplifies measurement noise, so the smallest interval is rarely the best one.
  • The units of a derivative are always (units of the output) divided by (units of the input). This alone identifies what a rate physically is.

Lesson 04 · Differentiation rules and the chain rule

  • Constant-multiple, sum, power, product and quotient rules: each is proved once from the limit definition and then used for life.
  • Chain rule: plant variables cause one another in chains — valve position sets flow, flow sets level, level sets pressure. Multiply the local sensitivities along the chain.
  • Relative rates are the engineer's form: divide a derivative by its own function. For the fan law \( P = kN^3 \), logarithmic differentiation gives \( dP/P = 3\,dN/N \) — a 2% speed increase costs about 6% more power.
  • That single result is why trimming fan or pump speed slightly is usually the cheapest energy project available.
  • Choose the rule from the structure of the expression before touching symbols.

Lesson 05 · Exponentials, logarithms, and growth/decay

  • \( e^x \) is its own derivative; exponential behaviour is what happens whenever the rate of change is proportional to the amount currently present.
  • The plant's standard curve is decay toward an offset, not toward zero: something approaches a final value \( y_\infty \) rather than vanishing.
  • The time constant \( \tau \) is the whole story: about 63% of the journey is complete after one \( \tau \), 95% after three, 99% after five.
  • Logarithms are how you solve for time — "when will it reach X" is a logarithm, every time.
  • Transient protocol: identify the initial value, the final value, and \( \tau \). With those three, the curve is fully determined.

Lesson 06 · Extrema, curve behavior, and optimization

  • Curves turn where the derivative vanishes; the second derivative says whether the turn is a maximum or a minimum.
  • Optimization recipe: express the objective in one variable, differentiate, solve for stationary points — then check the endpoints.
  • Checking endpoints is not a formality. Constrained optima frequently sit at a limit rather than at a turning point, and skipping the check is the classic way to report a wrong optimum confidently.
  • The hard part is translation, not calculus: turning a stated goal into an honest objective function with its real constraints.

Lesson 07 · The integral as accumulation

  • The derivative asks how fast; the integral asks how much in total. They are the two halves of the subject.
  • \( V = \int_0^T Q(t)\,dt \) — every totalizer in the plant is performing this calculation continuously.
  • Fundamental Theorem: accumulation and differentiation undo each other, so \( \int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a) \). Find any antiderivative and evaluate at the two ends.
  • Area under a curve is a physical total. Read the units of the area to know which total it is: rate × time is quantity, force × distance is work.

Lesson 08 · Integration techniques

  • Substitution is the chain rule run backwards — look for a function and its own derivative sitting in the same integrand.
  • Integration by parts is the product rule run backwards: \( \int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du \). Choose \( u \) to be the factor that simplifies when differentiated.
  • Know when to stop. Many perfectly ordinary integrals have no closed form, and a numerical answer is a legitimate engineering answer, not a defeat.

Lesson 09 · Applications: areas, volumes, work, and averages

  • Average value is the integral spread evenly across the interval. Every KPI beginning with the word "average" is this calculation — whether or not it was computed honestly.
  • Averaging a few spot readings taken at convenient times is not this calculation, and you can now say precisely why.
  • RMS is the average that heats. Electrical heating goes as the square of current, so the meaningful average for an alternating quantity is the root of the mean of the square — which is why vibration severity standards quote RMS velocity.
  • Work by integration when force varies along the path: \( W = \int F\,dx \). Emptying a tank is the signature case, because each layer must rise a different height.
  • Choosing the right average — mean, RMS, or time-weighted — is a field decision with different answers, not a matter of taste.

Lesson 10 · First-order differential equations

  • An equation containing its own rate of change describes nearly every tank, thermal mass and mixing problem you will meet.
  • Separation of variables: gather each variable on its own side, integrate both, then apply the initial condition to fix the constant.
  • Put the equation in the standard form \( \dot{y} = -(y - y_\infty)/\tau \) and the time constant can be read straight off the coefficients — tank volume over volumetric flow for a flushed tank, thermal mass over loss coefficient for a cooling body.
  • You can often read the dynamics without solving anything: the final value and the time constant answer most practical questions.
  • Modelling method: start from a balance statement — rate in minus rate out — and express each rate in terms of the state variable.

Lesson 11 · Taylor series and approximation

  • Every smooth function is locally a polynomial. That is the entire idea, and it is what makes linearization defensible rather than lazy.
  • The classic small approximations — \( \sin x \approx x \), \( (1+x)^n \approx 1 + nx \), \( e^x \approx 1 + x \) — are all the same theorem truncated after one term.
  • The remainder term is not optional bookkeeping: it is how you state the size of the error you just agreed to accept.
  • A linearization you can defend comes with its range of validity attached. Without that range, it is a guess wearing a formula.

PHY 107 — Engineering Physics I — Mechanics

Lesson 01 · Measurement, units, and vectors

  • A measurement is a contract: a value, a unit, and an honest statement of precision. Significant figures are that statement, not decoration.
  • Vectors carry direction; scalars do not. Which one a quantity is decides how it may be added.
  • Two products, two meanings. The dot product returns a scalar and answers "how much of this lies along that" — projection, work. The cross product returns a vector and answers "what axis does this turn about" — torque, area.
  • Working method: resolve into components, add componentwise, recombine at the end. Never add magnitudes of vectors that point differently.

Lesson 02 · Kinematics in one dimension

  • Three siblings chained by calculus: position \( x(t) \) says where, velocity \( v = dx/dt \) says how fast, acceleration \( a = dv/dt \) says how the velocity itself is changing.
  • The constant-acceleration toolkit — \( v = v_0 + at \), \( x = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 at^2 \), \( v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x \) — and the last one is the tool when time is not asked for and not known.
  • Signs carry the physics. Choose a positive direction once and keep it; deceleration is not a separate concept, only acceleration pointing against the velocity.

Lesson 03 · Kinematics in two dimensions

  • Two-dimensional motion is two independent motions sharing one clock. Horizontal and vertical are separate accounts, and time is the only entry they share.
  • Projectiles: constant velocity horizontally, constant acceleration \( g \) vertically. Solve whichever axis has enough information for time, then carry that time across.
  • Relative motion is vector addition of velocities — the reason a crosswind changes a heading but not an airspeed.
  • Working method: two columns, one per axis, filled in independently.

Lesson 04 · Newton's laws of motion

  • First law: velocity is constant unless a net force acts. So anything at rest or cruising steadily has balanced forces, and the engineering question is always "balanced by what?"
  • Second law: \( \mathbf{F}_{net} = m\mathbf{a} \) — the vector sum of everything acting produces acceleration in its own direction, scaled inversely by the mass.
  • Third law: forces come in equal and opposite pairs acting on different bodies. Pairs acting on the same body never cancel, because they do not exist.
  • Mass versus weight, permanently: mass is in kilograms and is a property of the object; weight is the gravitational force \( W = mg \) in newtons and is local to the gravity producing it.

Lesson 05 · Applying Newton's laws

  • Friction joins the free-body diagram: static friction up to its threshold, kinetic friction once sliding starts.
  • Ropes and pulleys give shared fates: bodies connected by an inextensible rope share the magnitude of their acceleration, which is what makes the simultaneous equations solvable.
  • Friction is not only a thief. It is the transmitter of motion — the reason a driven wheel moves a vehicle and a belt turns a pulley at all.
  • Working method: one free-body diagram per body, axes aligned with the motion, then solve the set together.

Lesson 06 · Work and energy

  • Work is energy in transit by force: \( W = Fd\cos\theta \), where only the component of force along the motion counts. Force perpendicular to motion does no work.
  • The work–energy theorem — net work equals the change in kinetic energy — shortcuts dynamics whenever the question does not mention time.
  • \( KE = \tfrac12 mv^2 \). That squared velocity matters more than any other exponent in safety: doubling speed quadruples the energy to be absorbed.
  • Power \( P = W/t \), in watts, is what actually sizes machines — a motor is bought for its rate of doing work, not its total work.

Lesson 07 · Potential energy and conservation

  • Potential energy is stored work: gravitational \( mgh \), elastic \( \tfrac12 kx^2 \).
  • Conservation: with no friction, kinetic plus potential energy is constant. The bookkeeping is between states — before and after — and never needs the path.
  • \( v = \sqrt{2gh} \) answers speed-from-height in a single line, with mass cancelling entirely.
  • With friction the ledger still balances. Nothing is lost, only transferred: the loss term is the friction force times the distance over which it acted.

Lesson 08 · Momentum, impulse, and collisions

  • Momentum \( p = mv \) is motion's currency, and the second law's deeper statement is that force is the rate of change of momentum.
  • Impulse is force integrated over contact time, and equals the change in momentum. This is why crumple zones and longer stopping times reduce force for the same speed change.
  • Momentum is conserved for an isolated system whether or not the collision is gentle.
  • Collisions: momentum is always kept; kinetic energy may not be. Elastic collisions keep both; perfectly inelastic ones stick together and lose the most energy available.

Lesson 09 · Rotation I: kinematics and torque

  • The angular vocabulary mirrors the linear one: angle \( \theta \), angular velocity \( \omega = d\theta/dt \), angular acceleration \( \alpha \). Radians are the natural unit because they make arc length \( s = \theta r \) work with no conversion factor.
  • The rolling connection \( v = \omega r \) and \( a_t = \alpha r \) runs every belt, chain and gear train you will meet.
  • Torque is force's rotational agent — force times perpendicular distance from the axis. On a belt drive the torque transmitted is the tension difference times radius, because both runs pull in opposition.
  • The constant-\( \alpha \) toolkit is the Lesson 2 toolkit with letters swapped.

Lesson 10 · Rotation II: dynamics and angular momentum

  • Moment of inertia is rotation's mass, but unlike mass it depends on where the material sits, not merely how much there is.
  • A spinning body banks kinetic energy \( KE = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 \), and the squared \( \omega \) makes speed the cheapest way to store it — the flywheel's whole principle.
  • Angular momentum \( L = I\omega \) is momentum's rotational sibling, conserved whenever no external torque acts. Pulling mass inward lowers \( I \) and must raise \( \omega \).
  • \( \Sigma \tau = I\alpha \) is the rotational second law, and the working method is identical to the linear one.

Lesson 11 · Oscillations first contact

  • The restoring recipe: a force proportional to displacement and directed against it produces simple harmonic motion. The spring–mass system \( F = -kx \) is the archetype.
  • \( \omega_n = \sqrt{k/m} \): stiffness accelerates the cycle, mass slows it. Crucially the natural frequency is a property of the machine, not of whatever disturbs it.
  • The pendulum repeats the recipe with gravity as the spring: \( T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g} \), with mass absent entirely.
  • Signature quantities: period \( T = 2\pi/\omega_n \), frequency \( f = 1/T \) in hertz, and energy sloshing between kinetic and potential twice per cycle.
  • Resonance, previewed: driving a machine near its own natural frequency is how machines shake themselves apart, and is why this lesson exists in a mechanical curriculum.

DRW 102 — Engineering Drawing & CAD

Lesson 01 · Why drawings rule the plant

  • A drawing is not a picture of a part; it is a contract about one — binding the designer's intent, the fabricator's obligation, the inspector's acceptance criteria, and later the maintenance engineer's only honest witness to what was built.
  • The revision letter is the most important character on the sheet. Torque a coupling to Rev B when the shop built Rev D and the bolts may be the wrong length.
  • Work only from the revision the document register calls current, and treat uncontrolled prints — the ones taped inside panel doors — as rumours.
  • As-built claims decay from the day they are stamped. Verify the two or three dimensions that matter against the metal before cutting steel, and red-line the drawing back to document control when reality wins.

Lesson 02 · Projection theory and orthographic views

  • Orthographic projection flattens three dimensions honestly: each view looks along one axis, so every view measures truly in its own plane.
  • First angle versus third angle changes where the views are placed, and the title-block symbol tells you which convention is in force. Reading a first-angle drawing as third-angle mirrors the part.
  • Choose the minimum set of views that shows the function, and put the most descriptive view as the front.

Lesson 03 · Isometric and pictorial views

  • Pictorials are for communication, not measurement — they show a reader what the part looks like, and are not the document a machinist works from.
  • They mislead predictably: in isometric, angles are not true angles and only edges along the three principal axes are to scale.
  • Freehand sketching is the engineer's fastest tool. A clear sketch on a scrap of paper settles more site arguments than any model.

Lesson 04 · Sections and conventions

  • A section cuts the part open on paper so interior features can be dimensioned properly instead of guessed at through hidden lines.
  • The section family: full, half, offset, revolved, removed and broken-out — each answers a different question about what needs exposing.
  • Conventions that lie honestly: ribs and webs are not hatched when cut along their length, and fasteners are shown uncut. The drawing departs from strict geometry deliberately, because the strictly correct view would read as a solid block and mislead.

Lesson 05 · Dimensioning practice

  • Dimensions carry function. Dimension what the part must achieve, not merely what is convenient to measure on the model.
  • Chains accumulate tolerance and baselines do not: a chain of five dimensions stacks five tolerances into the last gap, which is how parts pass every individual check and still fail to assemble.
  • Callouts have a grammar — diameters, radii, counterbores, depths and threads each have a defined form, and using it removes the need for a note explaining what you meant.

Lesson 06 · Tolerances, fits, and limits

  • Tolerance is a purchase. Every tightened band costs machining time, tooling and scrap, so specify the loosest tolerance that still works.
  • The ISO fit system names the intent: clearance fits always have a gap, interference fits never do, and transition fits may go either way within their limits.
  • Hole-basis is the usual default, because it is cheaper to vary a shaft than to vary a reamed hole.
  • Read limits like a fitter: convert the fit code to actual maximum and minimum sizes before deciding whether a shaft will enter a bore.

Lesson 07 · GD&T essentials

  • Coordinate dimensioning alone cannot express what a functional part actually requires — it constrains points, not relationships, and it makes the tolerance zone a square when the function wants a circle.
  • Datums are the measurement contract: they state where the part is held and in what order, so that the designer, machinist and inspector all measure from the same place.
  • Position, form and orientation controls say what the feature must be, independently of how it happens to be measured.
  • Runout is the rotating machine's own control — it asks the question a spinning shaft actually poses, which is how far the surface moves as it turns.

Lesson 08 · Fasteners, welds, and surface symbols

  • Symbols are high-density engineering language: one weld symbol replaces a paragraph, and reading it wrongly builds the wrong joint.
  • Thread callouts state form, diameter, pitch and class of fit; weld symbols state process, size, length, spacing and which side of the joint.
  • Surface finish is a functional requirement, not a cosmetic one — it governs sealing, fatigue life and friction, and it is specified where it matters rather than everywhere.

Lesson 09 · Parametric CAD: parts

  • Parametric means rebuildable. A model is a recipe of features and constraints, not a frozen shape, so changing a driving dimension rebuilds everything downstream.
  • Design intent is the recipe's grammar: constrain the model so that the relationships you actually care about survive a change. Poor intent produces a model that breaks the moment a real dimension moves.
  • From model to manufacture, the geometry must carry the information the process needs — draft, radii and access included.

Lesson 10 · Parametric CAD: assemblies and drawings

  • Assembly constraints are kinematics in disguise: mates that remove degrees of freedom describe how the mechanism is allowed to move, and over-constraining hides real interference.
  • The bill of materials is the assembly's ledger. If it is wrong, the right drawing still procures the wrong parts.
  • Drawings generated from models are still a craft. The software places views; the engineer decides which views, which sections, and which dimensions carry the function.

Lesson 11 · Reading plant drawings

  • The plant has an ecosystem of drawings, not one drawing: general arrangements, detail drawings, P&IDs, isometrics and loop diagrams, each answering a different question.
  • Navigating a general arrangement: find the grid and the elevations first, then the section marks that lead to the details.
  • Cross-references are the point — a GA tells you where, a detail tells you what, and a P&ID tells you why it is connected that way.
  • The drawing package is a system, and reading it competently means moving between sheets deliberately rather than studying one in isolation.

STA 103 — Engineering Statics

Lesson 01 · Forces, vectors, and resultants

  • A force is a vector: magnitude, direction and line of action. The arrow on the diagram is a contract about all three.
  • Weight is not mass. \( W = mg \) converts one to the other; kilograms are mass, newtons are force, and the distinction survives every later course.
  • Resolve into components to add, then recombine with \( R = \sqrt{R_x^2 + R_y^2} \) and a direction angle.
  • Cables pull only; struts can also push. What a member can carry is part of the geometry.
  • Working method: choose axes that kill the most unknowns — usually one axis along an unknown force.

Lesson 02 · Moments and couples

  • A moment is the turning effect of a force: magnitude times perpendicular distance to the point.
  • Varignon's theorem: the moment of a force equals the sum of the moments of its components — so you may replace an awkward perpendicular distance with convenient component distances.
  • A couple is two equal, opposite, non-collinear forces: pure rotation, no net force, and the same moment about every point in the plane.
  • Working method: choose the moment centre to eliminate unknowns — put it where the forces you do not want to solve for intersect.

Lesson 03 · The free-body diagram

  • Isolate the body, replace every contact with the force it can transmit, then label everything. Nothing else.
  • Supports are force vocabularies: a roller supplies one force, a pin two, a fixed support two plus a moment. Learn the menu and the diagram writes itself.
  • Practically every equilibrium error is a free-body-diagram error, not an algebra error.
  • Never write \( \Sigma F = 0 \) until the picture is finished.

Lesson 04 · Equilibrium of a particle

  • Concurrent forces act through one point, so only force balance is available: \( \Sigma F_x = 0 \) and \( \Sigma F_y = 0 \) (add \( \Sigma F_z = 0 \) in three dimensions).
  • There is no moment equation here, and none is needed — every line of action passes through the same point.
  • Components or geometry (the closed force triangle) are both valid; choose deliberately rather than by habit.
  • An ideal cable over a frictionless pulley carries the same tension along its whole length.

Lesson 05 · Equilibrium of rigid bodies

  • A rigid body in a plane obeys three independent equations: \( \Sigma F_x = 0 \), \( \Sigma F_y = 0 \), \( \Sigma M = 0 \) about any point you choose.
  • Two-force members carry force along the line joining their two connection points. Spotting them collapses the algebra before it starts.
  • Count unknowns against equations: more unknowns than equations means statically indeterminate, and statics alone cannot finish the problem.

Lesson 06 · Trusses

  • A truss is a set of two-force members: axial load only, tension or compression, no bending.
  • Method of joints when you need every member; method of sections when you need a few members deep inside the truss without working through it joint by joint.
  • Zero-force members carry nothing in this load case but hold geometry and resist other cases — recognise them to simplify, never to delete them.

Lesson 07 · Frames and machines

  • Frames are not trusses: they contain multi-force members, so the two-force shortcut does not apply.
  • Dismember at the pins and pay Newton his due — the force one member exerts on another appears equal and opposite on the mating diagram. Getting that sign wrong silently violates equilibrium for the pin itself.
  • Machines are frames built to multiply force; mechanical advantage falls out of the same equilibrium equations, with no new theory.

Lesson 08 · Internal forces: shear and bending moment

  • Cut the beam and three internal resultants restore equilibrium on the exposed face: normal force \( N \), shear force \( V \), bending moment \( M \).
  • The sign convention is arbitrary; consistency is not. Fix one and keep it for life.
  • The calculus that draws the diagrams: \( dV/dx = -w(x) \) and \( dM/dx = V \), where \( w \) is the distributed load intensity in N/m.
  • Between point loads \( V \) is constant and \( M \) runs straight; under uniform load \( V \) slopes and \( M \) curves. A point load steps \( V \); a couple steps \( M \).
  • The design headline: the bending moment is extremal where the shear crosses zero.

Lesson 09 · Friction

  • Dry friction has a threshold: \( F \le \mu_s N \). Equality holds only at impending motion — below it, friction is simply whatever equilibrium demands, no more.
  • That inequality is the single most misused relation in statics: friction is not automatically \( \mu_s N \).
  • Wedges and screws are friction devices, and are self-locking when the friction angle exceeds the lead angle — which is why a screw jack holds its load.
  • Belt friction is exponential in wrap angle, so a modest increase in wrap multiplies holding force. One extra turn round a capstan changes the problem.

Lesson 10 · Centroids and centers of gravity

  • A centroid is the average position of area; a centre of gravity is the average position of weight. They coincide only when the material is uniform.
  • Tabular method: one row per simple part, columns for \( A \), \( \bar{x} \), \( \bar{y} \), then the products. Holes enter as negative area — the arithmetic handles them without special cases.
  • Replace area with weight and the same table returns the centre of gravity, which is where the lifting hook must sit if the load is to hang level.

Lesson 11 · Moments of inertia

  • The second moment of area is area times distance squared: a section's resistance to bending, not a mass property.
  • Parallel-axis theorem: \( I = \bar{I} + Ad^2 \). The transfer term is squared in distance, so it usually dominates.
  • That squared distance is why sections look the way they do: an I-beam puts its material as far from the neutral axis as it can, and a tube beats a solid rod of equal weight.

MAT 104 — Materials Science I

Lesson 01 · Why materials fail the plant

  • Four families, four bargains: metals (ductile, conductive), ceramics (hard, brittle), polymers (light, temperature-limited), composites (bought properties, at a price).
  • The spine of the whole course: processing sets structure, structure sets properties, properties set performance — and the chain reads in both directions.
  • A failed part is therefore a materials question, answered backwards: performance failed, so which property fell short, so which structure produced it, so which processing produced that.
  • Quantify before judging: \( \sigma = F/A \) turns "the bolt looked thin" into a number. A 20% loss of section is a 25% rise in stress at constant load, because the divisor shrank.

Lesson 02 · Atomic bonding

  • A bond behaves like a spring with a depth: the curvature at the bottom of the well sets stiffness, the depth sets melting point and strength.
  • Four bonding types — ionic, covalent, metallic, and the weak secondary bonds — and most real materials are a mixture.
  • Pauling's relation estimates ionic character from the electronegativity difference: MgO comes out about 73% ionic, SiC about 12%, and their fracture and conduction behaviour follow.
  • Properties can be read straight off the bond: metallic bonding gives ductility and conduction; strong directional covalent bonding gives hardness and brittleness.

Lesson 03 · Crystal structures

  • Three unit cells carry most of metallurgy: FCC, BCC and HCP.
  • Packing predicts behaviour. Atomic packing factor is 0.74 for FCC and HCP against 0.68 for BCC.
  • FCC stays ductile from cryogenic temperatures to near melting. BCC slip is thermally assisted, which is the root of the ductile–brittle transition. HCP has few slip systems, hence the formability limits of magnesium and zinc.
  • \( \rho = nA/(V_C N_A) \) predicts density from a radius and an atomic mass alone. Run it in reverse on a real part: a measured density meaningfully below theoretical is porosity talking.

Lesson 04 · Crystallographic directions and planes

  • Miller indices name directions \( [uvw] \) and planes \( (hkl) \) so that crystallography can be written down and argued about precisely.
  • The labels earn their keep because slip occurs on close-packed planes along close-packed directions — the indices identify which ones those are.
  • Anisotropy leaves the lab: rolled sheet has direction-dependent properties, which is why deep-drawn parts ear and why sheet orientation appears on drawings.

Lesson 05 · Defects in solids

  • The perfect crystal is a useful fiction. Defects, not perfection, control the properties engineers care about.
  • Vacancies obey thermodynamics rather than being accidents: \( N_v/N = \exp(-Q_v/kT) \). Near the melting point roughly one site in ten thousand is empty; at room temperature, astronomically fewer.
  • That exponential is the deep reason every diffusion-controlled process accelerates so sharply with temperature — vacancies are diffusion's vehicle.
  • Dislocations let slip proceed one row of bonds at a time instead of all at once, which is why real metals are of the order of a thousand times weaker than a perfect crystal would be.

Lesson 06 · Diffusion

  • Diffusion is atoms hopping between sites, biased down the concentration gradient.
  • Fick's first law \( J = -D\,dC/dx \) covers steady state; Fick's second law covers the transient cases that are the plant's usual reality.
  • Temperature is the big lever, and it is exponential: \( D = D_0 e^{-Q_d/RT} \). A modest temperature rise buys what hours of extra soaking cannot.
  • Penetration depth scales as \( \sqrt{Dt} \), so four times the time buys only twice the depth. Carburising schedules, homogenising anneals and creep all run on this clock.

Lesson 07 · Mechanical properties I: elasticity and the tension test

  • One test does most of the work: pull a standard bar to fracture, record load and elongation, and normalise away the geometry.
  • Engineering stress and strain refer to original dimensions — \( \sigma = F/A_0 \), \( \epsilon = \Delta L/L_0 \).
  • Four numbers summarise the curve: elastic modulus \( E \) (stiffness, the initial slope), yield strength (where permanent deformation begins), tensile strength, and ductility.
  • True stress uses the actual instantaneous area, and the two diverge once necking starts — which is why the engineering curve appears to fall while the material is still hardening.
  • Tensile strength is not a design stress. Yield strength usually is.

Lesson 08 · Mechanical properties II: hardness and toughness

  • Hardness is the field's favourite proxy for strength because it is fast, cheap and nearly non-destructive.
  • It tracks strength because both are resistance to plastic flow — but it lies on work-hardened surfaces, on coatings, and across dissimilar material classes.
  • Toughness is energy absorbed before fracture, which is not the same as strength: the ductile–brittle transition is a toughness collapse, and it sank ships.
  • Hardness conversions between scales are empirical correlations, not identities. Quote the scale actually measured.

Lesson 09 · Dislocations and strengthening

  • One enemy — dislocation motion — and four ways to obstruct it.
  • Grain refinement, quantified by Hall–Petch \( \sigma_y = \sigma_0 + k_y d^{-1/2} \): finer grains are stronger, and uniquely among the four this usually improves toughness too. It is the closest metallurgy comes to a free lunch.
  • Solid solution, strain hardening and precipitation hardening make up the rest.
  • Every mechanism except grain refinement buys strength by spending ductility. Choosing among them is choosing which property you can afford to lose.

Lesson 10 · Recovery, recrystallization, and grain growth

  • Cold work stores energy in dislocations: strength and hardness up, ductility down.
  • Three stages on heating. Recovery relieves internal stress with the grains largely intact; recrystallization replaces the deformed grains outright and returns ductility; grain growth then coarsens what is left.
  • \( T_R \approx 0.4\,T_m \) on the absolute scale, and it falls with heavier prior cold work.
  • Hot working, defined properly, is working above \( T_R \) — deforming and recrystallising at the same time, so the metal never work-hardens.
  • Grain growth never stops at temperature, so an over-soaked anneal runs Hall–Petch in reverse and gives back the strength you were trying to control.

Lesson 11 · Solidification and cast structure

  • Nucleation is reluctant: a nucleus must exceed a critical radius \( r^* = 2\gamma T_m/(\Delta H_f \Delta T) \) before growth is cheaper than redissolving. Greater undercooling shrinks that radius.
  • Homogeneous nucleation needs implausible undercooling, so real melts nucleate heterogeneously on mould walls and deliberately added inoculants.
  • Three zones in a casting — chill, columnar and equiaxed — record the cooling history in the structure.
  • Segregation is solidification's chemical fingerprint: solute pushed ahead of the advancing front.
  • Chvorinov's rule \( t = B(V/A)^2 \) prices each region's freezing time by its modulus. The whole craft of risering is making the riser's modulus larger than the casting's, so the riser freezes last and feeds shrinkage instead of hosting it.

CMP 106 — Engineering Computing

Lesson 01 · Why engineers script

  • A script makes a calculation repeatable, inspectable and correctable. A spreadsheet hides its logic inside cells; a script states it in order, so a reviewer can find the mistake.
  • Anatomy of a calculation script: inputs at the top with units in the names or comments, the computation in clear steps mirroring how you would do it on paper, and output that states its units.
  • Write the calculation the way you would work it by hand, then let the machine repeat it without getting bored or careless.

Lesson 02 · Variables, types, and expressions

  • A type says what a value is — whole number, real number, text, true/false — and most beginner errors are really type errors wearing a disguise.
  • Floats tell the truth approximately. Never test two computed decimals for exact equality; compare their difference against a tolerance instead. This is the professional pattern, not a workaround.
  • Present numbers like an engineer: keep full precision through the computation, round only at output, and label the units every time.

Lesson 03 · Control flow

  • Decisions run if, elif, else: the first true branch wins, so the order of tests is part of the logic.
  • Repetition comes in two kinds — a for loop over a known collection, and a while loop that runs until a condition changes.
  • Guard clauses refuse bad input early. Rejecting a negative diameter at the top is worth more than any amount of careful arithmetic performed on it afterwards.

Lesson 04 · Functions

  • A function is a calculation with a name. One function should do one nameable thing.
  • Return, do not print. A function that returns its value can be composed into a larger calculation and tested; one that only prints is a dead end.
  • Scope means what happens inside a function stays inside it. Pass in what it needs rather than relying on values defined elsewhere.

Lesson 05 · Testing and debugging engineering code

  • Engineers test code because the dangerous failure is not a crash — it is a plausible-looking wrong number that goes into a report.
  • An assertion is a claim with teeth: it states what must be true and fails loudly at the exact moment the assumption breaks, instead of letting the error travel.
  • Exceptions let a program fail honestly rather than continuing on nonsense.
  • Pin a hand-checked case beside the function it certifies, so the code carries its own evidence.

Lesson 06 · Lists, dictionaries, and files

  • Lists hold ordered data; dictionaries hold records with named fields. Choose between them by how you intend to look things up — by position, or by name.
  • Files are where real data actually lives. Read it in rather than retyping it, and the retyping errors disappear with it.
  • The structure you choose early determines how painful every later question about the data will be.

Lesson 07 · Arrays and numerical work

  • The array idea: operate on the whole dataset at once instead of one element at a time. Shorter, faster, and far harder to get subtly wrong.
  • Reductions collapse many numbers into a few — sum, mean, maximum, standard deviation — and are most of what a data question actually asks for.
  • Masks ask questions of data ("which readings exceeded the limit?") and combine into compound questions, which is how filtering and counting are really done.

Lesson 08 · Plotting engineering data

  • A plot is an argument, not decoration. Axes get labels and units, or the argument cannot be checked.
  • Scales that reveal: a logarithmic axis turns exponential decay and power laws into straight lines, which is how you identify them by eye.
  • Sampling honesty: too few points invents a curve that was never in the data. The plot must not claim resolution the measurement did not have.

Lesson 09 · Curve fitting and interpolation

  • Two different questions. Interpolation honours every point and adds no opinion; fitting proposes a model and deliberately smooths over scatter. Each is wrong exactly where the other is right.
  • Linear interpolation between two table rows draws the chord between them — which is why property tables are published densely enough for that chord to be a good stand-in.
  • Least squares chooses the parameters that minimise the squared residuals, where a residual is measured minus predicted. \( R^2 \) reports how much of the variation the model accounts for.
  • A high-order polynomial can pass through every point exactly, residuals zero, and still swing wildly between them. Fitting every point is not the same as being right.

Lesson 10 · Small simulations

  • Most equations engineers actually meet are first-order rate equations with no closed form worth chasing, so they are marched forward numerically.
  • Euler's method is the honest workhorse: take the current rate, step forward, repeat. Crude, transparent, and enough to reason with.
  • When the step lies: stability. Too large a time step does not merely lose accuracy, it makes the answer diverge — the deviation grows each step instead of decaying.
  • Halve the step and re-run. If the answer moves, it had not converged, whatever the first plot looked like.

Lesson 11 · Project: a maintenance data mini-analysis

  • One project exercises the whole course: load real data, clean it, compute, plot, and report.
  • Cleaning is where the analysis is won or lost — mixed units, duplicated records and physically impossible values do more damage than any modelling choice made later.
  • KPIs need honest denominators. Availability, MTBF and MTTR mean nothing until you state what was counted and over what period.
  • Reproducibility is the deliverable: someone else, running your script on your data, must obtain your number. If they cannot, the number is an opinion.
