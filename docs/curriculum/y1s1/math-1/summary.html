MTH 101 · Lesson 01 · Functions, units, and engineering magnitudes

Lesson 01Functions, units, and engineering magnitudes

This lesson makes every later calculation in the programme checkable. It does three things: it treats a unit as an algebraic object that travels through an equation exactly as a number does, it reads a function as a written record of how a machine behaves, and it insists that you estimate a magnitude before you trust an arithmetic result. The habit it builds — substitute units before numbers — removes a large share of engineering calculation errors before they can be made.

Key points

  • A physical quantity is a number and a unit; the unit obeys algebra, so it can be cancelled, multiplied and raised to powers.
  • Dimensional homogeneity: both sides of a physical equation must carry identical dimensions. An equation that fails this is wrong before any number is substituted.
  • The arguments of \( \sin \), \( \ln \) and \( e^x \) must be dimensionless — there is no such thing as the logarithm of a metre.
  • A function records machine behaviour: a pump curve is head as a function of flow, written \( H(Q) \).
  • A domain is a physical statement, not a formality. Beyond a pump's runout flow the curve is forbidden, not merely undefined.
  • Convert every input to base SI before any arithmetic, and convert back only at the end.
  • Estimate first. If a suitcase-sized transfer pump computes to 300 kW, the arithmetic is wrong and you know it without re-checking.

MTH 101 · Lesson 01 · Functions, units, and engineering magnitudes · sheet 1 of 1

Units, functions and magnitudes

A physical quantity is a number together with a unit, and the unit is an algebraic object: it multiplies, divides and cancels exactly as a number does. That gives the most useful check in engineering. Dimensional homogeneity, written \( [\mathrm{LHS}] = [\mathrm{RHS}] \) and read ‘the dimensions of the left-hand side equal those of the right’, requires both sides of any physical equation to carry identical dimensions. Fail it and the equation is wrong before a single number is substituted.

Test hydraulic power, \( P = \rho g Q H \), read ‘P equals rho g Q H’, where the density is \( \rho \), the gravitational acceleration \( g \), the volumetric flow \( Q \) and the head \( H \). Substituting dimensions rather than values, with M for mass, L for length and T for time:

\[ \frac{M}{L^3}\cdot\frac{L}{T^2}\cdot\frac{L^3}{T}\cdot L = \frac{M L^2}{T^3} \]

which is the dimension of a watt. The check is necessary, not sufficient: a wrong constant survives it, a dropped term does not. One corollary is worth isolating — the argument of a transcendental function must be dimensionless, so there is no \( \ln \) of a metre, and whatever sits inside such a function is always a ratio.

Functions arrive as machine behaviour, not abstract rules: the head a pump gives at each flow is \( H(Q) \), read ‘H of Q’. Its domain is a physical statement — outside shut-off to runout the relationship is forbidden, not merely undefined, and reading off an extrapolated curve is how equipment gets mis-specified.

Finally, estimate before calculating: an answer wrong by a factor of a thousand is commoner than one wrong by ten per cent, and the cause is usually an unconverted unit. The working method — check dimensions, convert every input to base SI in one visible step, estimate to within a factor of ten, compute carrying units, then convert back.

MTH 101 · Lesson 01 · Functions, units, and engineering magnitudes · worked example 1

Hydraulic power and pressure head

Problem 1. A pump delivers \( Q = 45\ \mathrm{m^3/h} \) of water against \( H = 32\ \mathrm{m} \). Take \( \rho = 998\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} \), \( g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \). Find the hydraulic power.

Approach. Use \( P = \rho g Q H \), which returns watts only when every input is base SI. Convert the flow in its own visible step — done silently, the factor of 3600 goes missing. Estimate first: tens of cubic metres per hour against tens of metres of head is single-figure kilowatts.

Solution. \( Q = 45/3600 = 0.0125\ \mathrm{m^3/s} \); \( \rho g = 9790 \); \( P = 9790 \times 0.0125 \times 32 = 3916\ \mathrm{W} \).

Answer. 3.92 kW, as estimated. Leaving \( Q \) at 45 gives 14 098 kW — flawless arithmetic, wrong by exactly 3600.

Problem 2. Express 1 bar as a head of water, and 30 psi as a head in both metres and feet. Use \( 1\ \mathrm{psi} = 6894.76\ \mathrm{Pa} \).

Approach. Pressure head is \( h = p/(\rho g) \), with pressure written lower-case \( p \) to keep it distinct from power. Convert each pressure to pascals first; the equation returns metres only from pascals.

Solution. \( 10^5/9790 = 10.21\ \mathrm{m} \). Then \( 30 \times 6894.76 = 206\,843\ \mathrm{Pa} \), so \( 206\,843/9790 = 21.13\ \mathrm{m} \), and \( 21.13/0.3048 = 69.3\ \mathrm{ft} \).

Answer. 10.21 m; 21.13 m or 69.3 ft. One bar is very nearly 10.2 m of water, so 30 psi ≈ 2.07 bar ≈ 21 m checks the second result.

MTH 101 · Lesson 02 · Limits and continuity

Lesson 02Limits and continuity

A limit describes what a quantity is heading towards, which is not always the same as what it equals. Engineering needs the distinction constantly: a formula may be undefined at exactly the operating point you care about while still having a perfectly definite value approaching it. Continuity is the companion idea — the guarantee that a small change of input produces a small change of output, which is what makes a measurement trustworthy.

Key points

  • A limit asks what \( f(x) \) approaches as \( x \) approaches a value, deliberately ignoring what happens exactly at that value.
  • A limit can exist where the function does not. That is the useful case, not an edge case.
  • A two-sided limit exists only if the left-hand and right-hand limits agree.
  • A function is continuous at a point when the limit exists, the function is defined there, and the two are equal.
  • A removable discontinuity is a hole that algebra can cancel; a jump cannot be cancelled, and an infinite one means the output runs away.
  • \( 0/0 \) is not an answer, it is a signal to factor, cancel or rearrange before substituting.
  • Physically, continuity is the promise that a small nudge of input gives a small nudge of output — the assumption behind every calibration.

MTH 101 · Lesson 02 · Limits and continuity · sheet 1 of 1

Limits, and the three ways continuity fails

The limit \( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) \), read ‘the limit of f of x as x approaches a’, is what the output heads towards as the input closes in on \( a \). It says nothing about the value at \( a \), and that exclusion is the whole reason the idea earns its place.

A ratio that collapses at one operating point gives \( 0/0 \) on substitution — an indeterminate form, which is not a number and not a verdict but an instruction to do algebra first. Cancel the common factor and the survivor usually has an ordinary value there. The function had a hole; the physical quantity did not.

Approach can be made from either side. The left-hand limit \( \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) \) uses inputs below \( a \) only, the right-hand limit those above, and a two-sided limit exists exactly when both exist and agree. A controller that switches at a threshold must therefore be examined from both directions.

A function is continuous at a point when three things hold: the limit exists, \( f(a) \) exists, and they are equal — compactly \( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a) \). The three failures behave differently. A removable discontinuity is one missing point that algebra fills, usually an artefact of how the formula was written. A jump discontinuity has both one-sided limits existing but disagreeing, so no algebra repairs it — a relay changing state is a genuine jump. An infinite discontinuity means the output runs away, which is a warning that the model has left its valid range rather than a value.

The engineering content is a promise: a small change of input gives a small change of output. Every calibration, every interpolation between table entries and every controller tuned on a local slope depends on it, and where it fails they fail silently.

MTH 101 · Lesson 02 · Limits and continuity · worked example 1

A removable hole, and a continuity check

Problem 1. Evaluate \( \lim_{x \to 2} \dfrac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} \).

Approach. Substitute first — it either answers the question or names the technique needed. Here it gives \( 0/0 \), so the expression must be rearranged. The numerator is a difference of squares sharing a factor with the denominator. Reporting ‘undefined’ and stopping is the classic error: the function is undefined at 2, but the limit is a different question with an ordinary answer.

Solution. \( x^2 - 4 = (x-2)(x+2) \), so for every \( x \neq 2 \) the expression equals \( x + 2 \). The limit concerns only \( x \neq 2 \), so substituting gives \( 2 + 2 \).

Answer. 4. Inputs of 2.1, 2.01 and 2.001 give 4.1, 4.01 and 4.001, closing on 4.

Problem 2. A transmitter output is \( f(x) = 2x + 1 \) for \( x < 3 \) and \( f(x) = x + 4 \) for \( x \ge 3 \). Is it continuous at \( x = 3 \)?

Approach. Test all three conditions in order rather than eyeballing the graph. Checking only that the function is defined at the junction is the usual mistake — a jump discontinuity is defined there too, and jumps are exactly what this test exists to find.

Solution. Left-hand limit \( 2(3) + 1 = 7 \); right-hand limit \( 3 + 4 = 7 \); they agree, so the two-sided limit is 7; and \( f(3) = 7 \) matches it.

Answer. Continuous. Had the second rule been \( x + 5 \), the right-hand limit would be 8 against 7 — a one-unit jump and no two-sided limit.

MTH 101 · Lesson 03 · The derivative as a rate

Lesson 03The derivative as a rate

The derivative is the rate at which one quantity changes with respect to another, defined as the limit of an average rate as the measuring interval shrinks to nothing. Almost every engineering quantity that matters is a derivative of something else: velocity from position, flow from volume, heating rate from temperature. This lesson defines it and reads it as a slope.

Key points

  • An average rate of change over an interval is a difference quotient; the derivative is what it approaches as the interval shrinks to zero.
  • \( f'(x) \) and \( dy/dx \) name the same object — the instantaneous rate of change.
  • Geometrically the derivative is the slope of the tangent: the slope the curve has at one point rather than across a span.
  • The units of a derivative are always the output's units divided by the input's, which makes it self-checking.
  • A positive derivative means increasing, negative means decreasing, and zero means momentarily stationary.
  • Differentiability implies continuity, but continuity does not imply differentiability — a sharp corner is continuous and has no single slope.
  • Reading a derivative off equipment data is reading a gradient: how fast the reading moves, not how large it is.

MTH 101 · Lesson 03 · The derivative as a rate · sheet 1 of 1

From average rate to instantaneous rate

An average rate of change is total change over the interval it happened in. Written for a function it is the difference quotient \( \dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \), read ‘f of x plus h, minus f of x, all over h’, where \( h \) is the interval width. It is the slope of the line joining two points on the curve.

That average hides whatever happened inside the interval. A pump that ran hard for a minute then idled for nine has the same average as one that ran steadily, and if what you need is the rate now, the average is the wrong number. So shrink the interval: the derivative is what the difference quotient approaches as \( h \) approaches zero.

This is the previous lesson doing work. Setting \( h = 0 \) outright gives \( 0/0 \), because change and interval vanish together; the limit asks what the ratio approaches without ever setting \( h \) to zero, and that question has an answer. Two notations mean the same thing: \( f'(x) \), read ‘f prime of x’, is compact, while \( \dfrac{dy}{dx} \), read ‘d y by d x’, keeps both variables visible when several quantities vary at once.

Geometrically the derivative is the slope of the tangent line: as the second point slides towards the first, the cutting line pivots into the tangent. The sign carries most of the practical information — positive is increasing, negative decreasing, and \( f'(x) = 0 \) marks a stationary point. A bearing temperature whose derivative has just turned positive is a different situation from one merely high and steady.

The units are automatic: output units divided by input units, every time. A derivative in units that break that rule is a differentiation error you can catch without checking the algebra. One caution — a differentiable function is continuous, but not the reverse: a sharp corner is continuous and has no single slope, which is what a signal switching regime produces.

MTH 101 · Lesson 03 · The derivative as a rate · worked example 1

A derivative from first principles, and one with units

Problem 1. For \( f(x) = x^2 \), find \( f'(3) \) from the definition.

Approach. Build the difference quotient, simplify until \( h \) leaves the denominator, and only then let it approach zero. The order matters: cancelling before taking the limit is what makes the limit exist. Setting \( h = 0 \) in the unsimplified quotient returns \( 0/0 \) and looks like a dead end.

Solution. \( \dfrac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \dfrac{6h + h^2}{h} = 6 + h \) for every \( h \neq 0 \), which approaches 6.

Answer. \( f'(3) = 6 \). Numerically \( h = 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 \) give 6.1, 6.01 and 6.001.

Problem 2. A tank's stored volume varies with level as \( V = 0.8z^2 \), \( V \) in cubic metres and \( z \) in metres. Find \( dV/dz \) at \( z = 2.5\ \mathrm{m} \) and say what it means.

Approach. This derivative is with respect to level, not time, so it answers ‘how much more volume per extra metre’ — the local capacity. Keeping units in view guards against misreading it as a filling rate, which is a different derivative entirely.

Solution. The derivative of \( z^2 \) is \( 2z \), so \( dV/dz = 1.6z \), giving \( 1.6 \times 2.5 = 4.0 \). Cubic metres over metres is square metres.

Answer. \( 4.0\ \mathrm{m^2} \) — the cross-sectional area at that level. Because the tank widens with height this is local, not constant: at \( z = 1 \) it is only \( 1.6\ \mathrm{m^2} \).

MTH 101 · Lesson 04 · Differentiation rules and the chain rule

Lesson 04Differentiation rules and the chain rule

First principles work but are slow, so this lesson replaces them with rules. The product, quotient and chain rules let any combination of familiar functions be differentiated by inspection. The chain rule is the one that matters most in engineering, because real quantities are almost always functions of functions.

Key points

  • The power rule: the derivative of \( x^n \) is \( nx^{n-1} \).
  • Differentiation is linear — constants pull out, and sums differentiate term by term.
  • The product rule handles a product of two varying quantities and is not simply the product of the derivatives.
  • The quotient rule follows the same pattern with a minus sign, so the order of the two terms matters.
  • The chain rule differentiates a function of a function by multiplying the outer rate by the inner rate.
  • In Leibniz notation the chain rule reads as a cancellation of differentials, which is why it is easy to remember and easy to extend.
  • Identify the inner function first, before differentiating anything — most chain-rule errors are misidentification, not arithmetic.

MTH 101 · Lesson 04 · Differentiation rules and the chain rule · sheet 1 of 1

The rules, and the chain rule that matters most

The power rule \( \dfrac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1} \) does the routine work, and differentiation is linear — constants pass through, sums differentiate term by term. Together those handle every polynomial.

Products need more care. The product rule is \( (uv)' = u'v + uv' \), read ‘u v all prime equals u prime v plus u v prime’. Multiplying the two derivatives is simply wrong, and the reason is physical: when both quantities vary, the product changes because the first moved while the second held, and because the second moved while the first held. Two contributions, two terms. The quotient rule \( \left(\dfrac{u}{v}\right)' = \dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2} \) has the same shape with a minus sign, so the order of its two products is not interchangeable.

The chain rule handles a function of a function, which in engineering is the normal case. In Leibniz form it is \( \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du}\dfrac{du}{dx} \), read ‘d y by d x equals d y by d u times d u by d x’, where \( u \) is the inner function. The differentials appear to cancel, which is not a proof but is a reliable memory aid that extends to longer chains.

The working method. Name the inner function in writing. Differentiate the outer while treating the inner as one symbol. Multiply by the inner derivative. Substitute back.

Almost every chain-rule error is at the first step. Differentiating \( e^{-t/12} \) as though the exponent were \( -t \), and so losing the factor of one twelfth, gives an answer twelve times too large with no visible symptom. One derivative to carry forward: the exponential is its own derivative, \( \dfrac{d}{dx}e^{x} = e^{x} \), and with the chain rule that single fact generates every growth and decay rate in the next lesson.

MTH 101 · Lesson 04 · Differentiation rules and the chain rule · worked example 1

A cooling curve, and a product where the terms fight

Problem 1. A component cools as \( T(t) = 20 + 60e^{-t/12} \), \( T \) in °C and \( t \) in minutes. Find \( dT/dt \) at \( t = 0 \) and \( t = 12 \).

Approach. The constant differentiates to zero; the rest is an exponential of a function, so name the inner function first: \( u = -t/12 \), giving \( -1/12 \). Forgetting that factor overstates every cooling rate twelvefold, so write it down rather than carry it.

Solution. The derivative of \( 60e^{u} \) with respect to \( u \) is \( 60e^{u} \); multiplying along the chain gives \( dT/dt = -5e^{-t/12} \). At \( t = 0 \) that is \( -5 \); at \( t = 12 \), \( -5e^{-1} = -1.84 \).

Answer. Cooling at 5°C/min initially, 1.84°C/min after twelve minutes. Negative throughout, as any cooling curve must be.

Problem 2. During run-up a drive's torque rises as \( M = 50 + 2t \) N·m while speed falls as \( \omega = 100 - 0.5t \) rad/s. Power is \( P = M\omega \). Find \( dP/dt \) at \( t = 10\ \mathrm{s} \).

Approach. Both factors vary, so this is a product rule. The two contributions have opposite signs — torque helping, speed hurting — so the answer is a competition whose sign cannot be guessed. The shortcut of multiplying derivatives gives \( -1 \): not merely inaccurate but the wrong sign.

Solution. \( M' = 2 \), \( \omega' = -0.5 \); at \( t = 10 \), \( M = 70 \) and \( \omega = 95 \). So \( dP/dt = 2(95) + 70(-0.5) = 190 - 35 \).

Answer. \( 155\ \mathrm{W/s} \) — rising, because torque outweighs speed at this instant. It need not stay so as the run-up continues.

MTH 101 · Lesson 05 · Exponentials, logarithms, and growth/decay

Lesson 05Exponentials, logarithms, and growth/decay

Exponential behaviour appears wherever a quantity's rate of change is proportional to the quantity itself — cooling, discharge, vibration decay, corrosion allowance, radioactive sources. The logarithm is its inverse, and it is the tool that turns a multiplicative process into a straight line you can read. This lesson gives both, and the two time constants engineers actually quote.

Key points

  • Exponential change means the rate is proportional to the amount present, which is why it never quite reaches zero.
  • \( e^{x} \) is its own derivative; every growth and decay rate follows from that one fact and the chain rule.
  • Decay is written \( A_0e^{-t/\tau} \), where the time constant is the time to fall to about 37% of the start.
  • The half-life is the time to halve, and it is a fixed multiple of the time constant: \( t_{1/2} = \tau \ln 2 \).
  • Logarithms turn products into sums, which is why they linearise multiplicative data and why decibels exist.
  • A straight line on a log axis is an exponential on a linear one — the quickest way to identify the behaviour from plant data.
  • Solving for time means taking a logarithm of both sides, so the natural log is the working tool rather than a curiosity.

MTH 101 · Lesson 05 · Exponentials, logarithms, and growth/decay · sheet 1 of 1

Exponentials, and the logarithm that undoes them

A great many industrial quantities share one behaviour: the rate of change is proportional to how much is left. A hot component loses heat fastest when hottest, a vessel discharges fastest when fullest. Whenever that proportionality holds the curve is an exponential, because \( e^{x} \) is the one function equal to its own derivative and the chain rule supplies the constant.

Decay is \( A = A_0e^{-t/\tau} \), read ‘A equals A nought e to the minus t over tau’, with \( A_0 \) the initial value and the time constant \( \tau \) the time to fall to \( 1/e \), about 37%, of the start. Since the rate shrinks with the amount, the curve never reaches zero — which is why a purge is specified as five time constants rather than ‘until empty’.

Engineers more often quote the half-life, and the two are rigidly linked by \( t_{1/2} = \tau \ln 2 \), about 0.693 of the time constant. Neither depends on where you start measuring: the time to halve is the same from any point on the curve, which is what makes the number worth quoting.

The natural logarithm \( \ln x \) inverts the exponential, and its working purpose is narrow: when the unknown sits in an exponent, taking logarithms brings it down where algebra can reach it. Every ‘how long until’ question is answered this way.

Its second use is diagnostic. Logarithms turn products into sums, so a process multiplying by a fixed factor per unit time plots as a straight line on a logarithmic axis — the quickest test available on plant data, with the slope giving the time constant directly. The same property explains the decibel \( 10\log_{10}(P_2/P_1) \): every 10 dB is one factor of ten in power, and because it is a ratio the figure is meaningless until the reference is stated.

MTH 101 · Lesson 05 · Exponentials, logarithms, and growth/decay · worked example 1

Time constant from two readings, and a ratio in decibels

Problem 1. A machine's vibration amplitude is 12 mm when the drive trips and 3 mm eight seconds later. Find the time constant and half-life, and predict the amplitude at 20 s.

Approach. Two readings and one assumed shape fix the curve: substitute into \( A = A_0e^{-t/\tau} \) and take logarithms to release \( \tau \). Form the check first — 12 to 3 is two halvings in eight seconds, so the half-life must be 4 s. The commonest error is a dropped minus sign, which returns a negative time constant and a growing curve.

Solution. \( 3 = 12e^{-8/\tau} \) gives \( e^{-8/\tau} = 0.25 \), so \( -8/\tau = \ln 0.25 = -1.386 \) and \( \tau = 5.77\ \mathrm{s} \). Then \( t_{1/2} = 5.77 \times 0.693 = 4.00\ \mathrm{s} \), matching the check, and 20 s is five half-lives, so \( 12/2^5 \).

Answer. \( \tau = 5.77\ \mathrm{s} \), \( t_{1/2} = 4.00\ \mathrm{s} \), 0.375 mm at 20 s.

Problem 2. A modification raises radiated sound power by a factor of 100. Express it in decibels. Separately, what power ratio is a 3 dB rise?

Approach. The first part substitutes into the definition; the second runs it backwards, undoing a logarithm rather than taking one. The classic error is treating decibels as additive in the quantity: 20 dB is not twice 10 dB in power, it is ten times it.

Solution. \( 10\log_{10}(100) = 20 \). Backwards, \( \log_{10}(P_2/P_1) = 0.3 \), so the ratio is \( 10^{0.3} \).

Answer. 20 dB; and 3 dB is a doubling of power — worth memorising, since it recurs as a bandwidth and margin criterion.

MTH 101 · Lesson 06 · Extrema, curve behavior, and optimization

Lesson 06Extrema, curve behavior, and optimization

A stationary point is where a quantity stops rising or falling, and that is where optima live. This lesson uses the first derivative to find candidates and the second derivative to classify them, then applies both to a design question: choosing dimensions that minimise material for a required duty.

Key points

  • Optima occur where the first derivative is zero, or at the ends of the allowed range — endpoints are candidates too and are often forgotten.
  • The second derivative measures how the slope itself is changing, which is what distinguishes a minimum from a maximum.
  • Positive second derivative at a stationary point means a minimum; negative means a maximum.
  • An inflection is where curvature changes sign; a stationary point can be neither a maximum nor a minimum.
  • Real optimisation starts by writing the objective in ONE variable, using the constraint to eliminate the others.
  • The constraint is what makes the problem finite — without it, most engineering objectives have no interior optimum.
  • Check the answer is a minimum rather than assuming it; the algebra is happy to hand you a maximum.

Finding an optimum, and proving it is one

Where a quantity is largest or smallest it is momentarily neither rising nor falling, so solving \( f'(x) = 0 \) finds stationary points. That is a search for candidates, not answers, and two habits keep it honest.

First, remember the endpoints. Physical variables are always confined — a radius cannot be negative, a pressure cannot exceed a rating — and the extreme value may sit at an end of that range where the derivative is nowhere near zero.

Second, classify what you find. The second derivative \( f''(x) \), read ‘f double prime of x’, measures how fast the slope is changing, which is curvature. At a stationary point positive means curving upwards and so a local minimum; negative means a local maximum. If it is zero the test is silent — the point may be an inflection, where curvature changes sign with no optimum at all.

Real problems arrive with several dimensions free and one requirement fixed, and the method is to use the requirement to eliminate variables. The quantity to be optimised is the objective function; the fixed requirement is the constraint. Without a constraint most engineering objectives have no interior optimum at all — the cheapest vessel is one of zero size — so the constraint is what makes the question meaningful.

The working method. Write the objective in all its variables. Write the constraint as an equation. Solve the constraint for one variable and substitute, so the objective depends on one thing. Differentiate, set to zero, solve. Classify with the second derivative and check the endpoints. Back-substitute for the other dimensions. Most of the difficulty is in the substitution step; most of the errors are in skipping the classification, which is how a maximum gets presented as a minimum.

Least material for a required capacity, and how sharp it is

Problem 1. An open-top cylindrical vessel must hold \( 2\ \mathrm{m^3} \). Find the radius and height using least material, and the resulting surface area.

Approach. Objective: the area \( S \) of a base plus a wall, with no lid. Constraint: fixed volume. Eliminate the height so \( S \) depends only on \( r \). The trap is including a second circle — write the area from the actual geometry rather than recalling a closed-cylinder formula.

Solution. \( S = \pi r^2 + 2\pi rH \) with \( \pi r^2H = 2 \), so \( H = 2/(\pi r^2) \) and \( S = \pi r^2 + 4/r \). Then \( S'(r) = 2\pi r - 4/r^2 = 0 \) gives \( r^3 = 2/\pi \), \( r = 0.860\ \mathrm{m} \); \( S''(r) = 2\pi + 8/r^3 = 18.85 > 0 \), a minimum; and \( H = 0.860\ \mathrm{m} \).

Answer. \( r = H = 0.860\ \mathrm{m} \), \( S = 6.97\ \mathrm{m^2} \). Height equals radius exactly, whatever volume is required.

Problem 2. Evaluate the area at \( r = 0.70 \) and \( r = 1.00\ \mathrm{m} \) and comment on how sharp the optimum is.

Approach. The second-derivative test proves a minimum but says nothing about how much it matters. Evaluating nearby answers the practical question: how much material is lost by building a little off optimum. That decides whether the optimisation survives contact with a stock size.

Solution. \( S = \pi r^2 + 4/r \) gives \( 1.539 + 5.714 = 7.254 \) at 0.70, and \( 3.142 + 4.000 = 7.142 \) at 1.00, against 6.975 at the optimum.

Answer. Both neighbours cost more, confirming the minimum, but only by 4.0% and 2.4%. The optimum is shallow, so rounding to a stock radius costs very little.

MTH 101 · Lesson 07 · The integral as accumulation

Lesson 07The integral as accumulation

Differentiation asks how fast something changes; integration asks how much has accumulated. If you know a rate and want a total — a flow and want a volume, a power and want an energy, a corrosion rate and want a wall loss — the operation you need is a definite integral. This lesson builds it as a limit of sums and reads it as an area.

Key points

  • Integrating a rate over an interval gives the total accumulated over that interval, in the rate's units multiplied by the interval's.
  • A definite integral is defined as the limit of a sum of narrow strips, which is why it equals the area under the curve.
  • The area interpretation is literal: strip width times strip height, summed.
  • Areas below the axis count as negative, so an integral can represent a net rather than a gross total.
  • The Fundamental Theorem links the two halves of calculus: integrate a rate by finding a function whose derivative is that rate.
  • The average value of a quantity over an interval is its integral divided by the interval length.
  • A numerical sum with finitely many strips approaches the exact integral as the strips narrow — which is how instruments actually totalise.

MTH 101 · Lesson 07 · The integral as accumulation · sheet 1 of 1

Accumulating a rate: sums, integrals and averages

If a flow varies through a shift and you need the volume passed, chop the shift into short intervals, treat the rate as constant across each, multiply and add. That is a Riemann sum \( \sum f(x_i)\,\Delta x \), where \( \Delta x \) is one interval's width. Each term is a rectangle: a height from the curve, a width from the interval. It is wrong by however much the rate moved inside each interval.

Narrow the intervals and that error shrinks. The definite integral \( \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \), read ‘the integral from a to b of f of x d x’, is what the sum approaches as the strips become arbitrarily narrow. The elongated S is a sum sign and \( dx \) is the vanishing width, so the notation pictures the construction.

Two consequences follow at once. The integral is the area under the curve, because that is literally what was summed — and area below the axis counts negative, so an integral of a signed quantity gives a net, not a gross, total. And the units follow the multiplication: cubic metres per second integrated over seconds gives cubic metres.

Summing strips converges but is laborious. The exact route is the Fundamental Theorem, \( \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) \), where the antiderivative \( F \) is any function whose derivative is the integrand. Integration is differentiation run backwards, which is why the earlier rules are not left behind.

The integral also delivers the average value, \( \bar{f} = \dfrac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f\,dx \) — the constant value that would accumulate the same total in the same time. That is not the mean of a handful of readings: readings taken at uneven intervals, or while the quantity moved quickly, give a different and generally wrong answer, because the integral weights every instant by how long it actually lasted.

MTH 101 · Lesson 07 · The integral as accumulation · worked example 1

Volume from a ramping flow, and a sum converging to it

Problem 1. During start-up a flow rises as \( Q(t) = 0.05 + 0.01t \), \( Q \) in \( \mathrm{m^3/s} \), \( t \) in seconds. Find the volume passed in 60 s and the average flow.

Approach. Volume is the accumulation of flow, so integrate the rate over the interval; the antiderivative is immediate. The error to avoid is multiplying the final flow by elapsed time, which treats a ramp as though it ran at maximum throughout and overstates the volume by more than half.

Solution. \( F(t) = 0.05t + 0.005t^2 \), whose derivative is the integrand, so \( F(60) - F(0) = 3.0 + 18.0 \). The average is that total divided by 60.

Answer. 21.0 m³ at an average of 0.35 m³/s. The wrong method gives 39 m³. The average equals the flow at the midpoint, 30 s, which is a property of a linear ramp.

Problem 2. Approximate the same volume with left-endpoint sums of 6, 60 and 600 strips.

Approach. A left-endpoint sum takes each strip's height at the start of its interval, which for a rising rate is the lowest value in the strip — so every rectangle sits below the curve and the sum must under-estimate. Predicting the direction before computing is the point. This is how a flow totaliser accumulates from periodic samples.

Solution. With \( \Delta t = 10, 1 \) and \( 0.1\ \mathrm{s} \) the sums are 18.0, 20.7 and 20.97 m³.

Answer. Always low, as predicted, closing on 21.0. Each tenfold increase in strip count cuts the error tenfold — 3.0, 0.3, 0.03 — so a totaliser sampling once a second here under-reads by about 1.4%.

MTH 101 · Lesson 08 · Integration techniques

Lesson 08Integration techniques

Most integrals met in engineering are not in any table. Two techniques cover the great majority: substitution, which reverses the chain rule, and integration by parts, which reverses the product rule. Each is a differentiation rule read backwards, which is also how you decide which one a given integral needs.

Key points

  • Substitution reverses the chain rule: look for an inner function whose derivative also appears as a factor.
  • When substituting in a definite integral, change the limits too, or convert back before evaluating — never mix the two.
  • Integration by parts reverses the product rule and is the tool for a product of unlike things, such as a power multiplied by an exponential.
  • The choice of which factor to differentiate decides whether the new integral is easier or worse; pick the one that simplifies when differentiated.
  • By parts may need repeating, and each pass should visibly reduce the difficulty.
  • Every indefinite integral carries an arbitrary constant, which cancels in a definite integral and matters in a differential equation.
  • Check any integration by differentiating the answer — it is fast and it is conclusive.

MTH 101 · Lesson 08 · Integration techniques · sheet 1 of 1

Substitution and by parts: two rules read backwards

Differentiating a function of a function multiplies by the inner derivative, so integration must divide it out again. Substitution is the bookkeeping that does so safely.

The pattern to recognise is an integrand containing an inner expression together with that expression's own derivative as a factor. Name the inner expression \( u = g(x) \), form the differential \( du = g'(x)\,dx \), and the integral rewrites in \( u \) alone. If the derivative is absent, up to a constant multiple, substitution is the wrong tool and forcing it produces nonsense.

For a definite integral there are two honest routes and one common error. Either convert the limits into the new variable, or keep the old limits and convert back before substituting them. The error is to change the variable and keep the original limits, which evaluates the right function at the wrong points.

The product rule reversed gives integration by parts, \( \int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du \). It does not evaluate an integral outright; it trades one for another, and is worth using only when the trade is favourable. That makes the choice of \( u \) the whole skill: the factor you name \( u \) gets differentiated, so choose the one that simplifies. A power is the usual choice because differentiating lowers it; an exponential is the usual \( dv \) because integrating changes nothing. Choosing the other way round is not wrong so much as useless — if one application leaves you worse off, swap the roles rather than pressing on.

Every indefinite integral carries a constant of integration \( C \), because differentiation destroys constants. It cancels between limits and matters in a differential equation, which is two lessons ahead. Whatever technique is used, the check takes seconds: differentiate the answer and confirm the integrand returns.

A substitution with changed limits, and a decaying transient

Problem 1. Evaluate \( \int_0^2 2x\,e^{x^2}\,dx \).

Approach. The exponent \( x^2 \) is an inner function and its derivative \( 2x \) sits there as a factor — the signature of a substitution. Problem 2. Evaluate \( \int_0^{10} t\,e^{-t/5}\,dt \).

Lesson 09Applications: areas, volumes, work, and averages

Integration earns its keep when the thing being accumulated is not a rate in time. Areas between curves, volumes of revolution, work against a varying force and true averages are all the same operation applied to different integrands. This lesson is about choosing the integrand correctly, which is where the engineering sits.

Key points

  • Every application is the same construction: slice the object, write one slice's contribution, then integrate.
  • Getting the slice right is the whole problem; the integration afterwards is routine.
  • Work is force integrated over distance, and it needs an integral exactly when the force varies.
  • For pumping problems the varying quantity is usually the lift, not the weight of the slice.
  • The average value of a quantity is its integral divided by the interval, not the mean of a few readings.
  • Root-mean-square is a different average again — square, average, then take the root, in that order.
  • For a sinusoid the RMS value is the peak divided by root two, which is why AC quantities are quoted that way.

MTH 101 · Lesson 09 · Applications: areas, volumes, work, and averages · sheet 1 of 1

Slicing, and two averages that are not the same

Every application here uses one construction: slice the object thin enough that the varying quantity is constant across each slice, write what one slice contributes, then integrate. The integration is routine; writing the slice is where the engineering and the errors are. Check its units first — a slice of a work integral must come out in joules.

Consider emptying a tank. It is tempting to take the total weight and multiply by a height, but that is valid only if every part is lifted the same distance, and it is not: a slice near the top is barely lifted, one at the bottom is lifted the full depth. The thing that varies is the lift, so the lift belongs inside the integral. That single observation is what makes the answer \( \rho gAH^2/2 \) rather than \( \rho gAH^2 \) — a factor of two, and the one-half is the average lift being half the depth, produced by the integration rather than assumed.

The average value of a varying quantity is its integral divided by the interval length. For many purposes it is the wrong average: a quantity swinging symmetrically about zero — an alternating current, a vibration velocity — has a plain average of zero, which is true and useless.

What matters then is the root-mean-square, and the name is the recipe in the order it must be done: square, average, then take the root. Squaring removes the sign, averaging weights every instant by its duration, the root restores the units. For a sinusoid it works out as peak over root two, about 0.707. Contrast the average of the magnitude over half a cycle, \( 2/\pi \) or 0.637 — a different number for a different question, and quoting one where the other is meant is a standard source of an 11% discrepancy nobody can find.

MTH 101 · Lesson 09 · Applications: areas, volumes, work, and averages · worked example 1

Work to empty a tank, and RMS against mean

Problem 1. A vertical cylindrical tank of radius \( 1.5\ \mathrm{m} \) holds water 4 m deep. Find the work to pump it to the tank top, with \( \rho = 998\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} \), \( g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \).

Approach. Slice horizontally. Each slice has the same area and weight but a different lift, so the lift goes inside the integral: measuring \( y \) upward from the bottom, a slice is raised \( (4 - y) \). Multiplying total weight by full depth lifts every drop from the bottom and overstates the work by exactly two.

Solution. \( A = \pi(1.5)^2 = 7.069\ \mathrm{m^2} \); one slice contributes \( \rho gA(4-y)\,dy \); and \( \int_0^4 (4-y)\,dy = 8 \), so \( W = 998 \times 9.81 \times 7.069 \times 8 \).

Answer. 554 kJ, or 0.554 MJ — the general form \( \rho gAH^2/2 \).

Problem 2. For a sinusoid of peak 1, find the RMS value over a full cycle and the average magnitude over a half cycle.

Approach. Two averages of one waveform, so keep the operations in order. RMS squares first, turning the sine into a squared sine whose average over whole cycles is exactly one half. The magnitude average has no squaring, so it must be taken over a half cycle — over a full cycle the signed average is zero, which is the trap.

Solution. Mean square is \( 1/2 \), so RMS is \( 1/\sqrt{2} \). Over a half cycle the mean of the sine is \( 2/\pi \).

Answer. 0.707 and 0.637 of peak. They differ by 11% and answer different questions: RMS gives the same heating, while the magnitude average is what a simple rectifying instrument responds to.

Lesson 10First-order differential equations

A differential equation states a rule about how fast something changes, and solving it recovers the quantity itself. Most first-order models in plant engineering are separable, meaning the two variables can be pushed to opposite sides and integrated independently. This lesson solves that class and fixes the arbitrary constant from an initial condition.

Key points

  • A differential equation relates a quantity to its own rate of change.
  • Separable means the equation can be rearranged with each variable entirely on its own side before integrating.
  • Integrating both sides introduces one arbitrary constant, which the initial condition determines.
  • A general solution is a family of curves; a particular solution is the one that passes through the known starting point.
  • Proportional-rate models integrate to exponentials, which closes the loop with the growth-and-decay lesson.
  • Newton's law of cooling is the standard example, and its solution approaches ambient without ever reaching it.
  • Always verify by substituting the solution back into the original equation.

MTH 101 · Lesson 10 · First-order differential equations · sheet 1 of 1

Separable equations, and why cooling never finishes

A differential equation states a rule a quantity's rate must obey, and solving means finding the quantity whose rate obeys it. That is why integration is the prerequisite: solving is integrating, once the equation is arranged so integration is possible.

An equation is separable when it can be rearranged with each variable entirely on its own side, each with its own differential. Both sides are then integrated independently, which is legitimate precisely because each now involves only one variable.

Integrating produces one arbitrary constant. That is not untidiness but the honest statement that a rate rule alone cannot know where the quantity started: a cooling law describes every component cooling in that environment, whatever its starting temperature. That family is the general solution, and an initial condition selects one member as the particular solution.

The standard plant model says a body's rate of temperature change is proportional to how far it is from its surroundings — fast when the gap is large, slow when small. The minus sign does real work: it sends the temperature towards ambient from either side, so one equation covers heating and cooling. The constant \( k \) sets the pace and is the reciprocal of the time constant met earlier.

Separating and integrating turns the difference from ambient into an exponential decay, which closes the loop with the growth-and-decay lesson: the curve met there as a description is produced here as a consequence. Two features read straight off it. The solution approaches ambient asymptotically and never arrives, so ‘time to reach ambient’ is not well posed while ‘time to within two degrees’ is. And the constant appears only as \( kt \), so doubling the rate constant is indistinguishable from waiting twice as long.

MTH 101 · Lesson 10 · First-order differential equations · worked example 1

Solving a cooling law, and inverting it for a time

Problem 1. A component obeys \( dT/dt = -k(T - T_a) \) with \( T_a = 20\ ^\circ\mathrm{C} \), \( k = 1/12\ \mathrm{min^{-1}} \) and \( T(0) = 80 \). Solve for \( T(t) \) and evaluate at 30 minutes.

Approach. Separable once the temperature difference is treated as the working variable. Integrate, then fix the constant from the initial condition. The classic slip is losing the minus sign during separation, which produces a component heating towards infinity — absurd, and therefore easy to catch by glancing at the exponent's sign.

Solution. \( \dfrac{dT}{T - 20} = -k\,dt \) integrates to \( \ln(T - 20) = -kt + C \); at \( t = 0 \), \( T = 80 \) gives \( C = \ln 60 \); rearranging, \( T = 20 + 60e^{-t/12} \), and at 30 minutes \( 20 + 60e^{-2.5} \).

Answer. \( T(t) = 20 + 60e^{-t/12} \), giving 24.93°C. This is exactly the curve differentiated in Lesson 4 — there given, here derived from the rate rule that generates it.

Problem 2. How long until it reaches \( 35\ ^\circ\mathrm{C} \)? And why is ‘until it reaches 20°C’ not a fair question?

Approach. This inverts the solution, so the exponential must be undone with a logarithm. Work in the difference from ambient, since that is what decays exponentially; subtracting ambient at the wrong moment is the usual error.

Solution. The difference is \( 35 - 20 = 15 \) against an initial 60, so \( e^{-t/12} = 0.25 \), \( -t/12 = -1.386 \), \( t = 16.64 \).

Answer. 16.64 minutes. Reaching exactly 20°C needs the difference to be zero, and no finite time makes an exponential zero — so the criterion must be written as a tolerance to be testable.

Lesson 11Taylor series and approximation

A Taylor series replaces a complicated function near a point with a polynomial that matches its value and its derivatives there. That is the justification for every small-angle approximation, every linearised model and every controller tuned on a local slope. This lesson builds the series and, more importantly, bounds the error you accept by truncating it.

Key points

  • A Taylor polynomial matches the function's value and first several derivatives at one chosen point.
  • Each extra term buys accuracy near that point and buys nothing far from it.
  • Truncating leaves a remainder; an approximation without an error estimate is an assertion, not a result.
  • The first-order truncation is linearisation — the tangent line — and is the basis of most control models.
  • The small-angle approximation is the first-order Taylor series of the sine, and its error grows as the cube of the angle.
  • Convergence is local: a series about one point may be useless at another.
  • Check an approximation by computing the first neglected term, which usually bounds the error well.

Matching derivatives, and the error you accept

To approximate a function near an expansion point \( a \), build a polynomial agreeing with it there — not only in value but in slope, curvature, and as many further derivatives as you care to match. Each condition fixes one coefficient, and the coefficients come out as derivatives divided by factorials. The factorial is not decoration: differentiating a power term repeatedly generates exactly a factorial, so dividing by \( n! \) is what makes the polynomial's nth derivative equal the function's rather than some multiple of it. An expansion about zero is a Maclaurin series, the common case because most engineering approximations sit at a rest state or nominal operating point.

Accuracy is local: excellent near \( a \), degrading away from it, so a series built about one operating point may be worthless at another. Adding terms improves matters near \( a \) without necessarily helping far from it — more terms is no substitute for expanding about the right point.

Every practical use is a truncation: keep a few terms, accept a remainder. An approximation quoted without an error estimate is an assertion rather than a result, and the usable estimate is simply the first term you discarded.

The most important truncation is the shortest. Keeping the constant and linear terms gives linearisation, \( f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) \), which is the tangent line. Nearly every control model and sensitivity calculation is one, and they work because the discarded terms carry the deviation squared — negligible while it stays small. The small-angle approximation is the standard case: the first discarded term is the cube over six, so the error grows as the cube of the angle. That is the honest content of every ‘for small oscillations’ — a statement that a cubic term was discarded, carrying an obligation to check the amplitude stayed small.

Watching the error fall, and how small a small angle is

Problem 1. Use the Maclaurin series of \( e^{x} \) to approximate \( e^{0.1} \) to first, second and third order against the exact 1.1051709.

Approach. The exponential is its own derivative, so every derivative at zero is 1 and the coefficients are the reciprocal factorials. Compute each partial sum and its error, then test whether the first discarded term predicts that error — which is the practical reason for the exercise.

Solution. First order \( 1 + 0.1 = 1.1 \), error 0.005171; second \( +\,0.005 = 1.105 \), error 0.000171; third \( +\,0.1^3/6 \) gives 1.1051667, error 0.0000043. The first term discarded at second order is \( 0.1^3/6 = 0.000167 \).

Answer. Errors of 5.2×10⁻³, 1.7×10⁻⁴ and 4.3×10⁻⁶ — roughly two decimal places per order, with the first neglected term predicting the error to within 3%.

Problem 2. Quantify the small-angle approximation's error at 0.1 and 0.5 rad, and state the practical limit.

Approach. Compare against the true sine and express the discrepancy as a percentage, since that is what a tolerance is written in. Expect roughly cubic scaling, so a fivefold increase in angle should cost far more than fivefold in error.

Solution. At 0.1 rad the true sine is 0.0998334 against 0.1: error 0.000167, or 0.17%. At 0.5 rad it is 0.4794255 against 0.5: error 0.020574, or 4.29%. The angle grew fivefold and the absolute error about 123-fold, against the 125 a cubic law predicts.

Answer. 0.17% and 4.29%. Below about 0.1 rad, roughly 6 degrees, the approximation is good to a fifth of a percent; by 0.5 rad it costs over 4% and should be stated explicitly if used.
