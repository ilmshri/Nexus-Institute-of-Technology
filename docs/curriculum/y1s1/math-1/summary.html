MTH 101 · Lesson 01 · Functions, units, and engineering magnitudes

Lesson 01Functions, units, and engineering magnitudes

This lesson makes every later calculation in the programme checkable. It does three things: it treats a unit as an algebraic object that travels through an equation exactly as a number does, it reads a function as a written record of how a machine behaves, and it insists that you estimate a magnitude before you trust an arithmetic result. The habit it builds — substitute units before numbers — removes a large share of engineering calculation errors before they can be made.

Key points

  • A physical quantity is a number and a unit; the unit obeys algebra, so it can be cancelled, multiplied and raised to powers.
  • Dimensional homogeneity: both sides of a physical equation must carry identical dimensions. An equation that fails this is wrong before any number is substituted.
  • The arguments of \( \sin \), \( \ln \) and \( e^x \) must be dimensionless — there is no such thing as the logarithm of a metre.
  • A function records machine behaviour: a pump curve is head as a function of flow, written \( H(Q) \).
  • A domain is a physical statement, not a formality. Beyond a pump's runout flow the curve is forbidden, not merely undefined.
  • Convert every input to base SI before any arithmetic, and convert back only at the end.
  • Estimate first. If a suitcase-sized transfer pump computes to 300 kW, the arithmetic is wrong and you know it without re-checking.

MTH 101 · Lesson 01 · Functions, units, and engineering magnitudes · sheet 1 of 3

Units are algebra, not decoration

Every physical quantity is a number together with a unit, and the unit is not a label attached afterwards. It is an algebraic object that multiplies, divides and cancels exactly as a number does. Writing 45 cubic metres per hour as \( 45\ \mathrm{m^3/h} \) means literally 45 multiplied by cubic metres divided by hours, and every later step may treat it that way.

That gives the single most useful check in engineering calculation. The principle is called dimensional homogeneity, written \( [\mathrm{LHS}] = [\mathrm{RHS}] \) and read as ‘the dimensions of the left-hand side equal the dimensions of the right-hand side’. Both sides of any physical equation must carry identical dimensions. If they do not, the equation is wrong, and you know it before substituting a single number.

Take hydraulic power, the power delivered to a fluid, written \( P = \rho g Q H \) and read as ‘P equals rho g Q H’. Here the density, written rho \( (\rho) \), is mass per unit volume; the gravitational acceleration is \( g \); the volumetric flow rate is \( Q \); and the head is \( H \). Substitute dimensions rather than values, using M for mass, L for length and T for time:

\[ \frac{M}{L^3}\cdot\frac{L}{T^2}\cdot\frac{L^3}{T}\cdot L = \frac{M L^2}{T^3} \]

which is the dimension of a watt. The equation survives the check, so it is at least dimensionally possible. This is a necessary condition and not a sufficient one: a wrong constant in front of a correct grouping passes it. What it reliably catches is a dropped term, a mis-transcribed exponent, or a variable substituted into the wrong slot.

One consequence is worth stating on its own. The argument of a transcendental function must be dimensionless. There is no such thing as \( \ln \) of a metre. Whenever such a function appears, whatever sits inside it is a ratio, and identifying that ratio usually explains the physics.

A function is a machine's behaviour, written down

In service you rarely meet a function as an abstract rule. You meet it as a curve on a datasheet: the head a pump produces at each flow, the torque a motor produces at each speed, the temperature of a bearing against time after start-up. The function notation \( H(Q) \), read ‘H of Q’, simply names that relationship — the head that corresponds to a given flow.

Two things follow, and both matter more in practice than the algebra does. The first is that the domain is a physical statement. A pump curve is drawn between shut-off and runout, and outside that interval the relationship is not merely undefined in the mathematical sense — it is forbidden in the engineering sense. Reading a value off an extrapolated curve is how equipment gets specified into cavitation.

The second is that the shape carries the engineering. A curve that falls steeply with flow behaves differently in a system than one that is nearly flat, and the difference decides whether two pumps can be run in parallel, how a control valve behaves, and how sensitive the duty point is to a fouled strainer. You will meet the formal machinery for describing shape — slope, curvature, maxima — in later lessons. For now the discipline is to read a curve as a statement about a machine rather than as a picture.

Note the notation clash you will meet everywhere: \( P \) is used for power and \( p \) for pressure. They are different quantities with different dimensions, and the only defence is to state which you mean the first time you use it in any calculation.

Magnitude first, arithmetic second

An answer that is wrong by a factor of a thousand is far commoner than one that is wrong by ten per cent, because the usual cause is not faulty arithmetic but a unit left unconverted. A flow rate given in cubic metres per hour and used as though it were cubic metres per second is wrong by exactly 3600, and the arithmetic that follows it is flawless.

The protection is an estimate formed before the calculation. Small process pumps are kilowatt machines; a transfer pump you could carry is not a 300 kW machine, and any route to that number is wrong. Holding a rough expectation converts a silent error into an obvious one.

The working method, used from here on.

  • One. Write the equation and check its dimensions before any number goes in.
  • Two. Convert every input to base SI — metres, kilograms, seconds, pascals, watts. Do this in one place, visibly.
  • Three. Estimate the answer to within a factor of ten, and write the estimate down.
  • Four. Do the arithmetic, carrying units through it.
  • Five. Compare against the estimate, then convert to whatever unit the reader expects — kilowatts, bar, metres of water.

Step five matters as much as step two. A result is communicated to someone holding a gauge in bar or a datasheet in cubic metres per hour, and an answer left in base SI transfers the conversion risk to them.

MTH 101 · Lesson 01 · Functions, units, and engineering magnitudes · worked example 1

Hydraulic power, and the trap in the flow unit

Problem. A pump delivers \( Q = 45\ \mathrm{m^3/h} \) of water against a head of \( H = 32\ \mathrm{m} \). Take the density as \( \rho = 998\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} \) and \( g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \). Find the hydraulic power delivered to the water.

Approach. Use \( P = \rho g Q H \). The whole difficulty is in the units, not the algebra: the equation returns watts only when every input is in base SI, and flow on a datasheet is almost always quoted in cubic metres per hour. Convert the flow first, in its own visible step, because doing it silently inside the arithmetic is exactly how the factor of 3600 goes missing. Estimate before calculating: a few tens of cubic metres per hour against a few tens of metres of head is a small pump, so expect single-figure kilowatts.

Solution.

Step 1 — convert the flow to base SI.
\( Q = 45/3600 = 0.0125\ \mathrm{m^3/s} \).

Step 2 — group the constants.
\( \rho g = 998 \times 9.81 = 9790\ \mathrm{kg/(m^2 s^2)} \).

Step 3 — multiply through.
\( P = 9790 \times 0.0125 \times 32 = 3916\ \mathrm{W} \).

Step 4 — compare with the estimate and report.
3.92 kW is single-figure kilowatts, as expected.

Answer. \( P = 3.92\ \mathrm{kW} \).

The trap. Leaving \( Q \) at 45 gives 14 098 kW — a 14-megawatt transfer pump. The arithmetic is perfect and the answer is wrong by exactly 3600. The estimate in the Approach is what catches it.

MTH 101 · Lesson 01 · Functions, units, and engineering magnitudes · worked example 2

Pressure head, in SI and in customary units

Problem. (a) A gauge reads 1 bar. Express this as a head of water. (b) A datasheet from a supplier quotes 30 psi. Express that as a head of water in both metres and feet. Use \( \rho = 998\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} \), \( g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \), and \( 1\ \mathrm{psi} = 6894.76\ \mathrm{Pa} \).

Approach. Pressure head is \( h = p/(\rho g) \), read ‘h equals p divided by rho g’, where the pressure is written lower-case \( p \) to keep it distinct from power. Convert each pressure to pascals first; the equation returns metres only from pascals. Part (b) is included because supplier data does not arrive in SI, and converting at the boundary — on the way in, and again on the way out — is the habit that keeps the middle of a calculation clean.

Solution.

(a) Step 1. \( 1\ \mathrm{bar} = 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa} \).
Step 2. \( h = 10^5/(998 \times 9.81) = 10^5/9790 = 10.21\ \mathrm{m} \).

(b) Step 1. \( p = 30 \times 6894.76 = 206\,843\ \mathrm{Pa} \).
Step 2. \( h = 206\,843/9790 = 21.13\ \mathrm{m} \).
Step 3. \( 21.13/0.3048 = 69.3\ \mathrm{ft} \).

Answer. (a) 10.21 m of water. (b) 21.13 m, or 69.3 ft.

Worth remembering. One bar is very nearly 10.2 m of water, so a pressure in bar can be turned into an approximate head by multiplying by 10. That is a useful field estimate and a good check on part (b): 30 psi is about 2.07 bar, so roughly 21 m — which is what the full calculation returned.

MTH 101 · Lesson 02 · Limits and continuity

Lesson 02Limits and continuity

A limit describes what a quantity is heading towards, which is not always the same as what it equals. Engineering needs the distinction constantly: a formula may be undefined at exactly the operating point you care about while still having a perfectly definite value approaching it. Continuity is the companion idea — the guarantee that a small change of input produces a small change of output, which is what makes a measurement trustworthy.

Key points

  • A limit asks what \( f(x) \) approaches as \( x \) approaches a value, deliberately ignoring what happens exactly at that value.
  • A limit can exist where the function does not. That is the useful case, not an edge case.
  • A two-sided limit exists only if the left-hand and right-hand limits agree.
  • A function is continuous at a point when the limit exists, the function is defined there, and the two are equal.
  • A removable discontinuity is a hole that algebra can cancel; a jump cannot be cancelled, and an infinite one means the output runs away.
  • \( 0/0 \) is not an answer, it is a signal to factor, cancel or rearrange before substituting.
  • Physically, continuity is the promise that a small nudge of input gives a small nudge of output — the assumption behind every calibration.

What a limit is, and why the value at the point is excluded

The limit of a function, written \( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) \) and read aloud as ‘the limit of f of x as x approaches a’, is the value the output heads towards as the input closes in on \( a \). The definition deliberately says nothing about what happens when the input equals \( a \). That exclusion is not a technicality; it is the whole reason the idea earns its place.

Consider a quantity defined as a ratio that collapses at one particular operating point — a formula with \( x - 2 \) in the denominator, evaluated at \( x = 2 \). Substituting gives \( 0/0 \), which is an indeterminate form: not a number, and not a verdict, but an instruction to do some algebra before substituting. Cancel the common factor and the surviving expression usually has a perfectly ordinary value there. The function had a hole; the physical quantity did not.

Approaching can be done from two sides, and they need not agree. The left-hand limit \( \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) \), read ‘the limit as x approaches a from the left’, uses only inputs below \( a \); the right-hand limit uses only inputs above it. A two-sided limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal. This is why a controller that switches behaviour at a threshold has to be examined from both directions: the approach from below and the approach from above are different questions with possibly different answers.

MTH 101 · Lesson 02 · Limits and continuity · sheet 2 of 2

Continuity, and the three ways it fails

A function is continuous at a point when three separate things are true: the limit exists there, the function is actually defined there, and the two are equal — written compactly as \( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a) \). All three are required, and the three ways of failing are worth naming because they behave differently in practice.

A removable discontinuity is a single missing point. The limit exists, the function simply is not defined there, and algebra can fill the hole. Physically this is almost always an artefact of how the formula was written rather than anything the equipment does.

A jump discontinuity is different in kind. Both one-sided limits exist but they disagree, so no two-sided limit exists and no algebra repairs it. A relay changing state and a valve snapping between seats are genuine jumps: the output really does take two different values either side of the threshold.

An infinite discontinuity means the output grows without bound as the point is approached. In equipment terms that is a warning rather than a value — a model predicting infinite pressure or infinite current is telling you the model has left its valid range.

The engineering content of continuity is a promise: a small change of input gives a small change of output. Every calibration, every interpolation between two table entries and every controller tuned on a local slope assumes it. Where it fails, those methods fail with it, and the failure is usually silent.

MTH 101 · Lesson 02 · Limits and continuity · worked example 1

A hole that algebra removes

Problem. Evaluate \( \lim_{x \to 2} \dfrac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} \).

Approach. Substitute first, always — it either gives the answer immediately or tells you which technique you need. Here it gives \( 0/0 \), the indeterminate form, so the expression must be rearranged before it will yield anything. The numerator is a difference of two squares, which shares a factor with the denominator. The classic error is to report ‘undefined’ from the first substitution and stop; the function is indeed undefined at \( x = 2 \), but the limit is a different question and it has an ordinary answer.

Solution.

Step 1 — substitute. \( (4-4)/(2-2) = 0/0 \). Indeterminate, so continue.

Step 2 — factor. \( x^2 - 4 = (x-2)(x+2) \).

Step 3 — cancel. For every \( x \neq 2 \) the expression equals \( x + 2 \). The limit only concerns \( x \neq 2 \), so this cancellation is legitimate.

Step 4 — substitute into the survivor. \( 2 + 2 = 4 \).

Answer. The limit is 4. Numerically, inputs of 2.1, 2.01 and 2.001 give 4.1, 4.01 and 4.001 — closing on 4 as expected, which is a useful sanity check on any limit you have done algebraically.

MTH 101 · Lesson 02 · Limits and continuity · worked example 2

Checking a piecewise signal for continuity

Problem. A transmitter output is modelled as \( f(x) = 2x + 1 \) for \( x < 3 \) and \( f(x) = x + 4 \) for \( x \ge 3 \). Is the output continuous at \( x = 3 \)?

Approach. Continuity at a junction needs all three conditions, so test them in order rather than eyeballing the graph: the left-hand limit, the right-hand limit, and the defined value. The common mistake is to check only that the function is defined at the junction — a jump discontinuity is defined at the junction too, and jumps are exactly what this test exists to find.

Solution.

Step 1 — left-hand limit. Using the rule for \( x < 3 \): \( \lim_{x \to 3^-} f(x) = 2(3) + 1 = 7 \).

Step 2 — right-hand limit. Using the rule for \( x \ge 3 \): \( \lim_{x \to 3^+} f(x) = 3 + 4 = 7 \).

Step 3 — the two agree, so the two-sided limit exists and equals 7.

Step 4 — the defined value. \( f(3) = 3 + 4 = 7 \), which matches the limit.

Answer. Continuous at \( x = 3 \). Had the second rule been \( x + 5 \), the right-hand limit would have been 8 against a left-hand limit of 7 — a jump of one unit, no two-sided limit, and a signal that steps discontinuously as it crosses the threshold.

MTH 101 · Lesson 03 · The derivative as a rate

Lesson 03The derivative as a rate

The derivative is the rate at which one quantity changes with respect to another, defined as the limit of an average rate as the measuring interval shrinks to nothing. Almost every engineering quantity that matters is a derivative of something else: velocity from position, flow from volume, heating rate from temperature. This lesson defines it and reads it as a slope.

Key points

  • An average rate of change over an interval is a difference quotient; the derivative is what it approaches as the interval shrinks to zero.
  • \( f'(x) \) and \( dy/dx \) name the same object — the instantaneous rate of change.
  • Geometrically the derivative is the slope of the tangent: the slope the curve has at one point rather than across a span.
  • The units of a derivative are always the output's units divided by the input's, which makes it self-checking.
  • A positive derivative means increasing, negative means decreasing, and zero means momentarily stationary.
  • Differentiability implies continuity, but continuity does not imply differentiability — a sharp corner is continuous and has no single slope.
  • Reading a derivative off equipment data is reading a gradient: how fast the reading moves, not how large it is.

MTH 101 · Lesson 03 · The derivative as a rate · sheet 1 of 2

From average rate to instantaneous rate

An average rate of change is easy and familiar: total change divided by the interval it happened over. Written for a function it is the difference quotient \( \dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \), read aloud as ‘f of x plus h, minus f of x, all over h’, where \( h \) is the width of the interval. It is the slope of the straight line joining two points on the curve.

That average conceals whatever happened inside the interval. A pump that ran hard for one minute and idled for nine has the same average as one that ran steadily, and if what you need is the rate now, the average is the wrong number. The fix is to shrink the interval, and the derivative is what the difference quotient approaches as \( h \) approaches zero.

Notice that this is exactly the previous lesson's machinery doing work. Setting \( h = 0 \) outright gives \( 0/0 \) — the indeterminate form — because both the change and the interval vanish together. The limit asks what the ratio approaches without ever setting \( h \) to zero, which is precisely the question that has an answer.

Two notations are standard and mean the same thing. \( f'(x) \), read ‘f prime of x’, is compact. \( \dfrac{dy}{dx} \), read ‘d y by d x’, keeps both variables in view, which matters when several quantities vary at once and you must be explicit about what is changing with respect to what.

MTH 101 · Lesson 03 · The derivative as a rate · sheet 2 of 2

Reading a derivative: slope, sign, and units

Geometrically the derivative is the slope of the tangent line — the straight line that touches the curve at one point and shares its direction there. The difference quotient is the slope of a line cutting the curve at two points; as the second point slides towards the first, that cutting line pivots into the tangent.

The sign carries most of the practical information. Positive means the quantity is increasing, negative means decreasing, and zero means a stationary point \( f'(x) = 0 \), where the quantity is momentarily neither rising nor falling. A bearing temperature whose derivative has just turned positive is a different situation from one that is merely high and steady, and the derivative is what distinguishes them.

The units are automatic and worth using as a check. A derivative always carries the output's units divided by the input's: level in metres against time in seconds gives metres per second; volume against level gives cubic metres per metre. If a derivative comes out in units that do not match that rule, the differentiation went wrong, and you know it before checking the algebra.

One caution. A function that is differentiable at a point is certainly continuous there, but the reverse fails: a curve with a sharp corner is continuous and yet has no single slope at the corner, because the slope approaching from each side disagrees. Physical signals that switch regime produce exactly this.

MTH 101 · Lesson 03 · The derivative as a rate · worked example 1

A derivative from first principles

Problem. For \( f(x) = x^2 \), find \( f'(3) \) from the definition, and check it numerically.

Approach. Build the difference quotient, simplify it algebraically until \( h \) no longer sits in a denominator, and only then let \( h \) approach zero. The order matters: cancelling before taking the limit is what makes the limit exist at all. The classic error is to set \( h = 0 \) in the unsimplified quotient, which returns \( 0/0 \) and looks like a dead end.

Solution.

Step 1 — write the quotient at \( x = 3 \).
\( \dfrac{(3+h)^2 - 3^2}{h} \).

Step 2 — expand the numerator.
\( (9 + 6h + h^2) - 9 = 6h + h^2 \).

Step 3 — cancel \( h \).
\( \dfrac{6h + h^2}{h} = 6 + h \), valid for every \( h \neq 0 \).

Step 4 — take the limit. As \( h \) approaches zero, \( 6 + h \) approaches 6.

Answer. \( f'(3) = 6 \). Numerically, \( h = 0.1 \) gives 6.1, \( h = 0.01 \) gives 6.01 and \( h = 0.001 \) gives 6.001 — approaching 6 from above, exactly as the algebra says.

MTH 101 · Lesson 03 · The derivative as a rate · worked example 2

A rate with units attached

Problem. A tank's stored volume varies with level as \( V = 0.8h^2 \), with \( V \) in cubic metres and \( h \) in metres. Find \( dV/dh \) and evaluate it at \( h = 2.5\ \mathrm{m} \). State what the number means.

Approach. This derivative is taken with respect to level, not time, so it answers ‘how much extra volume per extra metre of level’ — the local capacity of the tank. Keeping the units in view is the guard against misreading it as a filling rate, which is a different derivative with respect to a different variable.

Solution.

Step 1 — differentiate. By the same first-principles argument used for \( x^2 \), the derivative of \( h^2 \) is \( 2h \), so \( dV/dh = 1.6h \).

Step 2 — evaluate. \( 1.6 \times 2.5 = 4.0 \).

Step 3 — attach units. Cubic metres divided by metres is square metres, so \( dV/dh = 4.0\ \mathrm{m^2} \) — which is the tank's cross-sectional area at that level, as it should be.

Answer. \( 4.0\ \mathrm{m^2} \): near a level of 2.5 m, each extra metre of level stores about 4 cubic metres more. Because the tank widens with height, this is a local figure and not a constant — at \( h = 1 \) it is only \( 1.6\ \mathrm{m^2} \).

MTH 101 · Lesson 04 · Differentiation rules and the chain rule

Lesson 04Differentiation rules and the chain rule

First principles work but are slow, so this lesson replaces them with rules. The product, quotient and chain rules let any combination of familiar functions be differentiated by inspection. The chain rule is the one that matters most in engineering, because real quantities are almost always functions of functions.

Key points

  • The power rule: the derivative of \( x^n \) is \( nx^{n-1} \).
  • Differentiation is linear — constants pull out, and sums differentiate term by term.
  • The product rule handles a product of two varying quantities and is not simply the product of the derivatives.
  • The quotient rule follows the same pattern with a minus sign, so the order of the two terms matters.
  • The chain rule differentiates a function of a function by multiplying the outer rate by the inner rate.
  • In Leibniz notation the chain rule reads as a cancellation of differentials, which is why it is easy to remember and easy to extend.
  • Identify the inner function first, before differentiating anything — most chain-rule errors are misidentification, not arithmetic.

The rules, and the one that is not a product

The power rule, \( \dfrac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1} \), read as ‘d by d x of x to the n equals n x to the n minus one’, does most of the routine work. Differentiation is also linear: a constant multiplier passes straight through, and a sum differentiates term by term. Those two facts alone handle every polynomial.

Products need more care. The product rule is \( (uv)' = u'v + uv' \) — ‘u v all prime equals u prime v plus u v prime’. The tempting shortcut, multiplying the two derivatives, is simply wrong, and the reason is physical rather than algebraic: when two quantities both vary, the product changes because the first changed while the second held, and because the second changed while the first held. Two contributions, hence two terms.

Mechanical power is the clean example. Power is torque times angular speed, and on a machine running up to load both are moving. The rate at which power changes has a term from the torque rising and a term from the speed falling, and those terms can partly cancel — which is exactly why the product rule, not a shortcut, is needed to say whether power is rising or falling at a given instant.

The quotient rule follows the same shape with a minus sign, \( \left(\dfrac{u}{v}\right)' = \dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2} \). Because of that minus, the order of the two products is not interchangeable; writing them the wrong way round flips the sign of every answer.

The chain rule: outer rate times inner rate

The chain rule handles a function of a function, which in engineering is the normal case rather than a special one. Written in Leibniz notation it is \( \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du} \dfrac{du}{dx} \), read as ‘d y by d x equals d y by d u times d u by d x’, where \( u \) is the inner function — whatever expression is sitting inside the other.

The notation is doing you a favour. The differentials appear to cancel, and while that is not a proof it is a reliable memory aid and it generalises to longer chains without modification. If a temperature depends on time through an exponent, and the exponent itself depends on time, the rate multiplies along the chain.

The working method. First name the inner function explicitly, in writing. Second, differentiate the outer function while treating the inner one as a single symbol. Third, multiply by the derivative of the inner function. Fourth, substitute the inner expression back.

Almost every chain-rule error is at the first step, not the third. Differentiating \( e^{-t/12} \) as if the exponent were simply \( -t \), and so forgetting the factor of one twelfth, is the standard slip; it produces an answer twelve times too large with no visible symptom. Naming the inner function on paper before differentiating is what prevents it.

One derivative to carry forward: the exponential is its own derivative, \( \dfrac{d}{dx}e^{x} = e^{x} \). Combined with the chain rule that single fact generates every growth and decay rate in the next lesson.

A cooling curve differentiated by the chain rule

Problem. A component cools according to \( T(t) = 20 + 60e^{-t/12} \), with \( T \) in °C and \( t \) in minutes. Find \( dT/dt \), and evaluate it at \( t = 0 \) and \( t = 12\ \mathrm{min} \).

Approach. The constant 20 differentiates to zero. The remaining term is an exponential of a function, so name the inner function first: \( u = -t/12 \), whose derivative is \( -1/12 \). Forgetting that factor is the standard error and would overstate every cooling rate by a factor of twelve, so it is worth writing down explicitly rather than carrying in your head.

Solution.

Step 1 — name the inner function. \( u = -t/12 \), so \( du/dt = -1/12 \).

Step 2 — differentiate the outer. The derivative of \( 60e^{u} \) with respect to \( u \) is \( 60e^{u} \).

Step 3 — multiply along the chain.
\( dT/dt = 60e^{-t/12} \times (-1/12) = -5e^{-t/12} \).

Step 4 — evaluate. At \( t = 0 \), \( -5(1) = -5 \). At \( t = 12 \), \( -5e^{-1} = -1.84 \).

Answer. \( dT/dt = -5e^{-t/12} \)°C/min: cooling at 5°C per minute initially, slowing to 1.84°C per minute after twelve minutes. The sign is negative throughout, as any cooling curve must be, and the units come out as degrees per minute — both worth checking.

MTH 101 · Lesson 04 · Differentiation rules and the chain rule · worked example 2

A product rule where the two terms fight

Problem. During a run-up, a drive's torque rises as \( T = 50 + 2t \) N·m and its angular speed falls as \( \omega = 100 - 0.5t \) rad/s, with \( t \) in seconds. Power is \( P = T\omega \). Find \( dP/dt \) at \( t = 10\ \mathrm{s} \) and say whether power is rising.

Approach. Both factors vary, so this is a product rule and not a case where one factor can be treated as constant. The interest is that the two contributions have opposite signs — torque is helping, speed is hurting — so the answer is a competition and its sign cannot be guessed. Multiplying the two derivatives together, the classic shortcut, would give 2 × (−0.5) = −1, which is not merely inaccurate but the wrong sign.

Solution.

Step 1 — the pieces. \( T' = 2 \), \( \omega' = -0.5 \).

Step 2 — values at \( t = 10 \). \( T = 50 + 20 = 70 \), \( \omega = 100 - 5 = 95 \).

Step 3 — apply the rule.
\( dP/dt = T'\omega + T\omega' = 2(95) + 70(-0.5) = 190 - 35 \).

Answer. \( dP/dt = 155\ \mathrm{W/s} \) — power is rising, because the torque term outweighs the speed term at this instant. Note it need not stay that way: as torque grows and speed falls, the second term grows in magnitude, and the sign can reverse later in the run-up.

MTH 101 · Lesson 05 · Exponentials, logarithms, and growth/decay

Lesson 05Exponentials, logarithms, and growth/decay

Exponential behaviour appears wherever a quantity's rate of change is proportional to the quantity itself — cooling, discharge, vibration decay, corrosion allowance, radioactive sources. The logarithm is its inverse, and it is the tool that turns a multiplicative process into a straight line you can read. This lesson gives both, and the two time constants engineers actually quote.

Key points

  • Exponential change means the rate is proportional to the amount present, which is why it never quite reaches zero.
  • \( e^{x} \) is its own derivative; every growth and decay rate follows from that one fact and the chain rule.
  • Decay is written \( A_0e^{-t/\tau} \), where the time constant is the time to fall to about 37% of the start.
  • The half-life is the time to halve, and it is a fixed multiple of the time constant: \( t_{1/2} = \tau \ln 2 \).
  • Logarithms turn products into sums, which is why they linearise multiplicative data and why decibels exist.
  • A straight line on a log axis is an exponential on a linear one — the quickest way to identify the behaviour from plant data.
  • Solving for time means taking a logarithm of both sides, so the natural log is the working tool rather than a curiosity.

MTH 101 · Lesson 05 · Exponentials, logarithms, and growth/decay · sheet 1 of 2

Why proportional rates produce exponentials

A great many industrial quantities share one behaviour: the rate at which they change is proportional to how much of them is left. A hot component loses heat fastest when it is hottest; a vessel discharges fastest when it is fullest; a vibration loses energy fastest when the amplitude is largest. Whenever that proportionality holds, the resulting curve is an exponential.

The reason sits in the previous lesson. The exponential \( e^{x} \), read ‘e to the x’, is the one function equal to its own derivative. So if a quantity's rate must stay proportional to the quantity, an exponential is the shape that satisfies it, and the chain rule supplies the constant of proportionality.

Decay is written \( A = A_0e^{-t/\tau} \), read ‘A equals A nought e to the minus t over tau’. Here \( A_0 \) is the initial value and \( \tau \), read ‘tau’, is the time constant — the time taken to fall to \( 1/e \), about 37%, of the start. Because the rate keeps shrinking with the amount, the curve never reaches zero. That is not a mathematical quibble: it is why a purge is specified as ‘five time constants’ rather than ‘until empty’.

Engineers more often quote the half-life, the time to fall to half. The two measures are rigidly linked, \( t_{1/2} = \tau \ln 2 \), so the half-life is always about 0.693 of the time constant. Neither depends on where you start measuring — the time to halve is the same from any point on the curve, which is the property that makes the number worth quoting at all.

Logarithms: releasing an exponent, and straightening a curve

The natural logarithm \( \ln x \) is the inverse of \( e^{x} \). Its working purpose is narrow and constant: when the unknown you want sits in an exponent, taking logarithms of both sides brings it down where algebra can reach it. Every ‘how long until’ question about an exponential process is answered this way.

The second use is diagnostic. Logarithms turn products into sums, so a process that multiplies by a fixed factor per unit time becomes a straight line when the quantity is plotted on a logarithmic axis. That is the quickest test available on plant data: plot the readings with a log vertical axis, and if they fall on a line the process is exponential and the slope gives the time constant directly. A curve that refuses to straighten is telling you the rate is not simply proportional to the amount, and that some other mechanism is present.

The same property explains the decibel, \( 10\log_{10}(P_2/P_1) \). Quantities spanning many orders of magnitude are unwieldy on a linear scale, and a logarithmic measure compresses them into a readable range while turning ratios into differences. Every 10 dB is one factor of ten in power, so a hundredfold increase is 20 dB — and because it is a ratio, a decibel figure is meaningless until the reference is stated.

Time constant and half-life from two readings

Problem. A machine's vibration amplitude is 12 mm when the drive trips and 3 mm eight seconds later. Assuming exponential decay, find the time constant and the half-life, and predict the amplitude at 20 s.

Approach. Two readings and one assumed shape determine the curve. Substitute both into \( A = A_0e^{-t/\tau} \) and take logarithms to release \( \tau \) from the exponent. Before calculating, notice that 12 to 3 is two halvings in eight seconds, so the half-life should come out at 4 s — a check worth forming first, because the commonest error here is a dropped minus sign, which returns a negative time constant and a curve that grows.

Solution.

Step 1 — substitute. \( 3 = 12e^{-8/\tau} \), so \( e^{-8/\tau} = 0.25 \).

Step 2 — take logarithms. \( -8/\tau = \ln 0.25 = -1.386 \).

Step 3 — solve. \( \tau = 8/1.386 = 5.77\ \mathrm{s} \).

Step 4 — half-life. \( t_{1/2} = \tau \ln 2 = 5.77 \times 0.693 = 4.00\ \mathrm{s} \), matching the check.

Step 5 — predict. Twenty seconds is five half-lives, so \( 12/2^5 = 0.375\ \mathrm{mm} \).

Answer. \( \tau = 5.77\ \mathrm{s} \), \( t_{1/2} = 4.00\ \mathrm{s} \), amplitude 0.375 mm at 20 s.

A ratio expressed logarithmically

Problem. A modification raises a fan's radiated sound power by a factor of 100. Express the increase in decibels. Separately, an instrument datasheet quotes a 3 dB rise — what power ratio is that?

The classic error is treating decibels as additive in the quantity rather than in the exponent: 20 dB is not twice 10 dB in power terms, it is ten times it.

Lesson 06Extrema, curve behavior, and optimization

A stationary point is where a quantity stops rising or falling, and that is where optima live. This lesson uses the first derivative to find candidates and the second derivative to classify them, then applies both to a design question: choosing dimensions that minimise material for a required duty.

Key points

  • Optima occur where the first derivative is zero, or at the ends of the allowed range — endpoints are candidates too and are often forgotten.
  • The second derivative measures how the slope itself is changing, which is what distinguishes a minimum from a maximum.
  • Positive second derivative at a stationary point means a minimum; negative means a maximum.
  • An inflection is where curvature changes sign; a stationary point can be neither a maximum nor a minimum.
  • Real optimisation starts by writing the objective in ONE variable, using the constraint to eliminate the others.
  • The constraint is what makes the problem finite — without it, most engineering objectives have no interior optimum.
  • Check the answer is a minimum rather than assuming it; the algebra is happy to hand you a maximum.

Finding candidates, then classifying them

Where a quantity is at its largest or smallest, it is momentarily neither rising nor falling, so its derivative is zero. That gives the search a starting point: solve \( f'(x) = 0 \) to find stationary points. It is a search for candidates, not for answers, and two habits keep it honest.

The first is to remember the endpoints. If the variable is confined to a range — and physical variables always are, since a radius cannot be negative and a pressure cannot exceed a rating — then the extreme value may sit at an end of that range where the derivative is nowhere near zero. Endpoints are candidates and must be evaluated alongside the stationary points.

The second is to classify what you find. The second derivative \( f''(x) \), read ‘f double prime of x’, is the derivative of the derivative: it measures how fast the slope is changing, which is curvature. At a stationary point a positive second derivative means the curve is bending upwards, so the point is a local minimum; negative means bending downwards and a local maximum.

If the second derivative is zero the test is silent and says nothing either way — the point may be an inflection, where curvature changes sign without any optimum at all. In that case fall back on examining the sign of the first derivative on both sides.

MTH 101 · Lesson 06 · Extrema, curve behavior, and optimization · sheet 2 of 2

Turning a design question into one variable

Optimisation problems arrive with several dimensions free and one requirement fixed, and the entire method is to use the requirement to eliminate variables until the quantity you care about depends on one thing.

The quantity to be minimised or maximised is the objective function — material area, cost, energy, pressure drop. The fixed requirement is the constraint — a duty, a capacity, a required flow. Without a constraint most engineering objectives have no interior optimum at all: the cheapest vessel is one of zero size, which is why the constraint is what makes the question meaningful rather than a complication.

The working method. One: name the variables and write the objective in terms of all of them. Two: write the constraint as an equation. Three: solve the constraint for one variable and substitute, so the objective depends on a single variable. Four: differentiate, set to zero, and solve. Five: classify with the second derivative, and check the endpoints of the physical range. Six: back-substitute to recover the other dimensions.

Step three is where most of the difficulty lives, and step five is where most of the errors do. A stationary point found and never classified is a common way to present a maximum as though it were a minimum, and the algebra will not object.

The least material for a required capacity

Problem. An open-top cylindrical vessel must hold \( 2\ \mathrm{m^3} \). Find the radius and height that use the least material, and the resulting surface area.

Approach. Objective: the surface area \( S \) of a base plus a wall, with no lid. Constraint: the fixed volume. The trap is forgetting that the vessel is open-topped and including a second circle, which changes the answer entirely — so write the area from the actual geometry rather than recalling a formula for a closed cylinder.

Solution.

Step 2 — eliminate \( H \). \( H = 2/(\pi r^2) \), so \( S = \pi r^2 + 4/r \).

Step 3 — differentiate and set to zero. \( dS/dr = 2\pi r - 4/r^2 = 0 \), giving \( r^3 = 2/\pi \) and \( r = 0.860\ \mathrm{m} \).

Step 4 — classify. \( S''(r) = 2\pi + 8/r^3 = 18.85 > 0 \), so it is a minimum.

Step 5 — back-substitute. \( H = 2/(\pi \times 0.740) = 0.860\ \mathrm{m} \).

Answer. \( r = H = 0.860\ \mathrm{m} \), \( S = 6.97\ \mathrm{m^2} \). The height equals the radius exactly — a general result for an open-top cylinder, independent of the volume required, and a useful thing to recognise on a drawing.

MTH 101 · Lesson 07 · The integral as accumulation

Lesson 07The integral as accumulation

Differentiation asks how fast something changes; integration asks how much has accumulated. If you know a rate and want a total — a flow and want a volume, a power and want an energy, a corrosion rate and want a wall loss — the operation you need is a definite integral. This lesson builds it as a limit of sums and reads it as an area.

Key points

  • Integrating a rate over an interval gives the total accumulated over that interval, in the rate's units multiplied by the interval's.
  • A definite integral is defined as the limit of a sum of narrow strips, which is why it equals the area under the curve.
  • The area interpretation is literal: strip width times strip height, summed.
  • Areas below the axis count as negative, so an integral can represent a net rather than a gross total.
  • The Fundamental Theorem links the two halves of calculus: integrate a rate by finding a function whose derivative is that rate.
  • The average value of a quantity over an interval is its integral divided by the interval length.
  • A numerical sum with finitely many strips approaches the exact integral as the strips narrow — which is how instruments actually totalise.

MTH 101 · Lesson 07 · The integral as accumulation · sheet 1 of 2

Accumulating a rate: sums that become an integral

Suppose a flow rate varies through a shift and you need the volume that passed. If the rate were constant, the answer would be rate times time. It is not constant, so the honest approach is to chop the shift into short intervals, treat the rate as constant across each one, multiply and add.

That is a Riemann sum, \( \sum f(x_i)\,\Delta x \), read as ‘the sum of f of x i times delta x’, where \( \Delta x \) is the width of one interval. Each term is one rectangle: a height taken from the rate curve and a width taken from the interval. The sum is an approximation, and it is wrong by however much the rate moved inside each interval.

Narrow the intervals and that error shrinks. The definite integral \( \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \), read ‘the integral from a to b of f of x d x’, is defined as what the sum approaches as the strips become arbitrarily narrow. The elongated S is a sum sign and the \( dx \) is the vanishing width, so the notation is a picture of the construction.

Two consequences follow immediately. The integral equals the area under the curve, because that is literally what was summed — and area below the axis counts as negative, so an integral of a signed quantity gives a net rather than a gross total. And the units follow the multiplication: a flow in cubic metres per second integrated over seconds gives cubic metres, which is the same self-check that worked for derivatives.

MTH 101 · Lesson 07 · The integral as accumulation · sheet 2 of 2

Evaluating exactly, and reading an average

Summing strips converges but is laborious. The exact route is the Fundamental Theorem, \( \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) \), where \( F \) is an antiderivative — any function whose derivative is the integrand. Integration is therefore differentiation run backwards: to accumulate a rate, find the quantity whose rate it is, and difference that quantity between the endpoints.

This is why the previous lessons' rules are not left behind. Knowing that the derivative of \( x^2 \) is \( 2x \) tells you at once that the accumulation of \( 2x \) is \( x^2 \), up to a constant that cancels in the difference.

The integral also delivers averages, which is often what is actually wanted. The average value over an interval is the integral divided by the interval length, \( \bar{f} = \dfrac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f\,dx \). Read physically, it is the constant value that would have accumulated the same total in the same time — the steady flow equivalent to a varying one, or the steady load equivalent to a fluctuating one.

That definition is worth holding onto because it is not the same as averaging a handful of instrument readings. Readings taken at uneven intervals, or during a period when the quantity moved quickly, give a different and generally wrong answer. The integral weights every instant by the time it actually lasted.

Volume from a ramping flow

Problem. During start-up a flow rises linearly as \( Q(t) = 0.05 + 0.01t \), with \( Q \) in \( \mathrm{m^3/s} \) and \( t \) in seconds. Find the volume passed in the first 60 s, and the average flow over that period.

Approach. Volume is the accumulation of flow, so integrate the rate over the interval. The integrand is a sum of two simple terms, so the antiderivative is immediate. The error to avoid is multiplying the final flow by the elapsed time — that treats a ramp as though it had been at its maximum throughout, and here it would overstate the volume by more than half.

Solution.

Step 1 — set up. \( \int_0^{60}(0.05 + 0.01t)\,dt \).

Step 2 — antiderivative. \( F(t) = 0.05t + 0.005t^2 \), whose derivative is the integrand.

Step 3 — difference the endpoints. \( F(60) - F(0) = (3.0 + 18.0) - 0 = 21.0 \).

Step 4 — average. \( 21.0/60 = 0.35\ \mathrm{m^3/s} \).

Answer. 21.0 m³ passed, at an average flow of 0.35 m³/s. The wrong method gives \( 0.65 \times 60 = 39 \) m³. Note the average equals the flow at the mid-point, 30 s, which is a property of a linear ramp and a quick check on this class of problem.

Watching a Riemann sum converge

This is exactly how a flow totaliser accumulates from periodic samples, so the convergence rate is a practical matter rather than an academic one.

Step 1 — six strips, \( \Delta t = 10\ \mathrm{s} \), heights taken at 0, 10, ... 50 s: total 18.0 m³.

MTH 101 · Lesson 08 · Integration techniques

Lesson 08Integration techniques

Most integrals met in engineering are not in any table. Two techniques cover the great majority: substitution, which reverses the chain rule, and integration by parts, which reverses the product rule. Each is a differentiation rule read backwards, which is also how you decide which one a given integral needs.

Key points

  • Substitution reverses the chain rule: look for an inner function whose derivative also appears as a factor.
  • When substituting in a definite integral, change the limits too, or convert back before evaluating — never mix the two.
  • Integration by parts reverses the product rule and is the tool for a product of unlike things, such as a power multiplied by an exponential.
  • The choice of which factor to differentiate decides whether the new integral is easier or worse; pick the one that simplifies when differentiated.
  • By parts may need repeating, and each pass should visibly reduce the difficulty.
  • Every indefinite integral carries an arbitrary constant, which cancels in a definite integral and matters in a differential equation.
  • Check any integration by differentiating the answer — it is fast and it is conclusive.

MTH 101 · Lesson 08 · Integration techniques · sheet 1 of 2

Substitution: the chain rule, read backwards

Differentiating a function of a function multiplies by the inner derivative. Integration therefore has to divide it out again, and substitution is the bookkeeping that does so safely.

The pattern to recognise is an integrand containing some inner expression together with that expression's own derivative as a factor. Name the inner expression \( u = g(x) \), form its differential \( du = g'(x)\,dx \), and the integral rewrites in \( u \) alone. If the derivative is not present, up to a constant multiple, substitution is not the right tool and forcing it produces nonsense.

For a definite integral there are two honest routes and one common error. Either convert the limits into the new variable and evaluate there, or keep the old limits, integrate, and convert back to \( x \) before substituting them. The error is to change the variable and then use the original limits, which silently evaluates the right function at the wrong points.

Every indefinite integral carries a constant of integration \( C \), because differentiation destroys constants and integration cannot recover them. In a definite integral it cancels between the two endpoints and can be ignored. In a differential equation it is the whole point, since it is what the initial condition fixes — which is the subject two lessons ahead.

MTH 101 · Lesson 08 · Integration techniques · sheet 2 of 2

By parts: the product rule, read backwards

The product rule says a product's derivative has two terms. Rearranged and integrated, it gives integration by parts, \( \int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du \), read as ‘the integral of u d v equals u v minus the integral of v d u’. It does not evaluate an integral outright; it trades one integral for another, and the technique is worth using only when the trade is favourable.

That makes the choice of which factor to call \( u \) the whole skill. The factor you name \( u \) gets differentiated, so choose the one that becomes simpler when differentiated. A power of the variable is the usual choice, because differentiating it lowers the power and repeating the process eventually reduces it to a constant. An exponential is the usual \( dv \), because integrating it changes nothing.

Choosing the other way round is not wrong so much as useless: the new integral comes out harder than the old one. The diagnostic is immediate — if one application leaves you worse off, swap the roles and start again rather than pressing on.

Integrals of the form ‘a power times an exponential’ are the standard industrial case, appearing whenever an accumulating quantity is weighted by a decaying one: energy dissipated during a decaying transient, or the mean time associated with an exponential reliability model.

Whatever technique is used, the check is the same and takes seconds: differentiate the answer and confirm you recover the integrand.

A substitution, with the limits changed

Problem. Evaluate \( \int_0^2 2x\,e^{x^2}\,dx \).

Approach. The exponent \( x^2 \) is an inner function and its derivative \( 2x \) is sitting right there as a factor, which is exactly the signature of a substitution. Change the limits along with the variable — when \( x \) runs 0 to 2, \( u \) runs 0 to 4. Using the original limits with the new variable would evaluate \( e^{u} \) between 0 and 2 instead of 0 and 4, giving 6.39 rather than the true value: a plausible-looking number with nothing wrong on the page.

Solution.

Step 1 — substitute. \( u = x^2 \), so \( du = 2x\,dx \).

Step 2 — convert the limits. \( x = 0 \Rightarrow u = 0 \); \( x = 2 \Rightarrow u = 4 \).

Step 3 — rewrite and integrate. \( \int_0^4 e^{u}\,du = e^{u}\big|_0^4 \).

Step 4 — evaluate. \( e^4 - e^0 = 54.598 - 1 = 53.598 \).

Answer. 53.598. Check by differentiating \( e^{x^2} \): the chain rule returns \( 2x\,e^{x^2} \), the original integrand.

By parts on a decaying transient

Problem. Evaluate \( \int_0^{10} t\,e^{-t/5}\,dt \).

Approach. A power multiplied by an exponential: no substitution will help, because the exponent's derivative is a constant and not the other factor. Use by parts, and take \( u = t \) so that differentiating reduces it to 1. Taking \( u = e^{-t/5} \) instead would leave \( t^2/2 \) in the new integral — a higher power than you started with, which is the signal to swap the roles rather than continue.

Solution.

Step 1 — assign the parts. \( u = t \), \( dv = e^{-t/5}\,dt \), so \( du = dt \) and \( v = -5e^{-t/5} \).

Step 2 — apply the formula. \( \left[-5t\,e^{-t/5}\right]_0^{10} + 5\int_0^{10} e^{-t/5}\,dt \).

Step 3 — first term. \( -50e^{-2} - 0 = -6.767 \).

Step 4 — second term. \( 5\left[-5e^{-t/5}\right]_0^{10} = 25(1 - e^{-2}) = 21.617 \).

Answer. \( -6.767 + 21.617 = 14.850 \). A numerical mid-point sum over the same interval gives 14.8499, confirming it. Note the first term is negative and the second positive — a sign pattern that is normal here and not an error.

Lesson 09Applications: areas, volumes, work, and averages

Integration earns its keep when the thing being accumulated is not a rate in time. Areas between curves, volumes of revolution, work against a varying force and true averages are all the same operation applied to different integrands. This lesson is about choosing the integrand correctly, which is where the engineering sits.

Key points

  • Every application is the same construction: slice the object, write one slice's contribution, then integrate.
  • Getting the slice right is the whole problem; the integration afterwards is routine.
  • Work is force integrated over distance, and it needs an integral exactly when the force varies.
  • For pumping problems the varying quantity is usually the lift, not the weight of the slice.
  • The average value of a quantity is its integral divided by the interval, not the mean of a few readings.
  • Root-mean-square is a different average again — square, average, then take the root, in that order.
  • For a sinusoid the RMS value is the peak divided by root two, which is why AC quantities are quoted that way.

MTH 101 · Lesson 09 · Applications: areas, volumes, work, and averages · sheet 1 of 2

Slice, write one contribution, integrate

Every application in this lesson uses one construction. Cut the object or the process into slices thin enough that the varying quantity is effectively constant across each one. Write what a single slice contributes. Then integrate that expression over the range of slices.

The integration is routine. Writing the slice is where the engineering is, and it is where the errors are. The reliable discipline is to write the slice's contribution as a product of a small quantity and whatever varies, then check its units before integrating — a slice contribution to a work integral must come out in joules, and if it does not, the slice is wrong and no amount of correct integration will rescue it.

Consider emptying a tank. It is tempting to compute the total weight of liquid and multiply by some height, but that is only valid if every part of the liquid is lifted the same distance, and it is not. A slice near the top is barely lifted; a slice at the bottom is lifted the full depth. The thing that varies from slice to slice is the lift, so the lift must be inside the integral.

That single observation is what makes the answer come out as \( \rho gAH^2/2 \) rather than \( \rho gAH^2 \) — a factor of two, which is the difference between sizing a pump correctly and sizing it at twice the duty. The one-half is the average lift being half the depth, and it appears because the integration did the averaging for you.

MTH 101 · Lesson 09 · Applications: areas, volumes, work, and averages · sheet 2 of 2

Two averages that are not the same

The average value of a varying quantity over an interval is its integral divided by the interval length: the constant value that would have accumulated the same total. This is not the same as the mean of a handful of readings unless those readings happen to be evenly spaced and the quantity happens to be well behaved between them.

For many purposes that plain average is the wrong one anyway. A quantity that swings symmetrically about zero — an alternating current, a vibration velocity, a pressure pulsation — has a plain average of zero, which is true and useless. What matters is the magnitude of the swing, and the standard measure is the root-mean-square.

The name is the recipe, and the order is not negotiable: square the quantity, average the square, then take the square root. Squaring first is what removes the sign; averaging afterwards weights every instant by its duration; the root returns the result to the original units.

For a sinusoid the RMS value works out as the peak divided by root two, about 0.707 of the peak. It is worth contrasting with the average of the magnitude over half a cycle, which is \( 2/\pi \) or about 0.637 of the peak — a different number for a different question. Quoting one where the other is meant is a standard source of an 11% discrepancy that nobody can find.

MTH 101 · Lesson 09 · Applications: areas, volumes, work, and averages · worked example 1

Work to empty a tank

Problem. A vertical cylindrical tank of radius \( 1.5\ \mathrm{m} \) is filled with water to a depth of \( 4\ \mathrm{m} \). Find the work needed to pump it all up to the level of the tank top. Take \( \rho = 998\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} \) and \( g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \).

Approach. Slice horizontally. Each slice has the same area and the same weight, but a different lift, so the lift is what goes inside the integral. Measuring height upward from the bottom, a slice at height \( y \) must be raised \( (H - y) \). The classic error is to multiply the total weight by the full depth, which lifts every drop from the bottom and overstates the work by exactly a factor of two.

Solution.

Step 1 — area. \( A = \pi(1.5)^2 = 7.069\ \mathrm{m^2} \).

Step 2 — one slice. Weight \( \rho gA\,dy \), lifted \( (4 - y) \), so \( dW = \rho gA(4-y)\,dy \).

Step 3 — integrate 0 to 4. \( \int_0^4 (4-y)\,dy = 8 \), so \( W = \rho gA \times 8 \).

Step 4 — evaluate. \( 998 \times 9.81 \times 7.069 \times 8 = 5.536\times10^5 \).

Answer. \( W = 554\ \mathrm{kJ} \), or 0.554 MJ. Note the general form \( \rho gAH^2/2 \): the average lift is half the depth, which the integral produced without being told.

MTH 101 · Lesson 09 · Applications: areas, volumes, work, and averages · worked example 2

RMS against mean, on the same waveform

Problem. For a sinusoid of peak value 1, find the root-mean-square value over a full cycle and the average of its magnitude over a half cycle. Comment on the difference.

Approach. Two different averages of the same waveform, so the point is to keep the operations in the right order. RMS squares first, which turns the sine into a squared sine whose average over a whole number of cycles is exactly one half. The magnitude average has no squaring, so it must be taken over a half cycle — over a full cycle the signed average is zero, which is the trap.

Solution.

Step 1 — mean square. The average of \( \sin^2 \) over a full cycle is \( 1/2 \).

Step 2 — take the root. \( 1/\sqrt{2} = 0.7071 \).

Step 3 — magnitude average. Over a half cycle the mean of \( \sin \) is \( 2/\pi = 0.6366 \).

Answer. RMS 0.707 of peak; magnitude average 0.637 of peak. The two differ by about 11%, and they answer different questions: RMS is the value that delivers the same heating or the same energy, while the magnitude average is what a simple rectifying instrument responds to. Instruments calibrated on one assumption and used on a distorted waveform are a routine source of disagreement between two meters on the same circuit.

Lesson 10First-order differential equations

A differential equation states a rule about how fast something changes, and solving it recovers the quantity itself. Most first-order models in plant engineering are separable, meaning the two variables can be pushed to opposite sides and integrated independently. This lesson solves that class and fixes the arbitrary constant from an initial condition.

Key points

  • A differential equation relates a quantity to its own rate of change.
  • Separable means the equation can be rearranged with each variable entirely on its own side before integrating.
  • Integrating both sides introduces one arbitrary constant, which the initial condition determines.
  • A general solution is a family of curves; a particular solution is the one that passes through the known starting point.
  • Proportional-rate models integrate to exponentials, which closes the loop with the growth-and-decay lesson.
  • Newton's law of cooling is the standard example, and its solution approaches ambient without ever reaching it.
  • Always verify by substituting the solution back into the original equation.

Separating the variables

A differential equation is a statement about a rate. It does not give the quantity directly; it gives a rule the quantity's rate must obey, and solving means finding the quantity whose rate obeys it. That is why the previous lessons on integration are the prerequisite: solving is integrating, once the equation is arranged so that integration is possible.

An equation is separable when it can be rearranged so each variable sits entirely on its own side, each with its own differential. Once separated, both sides are integrated independently — and this is legitimate precisely because each side now involves only one variable.

Integrating both sides produces one arbitrary constant. That is not untidiness; it is the honest statement that the rate rule alone cannot know where the quantity started. A cooling law describes every component cooling in that environment, whatever its starting temperature, and the family of curves it describes is the general solution. Supplying an initial condition selects one curve from the family and gives the particular solution.

The working method. One: get all of one variable with its differential on one side. Two: integrate both sides, writing the constant once. Three: apply the initial condition to evaluate it. Four: rearrange into the quantity you actually want. Five: substitute back into the original equation to verify.

MTH 101 · Lesson 10 · First-order differential equations · sheet 2 of 2

Newton's law of cooling, and why it never finishes

The standard first-order model in plant work says a body's rate of temperature change is proportional to how far it is from its surroundings: fast when the gap is large, slow when the gap is small. Written out, the rate of change of temperature equals minus a constant times the difference between the body and ambient.

The minus sign is doing real work. It says the temperature moves towards ambient from whichever side it starts — a hot body cools and a cold body warms, and the same equation covers both without modification. The constant \( k \), read ‘k’, sets the pace and is the reciprocal of the time constant met earlier, so a large \( k \) is a fast process.

Separating and integrating turns the difference from ambient into an exponential decay, which closes the loop with the growth-and-decay lesson: the exponential curve met there as a description is produced here as a consequence of the rate rule. Both describe the same physics, and seeing them connect is the point of solving rather than quoting.

Two features are worth reading off the solution. It approaches ambient asymptotically and never arrives, so ‘time to reach ambient’ is not a well-posed question — ‘time to reach within two degrees’ is. And the constant appears only in the combination \( kt \), so doubling the rate constant is indistinguishable from waiting twice as long. That is why a single cooling curve can identify the constant, and why two curves at different ambients cannot distinguish mechanisms that share it.

MTH 101 · Lesson 10 · First-order differential equations · worked example 1

Solving a cooling law from a rate rule

Problem. A component obeys \( dT/dt = -k(T - T_a) \) with ambient \( T_a = 20\ ^\circ\mathrm{C} \), rate constant \( k = 1/12\ \mathrm{min^{-1}} \), and an initial temperature of \( 80\ ^\circ\mathrm{C} \). Solve for \( T(t) \) and find the temperature at 30 minutes.

Approach. The equation is separable once the temperature difference is treated as the working variable. Integrate, then use the initial condition to fix the constant. The classic slip is to lose the minus sign during separation, which produces a component that heats up towards infinity — physically absurd, and therefore easy to catch if you glance at the sign of the exponent before continuing.

Solution.

Step 1 — separate. \( \dfrac{dT}{T - 20} = -k\,dt \).

Step 2 — integrate both sides. \( \ln(T - 20) = -kt + C \).

Step 3 — apply the initial condition. At \( t = 0 \), \( T = 80 \), so \( \ln 60 = C \).

Step 4 — rearrange. \( T = 20 + 60e^{-t/12} \).

Step 5 — evaluate at 30 min. \( 20 + 60e^{-2.5} = 24.93 \).

Answer. \( T(t) = 20 + 60e^{-t/12} \), giving 24.93°C at 30 minutes. This is exactly the curve differentiated two lessons ago — there it was given, here it is derived from the rate rule that generates it.

MTH 101 · Lesson 10 · First-order differential equations · worked example 2

Inverting the solution to find a time

Problem. For the same component, how long until it reaches \( 35\ ^\circ\mathrm{C} \)? And why is ‘how long until it reaches 20°C’ not a fair question?

Approach. The solution gives temperature from time; this asks the reverse, so the exponential must be undone with a logarithm — the same manoeuvre used for half-lives. Work with the temperature difference from ambient rather than the temperature itself, because that is the quantity that actually decays exponentially. Subtracting ambient at the wrong moment is the usual error and gives a badly wrong time.

Solution.

Step 1 — work in the difference. \( 35 - 20 = 15 \) against an initial difference of 60.

Step 2 — set up. \( 15 = 60e^{-t/12} \), so \( e^{-t/12} = 0.25 \).

Step 3 — take logarithms. \( -t/12 = \ln 0.25 = -1.386 \).

Step 4 — solve. \( t = 12 \times 1.386 = 16.64\ \mathrm{min} \).

Answer. 16.64 minutes. Reaching exactly 20°C, however, requires the difference to be zero, and no finite time makes an exponential zero — the logarithm of zero is undefined. The question has to be posed as a tolerance: within one degree, within two. That is not pedantry, it is how a cooling-time acceptance criterion has to be written to be testable.

Lesson 11Taylor series and approximation

A Taylor series replaces a complicated function near a point with a polynomial that matches its value and its derivatives there. That is the justification for every small-angle approximation, every linearised model and every controller tuned on a local slope. This lesson builds the series and, more importantly, bounds the error you accept by truncating it.

Key points

  • A Taylor polynomial matches the function's value and first several derivatives at one chosen point.
  • Each extra term buys accuracy near that point and buys nothing far from it.
  • Truncating leaves a remainder; an approximation without an error estimate is an assertion, not a result.
  • The first-order truncation is linearisation — the tangent line — and is the basis of most control models.
  • The small-angle approximation is the first-order Taylor series of the sine, and its error grows as the cube of the angle.
  • Convergence is local: a series about one point may be useless at another.
  • Check an approximation by computing the first neglected term, which usually bounds the error well.

Matching derivatives at a point

The idea is direct. To approximate a function near some expansion point \( a \), build a polynomial that agrees with it there — not merely in value, but in slope, in curvature, and in as many further derivatives as you care to match. Each condition fixes one coefficient, and the coefficients come out as the derivatives divided by factorials.

The factorial is not decoration. Differentiating a power term repeatedly generates exactly a factorial, so dividing by \( n! \) is what makes the nth derivative of the polynomial equal the nth derivative of the function rather than some multiple of it.

An expansion about zero is called a Maclaurin series and is the common case, because most engineering approximations are about a rest state, a zero deflection or a nominal operating point.

Two properties govern how the result behaves. Accuracy is local: the approximation is excellent near \( a \) and degrades as you move away, so a series built about one operating point may be worthless at another. And adding terms improves accuracy near \( a \) without necessarily helping far from it — more terms is not a substitute for expanding about the right point.

MTH 101 · Lesson 11 · Taylor series and approximation · sheet 2 of 2

The error you accept, and the linearisation you rely on

Every practical use of a series is a truncation: keep a few terms, discard the rest, and accept a remainder. An approximation quoted without an error estimate is an assertion rather than a result, and the usable estimate is simple — compute the first term you discarded. For the well-behaved series met in engineering it characterises the error closely.

The most important truncation is the shortest. Keeping only the constant and the linear term gives linearisation, \( f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) \), which is precisely the tangent line. Nearly every control model, sensitivity calculation and small-signal analysis in engineering is a linearisation, and the reason they work is that the discarded terms carry factors of the deviation squared — negligible while the deviation stays small, and not negligible otherwise.

The small-angle approximation is the standard case. Truncating the sine's series after the first term gives sine of an angle approximately equal to the angle itself, in radians. The first discarded term is the cube over six, so the error grows as the cube of the angle: tolerable at a tenth of a radian, visible at half a radian, and unacceptable beyond.

This is the honest content of every ‘for small oscillations’ in a vibration analysis. The phrase is not a formality — it is a statement that a cubic error term has been discarded, and it carries an obligation to check that the amplitude actually stayed small.

MTH 101 · Lesson 11 · Taylor series and approximation · worked example 1

Building a series and watching the error fall

Problem. Use the Maclaurin series of \( e^{x} \) to approximate \( e^{0.1} \) to first, second and third order, and compare each with the exact value 1.1051709.

Approach. The exponential is its own derivative, so every derivative at zero equals 1 and the coefficients are simply the reciprocal factorials. Compute each partial sum and the error it leaves, then check whether the first discarded term predicts that error — which is the practical reason for doing the exercise at all.

Solution.

Step 1 — first order. \( 1 + 0.1 = 1.1 \); error 0.005171.

Step 2 — second order. \( 1 + 0.1 + 0.005 = 1.105 \); error 0.000171.

Step 3 — third order. \( +\,0.001^3/6 \) gives 1.1051667; error 0.0000043.

Step 4 — test the rule. The first term discarded at second order is \( 0.1^3/6 = 0.000167 \), against an actual error of 0.000171.

Answer. Errors of 5.2×10⁻³, 1.7×10⁻⁴ and 4.3×10⁻⁶. Each order gains roughly two decimal places here, and the first neglected term predicts the error to within 3% — which is what makes it a usable field estimate.

MTH 101 · Lesson 11 · Taylor series and approximation · worked example 2

How small is a small angle

Problem. The small-angle approximation replaces the sine of an angle by the angle in radians. Quantify its error at 0.1 rad and at 0.5 rad, and state the practical limit.

Approach. Compare the approximation against the true sine at both angles, and express the discrepancy as a percentage of the true value — percentages, not absolute differences, are what an engineering tolerance is written in. Expect the error to scale roughly with the cube of the angle, since that is the first discarded term, so a fivefold increase in angle should cost far more than fivefold in error.

Solution.

Step 1 — at 0.1 rad. True sine 0.0998334 against an approximation of 0.1: absolute error 0.000167, or 0.17%.

Step 2 — at 0.5 rad. True sine 0.4794255 against 0.5: absolute error 0.020574, or 4.29%.

Step 3 — check the scaling. The angle grew fivefold and the absolute error grew about 123-fold, close to the 125 that a cubic law predicts.

Answer. 0.17% at 0.1 rad and 4.29% at 0.5 rad. Below about 0.1 rad, roughly 6 degrees, the approximation is good to a fifth of a percent and safe for most work; by 0.5 rad it is costing over 4% and should be stated explicitly if used at all. The cubic scaling is what makes the degradation so sudden.
