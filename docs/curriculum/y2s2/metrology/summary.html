MET 204 · Lesson 01 · Measurement fundamentals

Lesson 01Measurement fundamentals

Key points

  • Resolution is what the display can show, precision is agreement among repeats, bias is a systematic offset, and accuracy needs small bias and small scatter.
  • The worked micrometer reads 25.0052 mm on a 25.0000 mm block with \( s=1.03\ \mu\mathrm{m} \): precise and inaccurate, the combination that survives careless checking.
  • \( u(\bar{x})=s/\sqrt{n} \): ten readings tighten the mean to 0.33 µm and leave the 5.2 µm bias exactly where it was.
  • Judge a check against its reference: a 5.2 µm bias against a block certified to 0.2 µm is 26:1, so the error is the instrument's.
  • Traceability is an unbroken chain of calibrations to a national standard, each with a stated uncertainty; uncertainty accumulates downward and never shrinks.
  • A certificate states measurand, reference standard, result with uncertainty and coverage factor, and conditions. A result without an uncertainty is not a result.
  • Dimensional metrology is defined at 20°C, and \( \Delta L=L\alpha\Delta T \) is not optional arithmetic.
  • A 200 mm steel part at 25°C is 11.5 µm long — a quarter of an IT7 band; in aluminium, 23.1 µm.
  • Measuring aluminium with a steel gauge leaves the differential 11.6 µm, which no instrument quality removes.
  • The 4:1 rule compares tolerance with uncertainty; a known bias belongs corrected, not absorbed into the budget.

Assumed knowledge

  • Mean and standard deviation (Engineering Statistics L2) — the two numbers that separate scatter from offset.
  • The standard error of a mean (Engineering Statistics L5) — \( s/\sqrt{n} \), and what it does and does not improve.
  • Thermal expansion (Materials Science I L6) — \( \Delta L=L\alpha\Delta T \), applied here at micrometre scale.
  • Tolerances and IT grades (Engineering Drawing & CAD L6) — the bands these errors are being spent against.
  • SI units (Engineering Mathematics I L1) — the definition the traceability chain terminates at.

MET 204 · Lesson 02 · Dimensional instruments

Lesson 02Dimensional instruments

Key points

  • Calipers are wide-range and least accurate, micrometers trade range for accuracy, dial indicators measure differences only, gauge blocks are embodied length, and a comparator is an indicator plus a stand plus a block.
  • Abbe's principle: the measuring line should be collinear with the scale. Any offset \( h \) turns an angular error into \( \varepsilon = h\tan\theta \).
  • Abbe error is first order in tilt; cosine error \( L(1-\cos\theta) \), all that remains when \( h=0 \), is second order.
  • A caliper with a 40 mm offset tilted 0.1° gives 69.8 µm; a micrometer at the same tilt gives 0.038 µm — a factor of 1833, bought by geometry rather than workmanship.
  • Double the tilt and the caliper error doubles while the micrometer error quadruples from a number that was already negligible.
  • Gauge blocks wring so a stack acts as one length; build from the finest decimal first, and use as few blocks as possible — 41.275 mm as 1.005 + 1.27 + 8.0 + 31.0.
  • Every wrung interface adds uncertainty and a contamination risk, so four blocks beat seven for the same length.
  • A ratchet fixes the measuring force so contact deformation is repeatable and calibration can absorb it; feeling the fit converts a bias into scatter.
  • Parallax, anvil wear, dirt and hand heat are all systematic and none is fixed by buying a better instrument — a specification is an upper bound on performance, not a description of it.
  • An error that depends on technique passes a repeatability check and fails between operators, which is what gauge R&R exists to expose.

Assumed knowledge

  • Bias and precision (Lesson 1) — the distinction that decides which errors of use matter and how they show up.
  • Thermal error (Lesson 1) — \( \Delta L=L\alpha\Delta T \), applied here to hand heat and to steel gauge blocks.
  • Small-angle behaviour (Engineering Mathematics I L11) — why \( \tan\theta\approx\theta \) is first order and \( 1-\cos\theta \) is second.

  • Elastic contact (Strength of Materials L1) — why a measuring force must be controlled rather than merely light.
