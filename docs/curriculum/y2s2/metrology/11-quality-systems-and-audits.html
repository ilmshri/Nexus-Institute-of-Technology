Quality systems and audits

ISO 9001 logic: say it, do it, prove it. Nonconformance, CAPA, and audit trails. Where quality systems touch maintenance records — more often than either side admits.

§1 · Say it, do it, prove it

A quality management system looks, from outside, like a great deal of paper. Reduced to its logic it is three obligations, and every clause of a standard such as ISO 9001 exists to serve one of them: say what you intend to do, do it, and prove that you did. Document control, competence, management review and internal audit are the machinery; those three are the content.

Map that onto the calibration programme this course has been building and it stops being abstract. Say is a written procedure naming which instruments are controlled, the standard each is calibrated against, the interval, the acceptance criterion, who is competent to do it, and — the clause most often missing — what happens when an instrument is found out of tolerance. Do is instruments uniquely identified and labelled with a due date, calibrated by the stated method against traceable standards, with the traceability chain of Lesson 1 unbroken. Prove is the retained evidence: certificates carrying uncertainties, a record of every event, the uncertainty budgets of Lesson 3, the out-of-tolerance investigations, and the recall decisions that followed them.

The three failure modes are worth naming because each is common and each looks different in an audit. Say without do is paperwork — a procedure nobody follows, which is worse than no procedure because it is a documented commitment being breached. Do without say is craft: it may be excellent, it is not repeatable when the person leaves, and it cannot be defended to a customer. Do without prove is the one that catches good shops, because the work really was done and there is no evidence of it. In an audit, unprovable and undone are the same finding.

§2 · Nonconformance, and the difference CAPA depends on

A nonconformance is any failure to meet a requirement — a part outside its tolerance, a procedure not followed, an instrument past its due date. The record of one has a fixed shape: what was found, how much, where and by whom, what was done to contain it, and how it was disposed of. Disposition is a decision among use-as-is, rework, repair, regrade and scrap, and it is an engineering judgement that belongs to someone with the authority to make it, not to whoever found the problem.

Key distinction — the three actions

Correction fixes the affected product. Corrective action removes the cause so it does not recur. Preventive action acts on a cause that has not produced a failure yet.

Scrapping the batch is a correction. Re-cutting the fixture location that let the batch happen is a corrective action. They are routinely confused, and a system that only ever performs corrections will keep meeting the same nonconformance and closing it every time.

Both words are overloaded, and this course has already used them in other senses. A correction in Lesson 3 was the value added to a reading to remove a known bias; a disposition in Lesson 5 was how a gauge tolerance is placed inside a work tolerance. Neither has anything to do with the meanings here, and reading a nonconformance record with the metrology sense in mind is a genuine source of confusion.

Two disciplines make the difference real. The first is containment: the question is never only what is wrong with this part but what else is affected — which other batches, which other characteristics, what is already at the customer. The second is the effectiveness check: after a corrective action, evidence that the recurrence rate actually fell. A corrective action closed without one is a promise rather than an action, and it is the single most common weakness a third-party auditor finds.

The bridge to the rest of this course is that an instrument found out of tolerance at calibration is a nonconformance, and its containment scope is set by something already chosen: the calibration interval. Everything measured since the last good calibration is suspect, because nothing distinguishes readings taken before the drift from readings taken after it.

§3 · What a calibration interval is really buying

That last observation turns the interval from an administrative setting into an arithmetic one.

Key relations — exposure and the optimum interval

\[ N_{\text{suspect}}\approx r\,\frac{T}{2},\qquad K(T)=\frac{C_c}{T}+\frac{r\lambda T}{2}C_p,\qquad T^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{2C_c}{r\lambda C_p}} \]

\( r \) is the production rate, \( T \) the interval, \( \lambda \) the rate at which instruments drift out of tolerance, \( C_c \) the cost of one calibration and \( C_p \) the cost of dealing with one suspect part. If the drift can have occurred at any time in the interval, the expected exposure is half of it.

Take a line measuring 400 parts a day over 250 working days — 100,000 a year. On a twelve-month interval, an instrument found out of tolerance implicates on average six months of production: 50,000 parts, every one of which has to be re-inspected, justified, or accepted on an engineering rationale. On a three-month interval the same finding implicates 12,500. Nothing about the instrument changed; the exposure was set by a decision made a year earlier.

Neither extreme is right, because calibrating costs money too. Differentiating \( K(T) \) and setting the derivative to zero — the standard minimisation from Engineering Mathematics I — gives \( T^{*} \). With \( C_c=120 \), \( r=100{,}000 \), \( \lambda=0.15 \) per year and \( C_p=0.05 \), the optimum is 0.566 years, or 6.8 months, at an annual cost of 424. At that interval the two cost terms are each 212: at the optimum the cost of calibrating equals the cost of the exposure, which is a quick way to test an existing interval without redoing the calculus.

The shape of the curve matters as much as its minimum. Twelve months costs 495, 17% more; three months costs 574, 35% more. Over-calibrating is the more expensive error here, which is not the intuition most shops have, and the asymmetry comes from the \( C_c/T \) term rising without bound as the interval shrinks. Two qualifications keep this honest: \( \lambda \) has to come from the instrument's own drift history rather than from a table, and an instrument whose out-of-tolerance would implicate a safety characteristic is not on this curve at all, because \( C_p \) is not a re-inspection cost in that case.

§4 · Audits, maintenance records, and what "no findings" is worth

Audits come in three flavours — internal, done to yourself; second-party, done by a customer; third-party, done by a certification body — and all three work the same way. The auditor picks an endpoint and traces it backwards until the chain either closes or does not: a certificate on a wall back to the standard it was measured against, a part in a box back to the inspection record and the instrument that produced it, a control chart back to the gauge study that justified it.

Maintenance records enter that chain in three places, and shops are regularly surprised by all three. Equipment that produces or verifies product is subject to control, so a machine whose scheduled maintenance lapsed is direct evidence against the claim that the process was in control. Calibration is maintenance of the measuring system, so the two record sets have to agree about the same instrument's history — a repair in one that does not appear in the other is a finding on both. And after a nonconformance the maintenance log is where a root cause is confirmed or refuted: a spindle bearing replaced the same week the ranges on a chart widened is the strongest evidence anyone will get. The finding that catches good shops is a capability study performed before a major overhaul and never repeated after it: the evidence describes a machine that no longer exists.

Finally, what an audit result actually means, because an audit is a sample and everything this course knows about samples applies to it.

Key relations — the audit as a sample

\[ P(\text{find at least one})=1-(1-p)^{n},\qquad p_{95\%}\le 1-0.05^{1/n}\approx\frac{3}{n} \]

The first sizes an audit: to be 95% confident of finding a nonconformity present in 5% of records, \( n=\ln0.05/\ln0.95=58.4 \), so 59 records. The second reads a clean one: finding nothing in \( n \) records bounds the nonconformity rate at roughly \( 3/n \), not at zero.

Thirty records with no findings supports the statement "the nonconformity rate is below about 9.5%" and supports nothing stronger. Twenty records supports 13.9%. Neither is "the system conforms", and an internal audit programme that samples twenty records a quarter and reports no findings has demonstrated very little at considerable expense. The remedy is the same one Lesson 9 reached: sample size buys discrimination, so audit deeply in the areas where a failure would be expensive and accept that the shallow areas are being spot-checked rather than verified.

Which is the honest closing position for the whole course. A measurement is a claim about a part; a quality system is what makes that claim mean something six months later, to someone who was not there, about a part they cannot re-measure. The instrument, the uncertainty budget, the chart, the capability study and the audit trail are one apparatus, and the weakest of them sets what the rest is worth.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Price a calibration interval

Problem. A line measures 100,000 parts a year with one instrument. A calibration event costs 120, instruments drift out of tolerance at \( \lambda=0.15 \) per year, and each part implicated by an out-of-tolerance finding costs 0.05 to re-inspect. Write the annual cost as a function of the interval \( T \) in years, find the optimum interval in years, months and weeks, and compare the annual cost there with the cost at twelve months and at three months.

Solution. (1) Calibrations occur \( 1/T \) times a year, costing \( C_c/T \). If the drift can start at any time in the interval, the expected exposure is \( T/2 \), so the expected parts implicated per year are \( r\lambda T/2 \), each costing \( C_p \). Hence \( K(T)=\dfrac{C_c}{T}+\dfrac{r\lambda T}{2}C_p \).

(2) \( \dfrac{dK}{dT}=-\dfrac{C_c}{T^2}+\dfrac{r\lambda C_p}{2}=0 \), so \( T^{*}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2C_c}{r\lambda C_p}} \). The second derivative \( 2C_c/T^3 \) is positive, so it is a minimum.

(3) \( T^{*}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2(120)}{100000(0.15)(0.05)}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{240}{750}}=\sqrt{0.32} =0.566\ \mathrm{yr} \), that is 6.79 months or 29.4 weeks.

(4) \( K(T^{*})=120/0.566+100000(0.15)(0.566)(0.05)/2=212.13+212.13=424.26 \).

(5) \( K(1)=120+375=495 \), which is \( 495/424.26-1=16.7\% \) more.

(6) \( K(0.25)=480+93.75=573.75 \), which is 35.2% more.

Answer: \( T^{*}=0.566 \) yr = 6.79 months = 29.4 weeks, at an annual cost of 424.26; twelve months costs 495 (17% more) and three months 574 (35% more). Sanity check: at the optimum the two cost terms are each 212.13 — exactly equal, which is a general property of this cost shape and the fastest way to audit an existing interval without redoing the calculus. Note the asymmetry: over-calibrating costs more than under-calibrating here, because \( C_c/T \) grows without bound as \( T\to0 \) while the exposure term only grows linearly. Note finally what the model assumes — \( \lambda \) from the instrument's own drift history, and \( C_p \) a re-inspection cost. If the characteristic is safety-related, \( C_p \) is not a re-inspection cost and the instrument does not belong on this curve.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

An out-of-tolerance finding, and what it implicates

Problem. A bore gauge on a line producing 400 parts a day, 250 days a year, is found at its annual calibration to read 0.0001 in high. Express the error in micrometres. Assuming the drift could have begun at any time since the last good calibration, find the expected number of parts implicated on a twelve-month interval, a 6.79-month interval and a three-month interval. State what the quality system must record, and name the three actions the finding calls for.

Solution. (1) \( 0.0001\ \mathrm{in}\times25.4\ \mathrm{mm/in}=0.00254\ \mathrm{mm}=2.54\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(2) The line makes \( 400\times250=100{,}000 \) parts a year. Nothing distinguishes readings taken before the drift from readings taken after it, so everything since the last good calibration is suspect, and the expected exposure is half the interval.

(3) Twelve months: \( 100{,}000\times\tfrac{1}{2}=50{,}000 \) parts.

(4) 6.79 months: \( 100{,}000\times\tfrac{0.566}{2}=28{,}300 \) parts.

(5) Three months: \( 100{,}000\times\tfrac{0.25}{2}=12{,}500 \) parts.

(6) The record must carry the finding and its magnitude, the containment scope and how it was determined, the disposition of the affected population, the root cause, and an effectiveness check.

(7) The three actions: correction — adjust or replace the gauge and deal with the implicated parts; corrective action — remove whatever let the drift go undetected, which here is the interval itself or the absence of an intermediate check; preventive action — review other instruments on the same duty cycle before they do the same thing.

Answer: 2.54 µm; 50,000 parts implicated at twelve months, 28,300 at 6.79 and 12,500 at three; the containment scope is set by the interval, and the finding calls for a correction, a corrective action and a preventive action. Sanity check: the instrument's behaviour was identical in all three cases — only a decision made a year earlier changed the size of the problem, which is exactly why the interval is an engineering choice rather than an administrative one. Note the value of an intermediate check: verifying the gauge against a reference block weekly would cap the exposure at a week's production regardless of the calibration interval, which is usually far cheaper than shortening the interval itself.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

What a clean audit is worth

Problem. An internal audit samples records at random from a large population in which a fraction \( p \) are nonconforming. Find the sample size needed for a 95% chance of finding at least one nonconformity when \( p=0.05 \). Then find the probability of finding at least one from a sample of 30, and the 95% upper bound on \( p \) implied by finding none in 20 and in 30 records. Compare the exact bound with the "rule of three".

Solution. (1) The chance of missing every nonconformity is \( (1-p)^n \), so \( P(\text{at least one})=1-(1-p)^n \). Setting this to 0.95 gives \( (0.95)^n=0.05 \), so \( n=\ln0.05/\ln0.95=58.4 \) → 59 records.

(2) From 30 records: \( 1-0.95^{30}=1-0.2146=0.785 \). A one-in-five chance of missing a nonconformity running at 5%.

(3) Finding none in \( n \) records is consistent with any \( p \) for which \( (1-p)^n\ge0.05 \), so the 95% upper bound is \( p\le1-0.05^{1/n} \).

(4) \( n=20 \): \( 1-0.05^{1/20}=1-0.8609=13.9\% \). \( n=30 \): \( 1-0.05^{1/30}=1-0.9050=9.50\% \).

(5) The rule of three gives \( 3/20=15\% \) and \( 3/30=10\% \) — within a point of the exact values, and it needs no calculator.

Answer: 59 records for 95% confidence at \( p=0.05 \); 0.785 from a sample of 30; a clean audit of 20 bounds \( p \) at 13.9% and of 30 at 9.5%, against 15% and 10% from the rule of three. Sanity check: "no findings" from twenty records supports the claim that nonconformity is below about 14%, which is a much weaker statement than the phrase suggests and is the honest way to report it. The arithmetic is the same as Lesson 9's: a sample bounds a rate and never demonstrates zero. The practical consequence is to audit deeply where a failure would be expensive and to say plainly that the rest was spot-checked rather than verified.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

A shop performs a calibration correctly but retains no record of it. In an audit this is

Prove is one of the three obligations, not an afterthought to the other two. An auditor can only assess evidence, so unprovable and undone produce the same finding. This is the failure mode that catches competent shops, because the work genuinely was done and there is nothing to show for it.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

A batch is scrapped after a fixture was found mislocated. Scrapping the batch is

It fixes the affected product, which is a correction. The corrective action is whatever stops the fixture being mislocated again — a location feature, a check in the setup sheet. A system that only performs corrections meets the same nonconformance repeatedly and closes it every time.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A corrective action is closed without an effectiveness check. The consequence is that

The check is what converts an intended fix into a demonstrated one, and its absence is among the commonest third-party findings. Identifying a plausible root cause is not the same as showing the action removed it — the failure may recur for a reason nobody listed.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

An instrument is found out of tolerance at its annual calibration. The population of suspect parts is

Nothing in the readings distinguishes those taken before the drift from those taken after it, so the containment scope runs back to the last calibration that passed. On a line making 100,000 parts a year and an annual interval, that is 50,000 parts on average — a number fixed by a decision made a year earlier, not by the instrument.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

With \( K(T)=C_c/T+r\lambda TC_p/2 \), at the optimum interval

Setting \( dK/dT=0 \) gives \( C_c/T^2=r\lambda C_p/2 \), and multiplying by \( T \) shows \( C_c/T=r\lambda TC_p/2 \). Here each is 212.13 against a total of 424.26. It is the quickest audit of an existing interval: if one term dominates, the interval is wrong in the direction of the larger term.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

An internal audit samples 30 records and finds no nonconformities. The strongest honest conclusion is

\( 1-0.05^{1/30}=9.5\% \), close to the rule of three's \( 3/30=10\% \). A sample bounds a rate and never demonstrates zero — the same arithmetic as an acceptance sampling plan. Reporting "no findings" without the bound overstates what was demonstrated, and 30 records is not nothing, it is simply a weak bound.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

To be 95% confident of finding a nonconformity present in 5% of records, an audit must sample about

\( (0.95)^n=0.05 \) gives \( n=\ln0.05/\ln0.95=58.4 \), so 59. Most internal audits sample far fewer, which is a legitimate choice provided the report says what was actually demonstrated. Sample size buys discrimination in an audit exactly as it does in an acceptance plan.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A machine's capability study was performed before a major overhaul and never repeated. In an audit this is a finding because

An overhaul changes the equipment the evidence was about, so the study no longer supports any claim regarding the current machine. This is where maintenance records enter the audit trail: they are what reveals that the evidence and the equipment have diverged, and they are also where a root cause is confirmed after a nonconformance.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the finding that arrived a year late. A bore gauge on a line running 400 parts a day was calibrated annually. At one calibration it was found reading 2.54 µm high. The instrument was adjusted, the certificate was filed, and production continued. Eleven months later a customer returned a batch of undersized bores, and the investigation could not establish when the drift had begun.

The system had performed the correction and neither of the other two actions. The containment question — what else is affected — was never asked, and its answer was fixed by the interval: with 100,000 parts a year and nothing to distinguish readings before the drift from readings after it, the expected suspect population was 50,000 parts. Nor was there a corrective action, because the cause of the late detection was the interval itself and the absence of any intermediate check, and adjusting the gauge addressed neither. The certificate on file recorded that the instrument had been adjusted and proved nothing about the parts, which is the "prove" obligation failing while looking as though it had been met.

Two practices follow. Treat an out-of-tolerance finding as a nonconformance with a containment scope, not as a maintenance event: the first question is which parts, and the answer is already determined by the interval, so it can be worked out before the finding ever occurs. And where the exposure is large, buy detection rather than frequency — a weekly check against a reference block caps the suspect population at a week's production for a fraction of the cost of shortening the calibration interval, and it turns an unanswerable question into a bounded one.
