Springs

Helical spring stress and rate derived; solid height and buckling checks. Fatigue of springs under fluctuating load. Valve and relief springs as working examples.

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • What stress does a helical spring's wire actually carry?
  • What does solid height check against?

01 · A spring is a torsion bar wound up

Push down on a helical compression spring and the wire does not bend in any useful sense — it twists. Cut the wire anywhere and the internal action is almost entirely a torque \( T=FD/2 \), the axial force acting at the mean coil radius. That single observation gives the whole component, because the torsion of a round bar is already known from Strength of Materials.

Two corrections separate the real spring from that idealisation. The applied force also produces a direct transverse shear across the wire, and because the coil is curved, the material on the inside of the coil is shorter and carries more of the twist than the outside. Both effects raise stress on the inner fibre, and both are collected into a single multiplier.

Key relation — spring stress and the Wahl factor

\[ \tau = K_W\frac{8FD}{\pi d^3}, \qquad K_W = \frac{4C-1}{4C-4}+\frac{0.615}{C}, \qquad C=\frac{D}{d} \]

\( C \) is the spring index. At \( C=7.5 \), \( K_W = 1.197 \) — the inner fibre carries 20% more stress than the plain torsion formula predicts, and it is where every spring fails.

The spring index is worth reading as a manufacturability number as well as a stress one. Below about 4 the coil is so tightly wound that \( K_W \) climbs steeply and the wire is hard to form; above about 12 the spring is floppy and tangles in handling. Designs live between, and \( C \) between 6 and 9 is comfortable.

02 · Rate, and how violently it responds to wire size

Deflection accumulates twist over the whole developed length of the wire, which gives the rate directly.

Key relation — spring rate

\[ k=\frac{Gd^4}{8D^3N_a} \]

\( N_a \) is the number of active coils — the total less the dead coils at each end. Note the exponents: rate goes as the fourth power of wire diameter and the inverse cube of coil diameter.

Those exponents dominate spring design in practice. Changing the wire from 4.0 to 3.5 mm — a 12.5% reduction that no drawing reader would call significant — takes the rate from 9.40 to 5.51 N/mm, a loss of 41%. Wire diameter is not a tolerance-band decision; it is the design. It is also why a spring that has been substituted "like for like" from a bin of similar-looking parts is a real hazard on a machine whose behaviour depends on a specific rate.

03 · The two checks that are not stress

A spring can pass its stress calculation and still be unusable, in two distinct ways.

Solid height. Compress a spring far enough and the coils touch; it stops being a spring and becomes a steel tube. Solid length is \( L_s = d\,N_t \) with \( N_t \) the total coil count. Good practice is that a spring must survive being closed solid without yielding, because sooner or later something will close it — an overtravel, a misassembly, a maintenance hand. The worked spring reaches 564 MPa at solid against an 814 MPa allowable, a margin of 1.44: it takes the abuse and recovers.

Buckling. A compression spring is a slender column and will buckle sideways if it is too long for its diameter. The criterion depends on how the ends are held: a spring squared and ground at both ends against flat parallel surfaces is stable while \( L_0 < 2.63D/\alpha \) with \( \alpha=0.5 \), and one end free to pivot takes \( \alpha \) to 0.707 and the allowance down with it. The worked spring at \( L_0=90 \) against a limit of 158 mm has room; a long, slender relief-valve spring frequently does not, which is why such springs so often run inside a guide bore or over a rod.

04 · Springs under fluctuating load

A valve spring is a fatigue component by definition, and springs are unusual in one respect: they are almost never fully reversed. A compression spring is installed with preload and cycles between two positive forces, so the mean stress is large and the amplitude comparatively small — which is exactly the situation Lesson 4's constant-life diagram was built for, transposed into shear.

Compute \( \tau_a \) and \( \tau_m \) from the force amplitude and mean, then apply Goodman with torsional strengths: \( \tau_a/S_{se} + \tau_m/S_{su} = 1/n \). For the worked spring cycling between 100 and 250 N, that gives \( \tau_a=107 \) and \( \tau_m=250\ \mathrm{MPa} \), and a margin of 1.81 — acceptable but not generous, which is honest for a spring working that hard.

One design lever is specific to springs. Shot peening the wire leaves a compressive residual stress in exactly the inner-fibre surface where cracks start, and it routinely raises the torsional endurance limit by 20% or more. For a highly cycled spring it is usually the cheapest life available — the same reasoning as grinding a shaft fillet in Lesson 3, applied to a surface that is otherwise impossible to improve.

Closing. Torsion in a wound bar, with the Wahl factor putting the peak on the inside. Rate goes as \( d^4 \), so wire size is the design. Then check solid height and buckling, which stress never covers, and peen anything that cycles.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Torsion of a round bar (Strength of Materials L4) — \( \tau=16T/\pi d^3 \), the formula the spring equation is built on.
  • Direct shear (Strength of Materials L1) — the second of the two effects the Wahl factor collects.
  • Spring rate and stiffness (Physics I) — \( F=k\delta \), used here as a design target rather than a given.
  • Column buckling (Strength of Materials L10) — the end-condition idea reused for a spring's stability limit.
  • Goodman criterion (Lesson 4) — applied in shear, with torsional endurance and ultimate strengths.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Rate and stress at the working point

Problem. A helical compression spring has wire diameter \( d=4\ \mathrm{mm} \), mean coil diameter \( D=30\ \mathrm{mm} \), 10 active coils, and \( G=79.3\ \mathrm{GPa} \). It works at 20 mm deflection. The allowable shear stress is \( S_{sy}=814\ \mathrm{MPa} \). Find: the spring index, the Wahl factor, the rate, the working force, the stress and the factor of safety.

Solution. (1) \( C = 30/4 = 7.5 \). (2) \( K_W = \frac{4(7.5)-1}{4(7.5)-4}+\frac{0.615}{7.5} = \frac{29}{26}+0.082 = 1.197 \). (3) \( k = \frac{(79.3\times10^9)(0.004)^4}{8(0.030)^3(10)} = \frac{20.30}{2.16\times10^{-3}} = 9399\ \mathrm{N/m} = 9.40\ \mathrm{N/mm} \). (4) \( F = k\delta = 9.40(20) = 188\ \mathrm{N} \). (5) \( \tau = 1.197\frac{8(188)(0.030)}{\pi(0.004)^3} = 1.197(224.4) = 268.7\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (6) \( n = 814/268.7 = 3.03 \).

Answer: \( C=7.5 \), \( K_W=1.197 \), \( k=9.40\ \mathrm{N/mm} \), \( F=188\ \mathrm{N} \), \( \tau=269\ \mathrm{MPa} \), \( n=3.03 \). Sanity check: the uncorrected torsion formula alone would have given 224 MPa and reported \( n=3.63 \) — 20% optimistic, and optimistic exactly at the inner fibre where the spring actually breaks. \( C=7.5 \) sits mid-range, so this spring is comfortable to wind as well as to load.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Will it survive being closed solid?

Problem. The same spring is squared and ground, so total coils \( N_t = N_a+2 \), and its free length is 90 mm. Find: the solid length, the deflection and force at solid, the stress there, and the factor of safety against the 814 MPa allowable.

Solution. (1) \( N_t = 10+2 = 12 \), so \( L_s = dN_t = 4(12) = 48\ \mathrm{mm} \). (2) Deflection to solid: \( 90-48 = 42\ \mathrm{mm} \). (3) \( F = 9.40(42) = 394.7\ \mathrm{N} \). (4) Stress scales linearly with force: \( \tau = 268.7\times(394.7/188) = 564.2\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (5) \( n = 814/564.2 = 1.44 \).

Answer: \( L_s=48\ \mathrm{mm} \), 42 mm to solid at 395 N, \( \tau=564\ \mathrm{MPa} \), \( n=1.44 \) — it survives closure. Sanity check: the margin at solid is above 1, which is the design requirement, and the ratio \( 564/269 = 2.1 \) is exactly \( 42/20 \), confirming the linear scaling used in step 4. A spring that failed this check would take a permanent set the first time anything bottomed it out, and would then be a different spring — lower free length, lower installed force — without anyone having replaced a part.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

The spring as a fatigue component

Problem. The same spring cycles between 100 N and 250 N in service. Take the torsional endurance limit \( S_{se}=310\ \mathrm{MPa} \) and the torsional ultimate \( S_{su}=1212\ \mathrm{MPa} \). Find: the shear stress amplitude and mean, and the Goodman factor of safety.

Solution. (1) \( F_a=(250-100)/2 = 75\ \mathrm{N} \), \( F_m=(250+100)/2 = 175\ \mathrm{N} \). (2) Stress is proportional to force, and 188 N gave 268.7 MPa, so \( \tau_a = 268.7(75/188) = 107.2 \) and \( \tau_m = 268.7(175/188) = 250.1\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (3) Goodman: \( 107.2/310 + 250.1/1212 = 0.3458+0.2064 = 0.5522 \). (4) \( n = 1/0.5522 = 1.81 \).

Answer: \( \tau_a=107\ \mathrm{MPa} \), \( \tau_m=250\ \mathrm{MPa} \), \( n=1.81 \). Sanity check: the mean stress is more than twice the amplitude, which is the normal signature of a preloaded compression spring and the reason the mean-stress treatment of Lesson 4 cannot be skipped here. Note where the margin is being spent: the amplitude term contributes 0.35 of the 0.55 total, so shot peening — which acts on \( S_{se} \) — attacks the larger term and is the effective intervention.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

The dominant internal action in the wire of a helical compression spring under axial load is

The axial force acts at the mean coil radius, so a section cut through the wire carries a torque \( T=FD/2 \); the spring is a torsion bar that has been wound into a helix. Direct transverse shear is present but secondary, and it is folded into the Wahl factor along with the curvature effect. Recognising the action as torsion is what lets the whole component be built from a formula already known from Strength of Materials.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

The Wahl factor exceeds 1 because

Two effects act in the same direction on the same side of the wire. The inside of the coil is on a shorter radius, so it takes more than its share of the twist, and the direct transverse shear adds to the torsional shear there rather than opposing it. The result is a peak on the inner fibre — 20% above plain torsion at \( C=7.5 \) — which is precisely where spring fractures initiate. Work hardening affects the material's strength, not the stress distribution.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A spring's wire diameter is increased from 4.0 mm to 4.5 mm with \( D \) and \( N_a \) unchanged. The rate increases by a factor of about

Rate goes as \( d^4 \), so the factor is \( (4.5/4.0)^4 = 1.60 \). The 1.13 answer is the diameter ratio itself and 1.27 its square — both badly understate how sharply a spring responds to wire size. This sensitivity cuts both ways and is the single most important practical fact about springs: it makes wire diameter a powerful design lever and makes an approximate substitution a genuine hazard.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Good practice requires a compression spring to withstand being compressed to its solid length without yielding because

Springs are not designed to work solid, but over a service life something will close one — an overtravelling mechanism, an assembly error, a hand during maintenance. If that yields the wire the spring takes a permanent set, and it returns to service with a shorter free length and a lower installed force while looking entirely normal. That is a silent change to a machine's calibration, which is why the solid check is a standard requirement rather than a refinement.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A compression spring with \( D = 30\ \mathrm{mm} \) is squared and ground on flat parallel surfaces (\( \alpha = 0.5 \)). It is stable against buckling while its free length is less than about

\( L_0 < 2.63D/\alpha = 2.63(30)/0.5 = 158\ \mathrm{mm} \). The 112 mm figure is the answer for \( \alpha = 0.707 \), one end free to pivot — a real and much tighter limit, which is why end conditions must be identified before this check means anything. A spring that fails it does not lose strength; it bows sideways, rubs its bore, and delivers an unpredictable force, which is normally solved by running it over a rod or inside a guide.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Shot peening a spring's wire improves fatigue life primarily by

Peening plastically works the surface and leaves it in residual compression, which must be overcome before an applied tensile or shear cycle can open a crack. Applied to the inner fibre — the one location the Wahl factor identifies as critical and the one that cannot be improved by machining — it routinely buys 20% or more on the torsional endurance limit. It changes no dimension, no modulus, and no index; \( G \) is a material constant that no surface treatment alters.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A relief-valve spring is replaced by one identical in free length and coil diameter but wound from 3.5 mm wire instead of 4.0 mm. The valve will

Rate goes as \( d^4 \), so \( (3.5/4.0)^4 = 0.586 \) — a 41% loss. Installed at the same compression, the spring delivers 41% less seat load and the valve lifts well below its set pressure. Nothing about this is visible on inspection: free length and coil diameter match, and the half-millimetre of wire is easy to overlook. It is the reason a spring whose job is a set pressure should be identified by its measured rate and treated as a calibrated item.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Compared with a fully reversed load, the fatigue state of a typical installed compression spring is

A compression spring is installed with preload and cycles between two positive forces, so it never sees a reversal — the worked spring runs at 250 MPa mean against only 107 MPa amplitude. That is exactly the regime Lesson 4's constant-life diagram addresses, transposed into shear with \( S_{se} \) and \( S_{su} \). Far from being inapplicable, Goodman is the standard tool here, and ignoring the mean stress would badly overstate the margin.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the relief spring that was replaced with a near-enough one. A pressure relief valve begins lifting early and chattering. Records show the spring was replaced during a routine overhaul with a part from stores that measured correctly on free length and coil diameter and looked identical. Its wire measured 3.5 mm against the original 4.0. Because rate goes as \( d^4 \), that half-millimetre took the spring from 9.40 to 5.51 N/mm — 41% softer — so the same installed compression produced 41% less seat load and the valve lifted well below its set pressure. Chattering followed for a related reason: with a softer spring the valve's own dynamics sit closer to the flow's, so the disc oscillates rather than lifting cleanly. Nothing was mismeasured and nothing was worn. The fault was treating wire diameter as a dimension to be matched approximately, when it is the single most sensitive parameter in the component. Two practices follow: a spring is identified by its rate, verified on a tester, not by its appearance; and any spring whose function is a set pressure or a set force should be treated as a calibrated item, not a consumable.
