Welded joint design

Fillet weld sizing under direct and torsional shear. Weld groups treated like bolt groups. Fatigue of welds — why the toe is where cracks start.

Weld Analysis IV: Eccentric Loading (Force + Moment, Weld Groups)MATT HABERLAND

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Why do fillet welds fail at the throat, and what area do you size?
  • Why do weld toes start fatigue cracks?

01 · The throat, and why leg size is not the strength

A fillet weld is drawn and specified by its leg length \( h \), the size measured along either fused face. It does not fail there. The failure plane is the throat, the shortest path across the weld, and for the standard equal-leg fillet that path is \( t = h\cos45° = 0.707h \).

Everything follows from that. A 6 mm fillet has 4.24 mm of resisting thickness, not 6 — specify a weld by its leg and calculate it by its throat, and never confuse the two. It also explains why doubling a fillet's leg doubles its cost in deposited metal roughly fourfold while doubling its strength only twofold: weld volume goes as \( h^2 \), strength as \( h \). Long, small fillets are almost always cheaper than short, large ones for the same capacity.

Key relation — fillet weld capacity

\[ \tau=\frac{F}{0.707\,h\,L}, \qquad \tau\le\tau_{all} \]

Fillet welds are always checked in shear on the throat, whatever direction the load comes from — a transversely loaded fillet is stronger than a longitudinally loaded one, but design practice conservatively treats them alike.

02 · Weld groups behave exactly like bolt groups

An eccentric load on a weld group is the same problem as an eccentric load on a bolt group, and the same method solves it. Reduce the load at the centroid to a force plus a couple; the force produces a uniform direct shear over the whole weld, and the couple produces a torsional shear proportional to distance from the centroid. Add the two as vectors at each candidate point, and the largest resultant governs.

The one convenience welds allow is the unit-throat trick. Since throat thickness is constant and unknown at the start, treat the welds as lines of unit thickness, compute the group's properties per unit throat, get a result in force per unit length, and divide by the allowable stress at the end to obtain the throat required. The algebra stays linear and the throat never has to be guessed.

Key relation — eccentric weld group, unit throat

\[ \tau'=\frac{P}{A_w}, \qquad \tau''=\frac{Tr}{J_u}, \qquad J_u=I_x+I_y, \qquad R=|\vec\tau'+\vec\tau''| \]

\( A_w \) is total weld length, \( J_u \) the polar second moment of the weld lines about the group centroid, and \( r \) the distance to the point being checked. Required throat is \( t=R/\tau_{all} \).

Two habits matter. Take the components of \( \tau'' \) rather than its magnitude — the resultant depends on the angle between the two shears, and adding magnitudes overstates the answer at some corners and understates it at others. And check every corner: on an asymmetric group the worst point is not always the farthest one, because the direct shear may oppose the torsional shear there.

03 · Where welds crack

Welds fail in fatigue at the toe — the line where the weld metal meets the parent plate. Three things meet at that line and all of them are unhelpful. The geometry changes abruptly, so there is a stress concentration. The weld's own cooling contraction leaves a tensile residual stress there, often near yield. And the toe is where slag inclusions and undercut end up, so it is where the sharpest defects are.

The consequence is a fact that surprises people the first time: the fatigue strength of a welded detail is essentially independent of the parent steel's strength. Specifying a higher-grade steel raises the static capacity and does nothing whatever for the fatigue life of the weld, because the crack starts in a residual-stress field at a geometric notch that the material upgrade has not changed. Fatigue of welded structures is therefore classified by detail geometry — a category number giving the stress range the detail survives at two million cycles — not by material.

04 · Designing for weld fatigue

Those categories run on a law of the form \( \Delta\sigma^3 N = \text{constant} \), and the cube is the whole story. A detail good for 80 MPa of stress range at two million cycles survives about 4.7 million cycles at 60 MPa, and only about 1.02 million at 100 MPa. A 25% increase in stress range costs half the life.

Design follows directly. Reduce the stress range at the toe rather than raising strength anywhere: use a better detail category — a full-penetration butt weld ground flush is in a different class from a fillet-welded lap — put the weld where the stress is low, avoid stopping and starting welds at highly stressed points, and grind or peen the toe when a detail cannot be improved. All of these change the geometry, which is the only variable weld fatigue actually responds to.

Closing. Specify by leg, calculate on throat. Treat a weld group exactly like a bolt group and add the two shears as vectors. Then design the fatigue case around detail geometry and stress range, because the steel grade will not help.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Direct and torsional shear (Strength of Materials L3–L4) — \( \tau=Tr/J \) applied here to a group of lines rather than a solid section.
  • Second moment of area and the parallel-axis theorem (Statics) — assembling \( I_x \) and \( I_y \) for a weld pattern.
  • Vector addition — the two shears act in different directions and must be combined by components.
  • Fatigue and stress range (Lessons 3–4) — the S–N idea, restated here with a fixed exponent and geometry-based categories.
  • Welding processes and defects (Manufacturing Processes II) — undercut, slag, and the residual stresses this lesson attributes toe cracking to.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

An eccentrically loaded weld group

Problem. A bracket is attached to a column by two vertical fillet welds, each 150 mm long and 100 mm apart. A vertical load of 20 kN acts 200 mm out from the weld group's centroid. Using the unit-throat method, find: \( A_w \), \( J_u \), the direct and torsional shears, and the resultant at the critical corner.

Solution. (1) \( A_w = 2(150) = 300\ \mathrm{mm} \). (2) \( I_x = 2(150)^3/12 = 562{,}500 \); \( I_y = 2(150)(50)^2 = 750{,}000 \); \( J_u = 1{,}312{,}500\ \mathrm{mm^3} \). (3) Direct: \( \tau' = 20{,}000/300 = 66.7\ \mathrm{N/mm} \), acting downward. (4) \( T = 20{,}000(200) = 4\times10^6\ \mathrm{N\,mm} \). At the corner \( (x,y)=(50,75) \): \( \tau''_x = Ty/J_u = 228.6 \), \( \tau''_y = Tx/J_u = 152.4\ \mathrm{N/mm} \). (5) At the bottom-right corner the torsional and direct vertical parts add: \( R_x = 228.6 \), \( R_y = 152.4+66.7 = 219.1 \), so \( R = \sqrt{228.6^2+219.1^2} = 316.6\ \mathrm{N/mm} \).

Answer: \( A_w=300\ \mathrm{mm} \), \( J_u=1.3125\times10^6\ \mathrm{mm^3} \), \( R=316.6\ \mathrm{N/mm} \). Sanity check: the resultant is 4.75 times the direct shear alone, which is the measure of how much the eccentricity costs. Note also that adding the magnitudes \( 66.7+274.7=341.4 \) would overstate the answer by 8% — the two shears are not parallel, and components are not optional.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

From resultant to a drawing callout

Problem. The weld group above carries \( R = 316.6\ \mathrm{N/mm} \) at unit throat. The allowable shear stress on the weld metal is 145 MPa. Find: the required throat and leg size, and the factor of safety if a 6 mm fillet is specified instead.

Solution. (1) \( t = R/\tau_{all} = 316.6/145 = 2.18\ \mathrm{mm} \). (2) \( h = t/0.707 = 3.09\ \mathrm{mm} \). (3) A 6 mm fillet gives \( t = 0.707(6) = 4.24\ \mathrm{mm} \), so the stress is \( 316.6/4.24 = 74.6\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (4) \( n = 145/74.6 = 1.94 \).

Answer: 2.18 mm throat, 3.09 mm leg required; a 6 mm fillet gives \( n = 1.94 \). Sanity check: strength called for about 3 mm, and 6 mm was specified — not waste, but the minimum fillet size that plate thickness and process control permit. This is the normal outcome for bracket welds and worth recognising: like the key in Lesson 6, the weld is usually sized by something other than the load it carries.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

The cube law in practice

Problem. A welded detail is classified at 80 MPa stress range at \( 2\times10^6 \) cycles, on the law \( \Delta\sigma^3N = \) constant. Find: the constant, the life at 100 MPa and at 60 MPa, and comment on what a 25% reduction in stress range is worth.

Solution. (1) \( C = 80^3(2\times10^6) = 1.024\times10^{12} \). (2) At 100 MPa: \( N = 1.024\times10^{12}/100^3 = 1.02\times10^6 \) cycles. (3) At 60 MPa: \( N = 1.024\times10^{12}/60^3 = 4.74\times10^6 \) cycles. (4) Going from 80 to 60 MPa — a 25% reduction — multiplies life by \( (80/60)^3 = 2.37 \).

Answer: \( C=1.024\times10^{12} \); 1.02 million cycles at 100 MPa and 4.74 million at 60 MPa. A 25% cut in range is worth about 2.4× the life. Sanity check: the relation is symmetric and brutal in both directions — raising the range from 80 to 100 MPa leaves \( (80/100)^3 = 0.512 \), almost exactly half the life. Any change that quietly increases stress range at a weld toe by a quarter has halved the structure's fatigue life, which is why bracing and detail changes outperform material changes so decisively here.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

A fillet weld is specified with a 10 mm leg. The thickness that actually resists the load is

Failure runs across the throat, the shortest path through the weld, which for an equal-leg fillet is \( 0.707h = 7.07\ \mathrm{mm} \). Designing on the 10 mm leg would overstate capacity by 41%. The 14.1 mm figure is the hypotenuse across the two legs, which is the longest path rather than the shortest, and 5 mm confuses the throat with half the leg. Specify by leg, calculate on throat.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

In the unit-throat method for weld groups, the welds are treated as lines because

At the start of the calculation the throat is precisely what is not yet known. Computing \( A_w \) and \( J_u \) at unit thickness gives a resultant in force per unit length, and dividing by the allowable stress at the end returns the throat directly — no iteration and no guessed starting size. The throat is not negligible, and thickness certainly does affect the stress; it is simply factored out of the geometry and restored at the last step.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

When combining direct and torsional shear on a weld group, using the magnitudes \( \tau'+\tau'' \) rather than vector components

The two shears are vectors with different directions at every point, so the resultant follows from components. On the worked bracket, adding magnitudes gives 341.4 against the correct 316.6 N/mm — 8% high at that corner. It is not reliably conservative either: at a corner where the torsional component opposes the direct shear, the true resultant is smaller still, and at some geometries the worst point is not the farthest one at all. Symmetry does not rescue the shortcut.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Fatigue cracks in welded joints characteristically start at the weld toe because

Three unfavourable things meet at the toe: an abrupt change of section that concentrates stress, a tensile residual stress left by the weld's own contraction that is often close to yield, and the undercut and slag that tend to end up exactly there. Weld metal is normally overmatched — stronger than the parent plate — so material weakness is not the explanation. The throat is a plane through the weld, not a feature of the toe.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Specifying a higher-strength parent steel for a fillet-welded detail loaded in fatigue will

The crack initiates at a geometric notch inside a near-yield residual stress field, and a stronger steel changes neither. This is why welded structures are classified by detail category — a number describing the joint's geometry — and why the design curves carry no material term at all. The practical consequence is that the useful interventions are geometric: a better detail, a ground toe, a weld relocated to lower stress, or a reduced stress range.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A welded detail's stress range is reduced from 100 MPa to 80 MPa. On \( \Delta\sigma^3N=\text{const} \), the life is multiplied by about

\( (100/80)^3 = 1.953 \) — a 20% cut in stress range nearly doubles the life. The 1.25 answer treats the law as linear, which badly undersells what detailing work is worth. The 0.51 figure is the same cube applied the wrong way round, and is what happens if the range goes up from 80 to 100. The cube is the reason small reductions in stress range are the most cost-effective intervention available on a fatigue-limited welded structure.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A 200 mm run of 8 mm fillet weld carries a concentric load. With an allowable shear stress of 145 MPa, its capacity is closest to

The throat is \( 0.707(8) = 5.66\ \mathrm{mm} \), so the resisting area is \( 5.66\times200 = 1131\ \mathrm{mm^2} \) and the capacity is \( 1131(145) = 164\ \mathrm{kN} \). The 232 kN answer uses the 8 mm leg as the resisting thickness — the standard error, and 41% unconservative. Halving to 82 kN would follow from taking the throat as half the leg rather than \( 0.707 \) of it. Note the useful shortcut this suggests: an 8 mm fillet is worth about 0.82 kN per millimetre of run at this allowable.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Two designs carry the same load with the same total weld capacity: design A uses a short 12 mm fillet, design B a longer 6 mm fillet. Design B generally

Strength is proportional to \( 0.707hL \) — linear in leg size — while the cross-sectional area of deposited metal goes as \( h^2/2 \). Halving the leg and doubling the length therefore holds capacity constant while roughly halving the metal deposited, along with the heat input and distortion that come with it. This is why weld callouts favour long small fillets, subject to the minimum size the plate thickness requires and to fitting the weld into the space available.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the bracket that was upgraded into failure. A pipe-support bracket fillet-welded to a structural column develops cracks in its welds after about two years of service under a pulsating line. The repair specification calls for the bracket to be remade from a higher-grade steel, on the reasoning that a stronger bracket will last longer. It cracks again in fourteen months, in the same place — along the weld toe. Nothing had been done about the actual mechanism. Weld-toe fatigue is governed by the detail's geometry and the stress range at the toe, and both were unchanged; the upgraded plate raised static capacity and left the residual stress field, the notch at the toe, and the applied stress range exactly as they were. The successful repair was geometric: the attachment was extended so the weld ended in a region of lower stress, the toe was ground to a smooth radius, and the support was re-braced to cut the stress range by roughly a quarter — which, on a cube law, is worth about double the life on its own. The general lesson is worth carrying into any review: when a crack is at a weld toe, the question is never which steel, it is which detail and how large a stress range.
