Lesson 01 · Design methodology and factors of safety

01 · Design is analysis run backwards

Analysis asks: given this part, what stress appears? Design asks the harder inverse: given this load and this consequence of failure, what part? Analysis has one answer; design has a family of them, and the engineering is in the choosing. Every design decision is therefore two decisions — a size, and a reason for that size that survives being questioned a year later by someone holding a broken piece of it.

02 · Load cases before geometry

The first discipline is refusing to size anything until the load cases are written down. Steady operating load is rarely the governing one. Start-up torque, upset pressure, thermal growth against a fixed anchor, a dropped tool, a lifting case during maintenance, and the seismic or wind case if the structure is outdoors — each is a separate load case, and the part must survive the worst of them, not their average. A part that fails in service almost never failed under the load the designer analysed; it failed under the one nobody wrote down.

03 · The factor of safety, honestly

The factor of safety is the ratio of the strength you rely on to the stress you expect:

Key relation — design margin

\[ n = \frac{S}{\sigma}, \qquad \sigma_{allow} = \frac{S}{n} \]

\( S \) is the governing strength — yield for permanent-deformation limits, ultimate for rupture, endurance limit for fatigue — and \( \sigma \) is the stress the load case produces. Choosing \( n \) is choosing how much ignorance you are buying insurance against.

What \( n \) actually covers is worth naming, because it is not one thing: scatter in material properties, uncertainty in the load, idealisation error in the analysis, degradation in service, and the consequence of failure. A bracket holding a cable tray and a bracket holding a pressure vessel get different numbers for the last reason alone. A large \( n \) is not conservatism; it is an admission of how much you do not know, and it is expensive in mass, cost and — in rotating equipment — in the very inertia you are trying to control.

04 · The design working method

Use the same sequence every time. One: write the load cases, each with its source and its own worst-credible magnitude. Two: identify the failure mode each case could provoke — yielding, rupture, fatigue, buckling, wear, leakage — because the governing strength depends on the mode, not on the load. Three: choose \( n \) and record why, in the calculation, in one sentence. Four: size the part, then check every other failure mode against that size; the mode that governs is rarely the one you started with. Five: state the result as a drawing callout, not as a stress — a number that cannot be inspected has not been designed.

Codes and standards are encoded experience, and they deserve respect precisely because they are other people's failures written down. But a code gives a floor, not an answer: it tells you the minimum a competent engineer may claim, and it assumes you have identified the right load cases and the right failure mode. Meeting a code with the wrong load case is fully compliant and fully wrong.

Closing. Design is a defensible size. The margin is yours to justify, the load cases are yours to enumerate, and the code is a floor beneath both.

Lesson 02 · Static failure design

01 · One number from a stress state

Material strength is quoted from a tension test — a single axis, a single number. Real parts carry combined stress: bending with torsion, pressure with axial load, a bolt in tension while a joint shears. A failure theory is the bridge, collapsing a multiaxial stress state into one equivalent stress that may be compared against the tensile yield strength honestly.

02 · Von Mises and Tresca

Key relations — the two static theories

\[ \sigma' = \sqrt{\sigma_x^2 - \sigma_x\sigma_y + \sigma_y^2 + 3\tau_{xy}^2}, \qquad \sigma_{Tresca} = \sigma_{max} - \sigma_{min} \]

Von Mises (distortion energy) predicts yielding of ductile metals most accurately; Tresca (maximum shear) is always the more conservative of the two, by up to about 15%.

Both take the same input and answer the same question, and Tresca never predicts a higher capacity than von Mises. That is why codes fond of conservatism adopt Tresca while a designer chasing mass adopts von Mises — and why quoting a factor of safety without naming the theory is an incomplete statement.

03 · Stress concentrations, counted honestly

Nominal stress is what the average section carries; a hole, a fillet or a keyway multiplies it locally by a geometric factor \( K_t \), so \( \sigma_{max} = K_t \sigma_{nom} \). For a static load on a ductile material, local yielding blunts the peak and redistributes it, so \( K_t \) is commonly set aside. Under fatigue, or in a brittle material, that permission is withdrawn entirely — which is the bridge to Lesson 3, and the reason a shaft's shoulder radius is a design variable rather than a drafting convenience.

04 · The static sizing method

One: resolve the stress state at the worst point — usually a surface, usually at a discontinuity. Two: get the principal stresses, by Mohr's circle or by formula. Three: pick a theory and say which. Four: compare against \( S_y/n \). Five: decide whether \( K_t \) applies — ductile and static, usually not; brittle or fatigue, always. The commonest error in practice is comparing a raw shear stress against a tensile yield strength, which mixes two incompatible quantities and is optimistic by roughly the factor \( \sqrt{3} \).

Closing. A failure theory is a translator, not a truth. Name the one you used, and the number you report becomes checkable.

Lesson 01 · Design methodology and factors of safety

What this lesson assumes

  • Axial stress and strain (Strength of Materials L1) — \( \sigma = F/A \) and what yield means physically.
  • Material property vocabulary (Materials I L7) — yield strength, tensile strength, ductility, and which is a design stress.
  • Free-body diagrams (Statics L3) — every load case begins as one.

What this lesson assumes

  • Yield strength (Materials I L7) — a tensile-test number being borrowed for a multiaxial state.
