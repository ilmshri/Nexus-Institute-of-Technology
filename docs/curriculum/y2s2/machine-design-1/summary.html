MDN 251 — Machine Design I

Lesson 01 · Design methodology and factors of safety

  • Analysis asks what stress a given part sees; design asks the inverse — given the load and the consequence of failure, what part? Analysis has one answer, design has a family.
  • Write the load cases before sizing anything: start-up, upset, thermal growth against a fixed anchor, dropped tool, maintenance lifting, wind or seismic. The part must survive the worst, not the average.
  • Parts that fail in service rarely failed under the load that was analysed — they failed under the one nobody wrote down.
  • The design margin is \( n = S/\sigma \), with \( S \) the strength governing the failure mode: yield for permanent set, ultimate for rupture, endurance limit for fatigue.
  • \( n \) covers several distinct things — material scatter, load uncertainty, idealisation error, service degradation, and the consequence of failure. A cable-tray bracket and a pressure-vessel bracket differ on the last alone.
  • A large \( n \) is not conservatism but an admission of ignorance, and it costs mass, money and — on rotating equipment — the inertia the design is trying to control.
  • Work the same sequence every time: load cases, failure modes, choose and record \( n \), size, then re-check every other mode against that size — the governing mode is rarely the one you started with.
  • Codes and standards are encoded experience: they carry the failures that shaped them, which is why departing from one requires an argument rather than a preference.

Lesson 02 · Static failure design

  • Strength is quoted from a uniaxial tension test; real parts carry combined stress. A failure theory collapses a multiaxial state into one equivalent stress that can be compared against that single number honestly.
  • Von Mises: \( \sigma' = \sqrt{\sigma_x^2-\sigma_x\sigma_y+\sigma_y^2+3\tau_{xy}^2} \) — the most accurate predictor of yielding in ductile metals.
  • Tresca: \( \sigma_{max}-\sigma_{min} \) — always the more conservative, by up to about 15%, and never predicting a higher capacity than von Mises.
  • A factor of safety quoted without naming the theory used is an incomplete statement.
  • A discontinuity multiplies nominal stress geometrically: \( \sigma_{max}=K_t\sigma_{nom} \).
  • For a static load on a ductile material, local yielding blunts and redistributes the peak, so \( K_t \) is commonly set aside. That permission is withdrawn entirely for brittle materials and under fatigue — the bridge into Lesson 3.
  • Method: resolve the stress state at the worst point, get the principal stresses, name a theory, compare against \( S_y/n \), then decide whether \( K_t \) applies.
  • The commonest practical error is comparing a raw shear stress against a tensile yield strength — optimistic by roughly \( \sqrt3 \).

Lesson 03 · Fatigue design I: the S–N method

  • A part that never approaches yield can still fail: fatigue accumulates damage under fluctuating stress, and most rotating-machinery failures are fatigue failures invisible to a yield check.
  • For wrought steels a first estimate of the specimen endurance limit is \( S_e' = 0.5S_{ut} \), capped near 700 MPa — a mirror-polished laboratory value, not a design stress.
  • The Marin factors correct it to the real part: \( S_e = k_ak_bk_ck_dk_e S_e' \). Each is at most 1.0, and because they multiply they stack fast.
  • Surface finish dominates, because fatigue cracks start at the surface and roughness supplies ready-made initiation sites. Size punishes the larger stressed volume; reliability is a choice about where in the scatter band you design, not a material property.
  • On the worked 30 mm machined shaft, five modest factors left 58% of the specimen value — \( S_e = 174 \) MPa against \( S_e' = 300 \).
  • Compare the stress amplitude against \( S_e/n \), never the peak: fatigue is driven by the alternating component.
  • Carry \( K_f \) always — the ductile-static permission of Lesson 2 does not survive cyclic loading, because the notch root is where the crack grows a little on every cycle.
  • Designing directly against \( 0.5S_{ut} \) claims about 1.7 times the fatigue strength the part actually has, with every individual arithmetic step correct.

Lesson 04 · Fatigue design II: mean stress and cumulative damage

  • A tensile mean stress reduces the amplitude a part can survive, so a fatigue state needs two coordinates — \( \sigma_a \) and \( \sigma_m \) — not one.
  • Constant-life criteria all run between \( S_e \) on the amplitude axis and \( S_{ut} \) on the mean axis. Goodman is the straight line between them: \( \sigma_a/S_e + \sigma_m/S_{ut} = 1/n \).
  • Gerber's parabola is less conservative (2.56 vs Goodman's 2.09 on this lesson's case); Soderberg anchors at \( S_y \) and alone rules out first-cycle yielding.
  • Setting \( \sigma_m = 0 \) returns every criterion to \( n = S_e/\sigma_a \), Lesson 3's result.
  • Variable-amplitude duty is summed with Miner's rule, \( D=\sum n_i/N_i \), with lives read off the finite-life line \( S_f = aN^{\,b} \).
  • Miner's rule ignores load order and real failures scatter between \( D\approx0.5 \) and \( D\approx2 \). Treat \( D=0.9 \) as no demonstrated margin.

Lesson 05 · Shaft design

  • A rotating shaft under fixed transverse load sees fully reversed bending (\( \sigma_m=0 \)) but steady torque (\( \tau_a=0 \)) — the two loads enter the fatigue criterion through different terms.
  • DE-Goodman inverts to a diameter directly: \( d^3=\frac{16n}{\pi}\left[\frac{2K_fM_a}{S_e}+\frac{\sqrt3K_{fs}T_m}{S_{ut}}\right] \).
  • Size at the critical section, not the maximum-moment section — a shoulder or keyway with a high \( K_f \) often governs.
  • Diameter responds to the cube root: doubling the factor of safety costs only 26% more diameter, so fatigue margin on a shaft is cheap.
  • Strength is not enough. Check bearing slope (about 0.001 rad) and gear-mesh deflection — stiffness depends on \( d^4 \) and \( E \), so a stronger steel does not help.
  • Check first critical speed, \( \omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}=\sqrt{g/\delta} \), and keep the operating speed well below it.
  • When a shaft is marginal, a bigger fillet radius, a ground finish, or a relocated keyway usually buys more life per unit cost than extra diameter.

Lesson 06 · Keys, splines, and retaining hardware

  • Torque reaches a clearance-fitted hub through a key; everything else follows from the tangential force \( F = 2T/d \).
  • Two checks, same force, different areas: shear over \( wL \) along the interface, bearing over \( (h/2)L \) on the driving faces.
  • The capacities divide down to \( T_{bearing}/T_{shear} = h/w \) — bearing governs for any key flatter than it is wide, and the two are equal for a square key.
  • Key length is set by the hub, not by the torque: the Lesson 5 duty needs 7.3 mm and gets 60 mm, about eight times the strength requirement.
  • That surplus is intentional. Keys must survive start and stall transients, and the key is designed as the mechanical fuse — yielding near 1630 N·m against the shaft's 2610 N·m.
  • A keyway costs \( K_f \ge 2 \) in the shaft. Splines (about 1070 N·m here, only ~25% of teeth assumed loaded) and interference fits (\( T=\mu p\pi d^2L/2 \), about 905 N·m) avoid the slot.
  • Retaining rings carry thrust, not torque, and their groove dimensions — depth, width, corner radius — are structural, not cosmetic.

Lesson 07 · Threaded fasteners and bolted joints

  • A preloaded joint is two springs in opposition: the bolt stretches, the members are compressed, and an external load is shared between them.
  • \( C=k_b/(k_b+k_m) \) is the fraction of external load reaching the bolt. For M12 through a 40 mm steel grip, \( C=0.156 \) — a 20 kN load raises bolt tension by only 3.1 kN.
  • \( F_b=F_i+CP \) and \( F_m=F_i-(1-C)P \), and always \( F_b-F_m=P \).
  • Separation at \( P_0=F_i/(1-C) \) is usually the governing failure, not bolt yield — here 2.25 against 4.05. Design for clamp force.
  • Preload is normally \( 0.75A_tS_p \) for reusable joints; the tensile stress area follows from \( A_t=\frac{\pi}{4}(d-0.9382p)^2 \).
  • Under cyclic load the bolt's amplitude is \( CP/2A_t \) — smaller than an unpreloaded bolt's by exactly \( 1/C \), here 6.4×. Tightening a bolt properly makes it last longer.
  • Softening the members raises \( C \). A gasket at \( k_m/4 \) takes the bolt's stress amplitude from 18.5 to 50.4 MPa with no change to the applied load.

Lesson 08 · Welded joint design

  • Fillet welds are specified by leg \( h \) but fail on the throat \( t=0.707h \) — a 6 mm fillet resists on 4.24 mm.
  • Weld volume grows as \( h^2 \) while strength grows as \( h \), so long small fillets beat short large ones for the same capacity.
  • An eccentric load on a weld group is solved exactly like a bolt group: direct shear \( P/A_w \) plus torsional shear \( Tr/J_u \), added as vectors at each corner.
  • Use the unit-throat method — treat welds as lines, work in force per unit length, and divide by the allowable at the end to get the throat.
  • On the worked bracket, eccentricity took 66.7 N/mm of direct shear to a resultant of 316.6 N/mm — a factor of 4.75 from the moment alone.
  • Welds crack at the toe, where a geometric notch, near-yield tensile residual stress, and the worst defects coincide.
  • Weld fatigue strength is essentially independent of the parent steel's grade; details are classified by geometry, on \( \Delta\sigma^3N=\text{const} \).
  • The cube law is severe: 80 → 100 MPa of stress range halves the life. Improve the detail or cut the range; do not upgrade the steel.

Lesson 09 · Springs

  • A helical spring works in torsion: the axial force acts at the mean coil radius, so \( T=FD/2 \).
  • The Wahl factor \( K_W \) collects coil curvature and direct shear, putting peak stress on the inner fibre — 1.197 at \( C=7.5 \), and that is where springs fail.
  • Spring index \( C=D/d \) should sit between about 4 and 12; below 4 the coil is hard to wind and \( K_W \) climbs steeply.
  • Rate \( k=Gd^4/(8D^3N_a) \) — the fourth power means a 12% thinner wire loses 41% of the rate. Wire diameter is the design, not a tolerance.
  • Check solid height \( L_s=dN_t \): a good spring survives being closed solid without yielding (here 564 MPa against 814, \( n=1.44 \)).
  • Check buckling: stable while \( L_0<2.63D/\alpha \), with \( \alpha \) set by the end conditions.
  • Springs cycle between two positive forces, so fatigue is a large-mean, small-amplitude Goodman problem in shear.
  • Shot peening puts compression into the inner fibre where cracks start and typically buys 20% or more on endurance — usually the cheapest life available.

Lesson 10 · Power screws

  • A screw thread is an inclined plane wrapped round a cylinder: \( \tan\lambda=l/\pi d_m \), with \( d_m=d-p/2 \).
  • Lead is travel per revolution and equals pitch only for a single start — a double start doubles the lead and the efficiency benefit that comes with it.
  • Raising and lowering torques differ only in the sign of the friction terms; a positive \( T_L \) means the screw is self-locking.
  • Self-locking requires \( \mu>\tan\lambda\cos\alpha \) — independent of load, but dependent on a friction coefficient that can drift with lubricant and wear.
  • Self-locking caps efficiency: at the boundary it is 48.9%, and no self-locking screw exceeds 50%. The worked screw runs at 30% on the thread alone.
  • Thread angle enters through \( \sec\alpha \) — a square thread is the most efficient form and the hardest to manufacture.
  • Collar friction \( T_c=F\mu_cd_c/2 \) does no useful work. Here it is 46% of the total torque and drops overall efficiency from 29.5% to 16.1%.
  • A thrust bearing in place of a plain collar is usually the cheapest large improvement available.

Lesson 11 · Design project: a driven shaft assembly

  • State the duty once — \( T=P/\omega=108.3\ \mathrm{N\,m} \) — and reference it everywhere; every downstream number inherits it.
  • Trace the load path: belt → pulley → key → shaft → bearings → pedestals → welds, with torque branching through the coupling.
  • A belt drives on \( T_1-T_2 \) but loads the shaft with \( T_1+T_2 \) — here 866 N of useful force against a 1733 N transverse pull.
  • Bending (130 N·m) exceeds torque (108 N·m), so this shaft is bending-governed, as belt drives usually are.
  • Results: shaft 34.0 mm computed and 35 mm specified (\( n=2.19 \)); key \( n=6.0 \); friction-driven coupling bolts \( n=6.5 \); pedestal welds \( n=55 \).
  • The margins span 25×. The design lives in the shaft; everything else is set by hub length, standard bolt size or minimum fillet — so that is where any improvement or uprate must go.
  • Document so it can be reviewed: assumptions attributed, governing criterion named per component, and the computed answer kept distinct from the specified size.
