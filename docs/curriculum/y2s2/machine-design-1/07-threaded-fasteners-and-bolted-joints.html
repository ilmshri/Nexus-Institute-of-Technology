Threaded fasteners and bolted joints

Bolt mechanics: preload, joint stiffness, and the load-sharing triangle. Torque–preload relations and their scatter. Gasketed-joint implications for every flange a plant owns.

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Why does preload protect a bolt from fatigue?
  • What fraction of external load does a stiff joint's bolt actually see?

01 · What the manufacturing course established, and what it left

Manufacturing Processes II settled how preload is achieved: torque is only a proxy, the nut factor \( K \) is an assumption rather than a constant, and the same torque on a lubricated bolt delivers substantially more preload than on a dry one. Take all of that as given here. This lesson answers the design question underneath it — why the joint wants preload at all, and how much.

The naive picture is that a bolt holds a joint the way a hook holds a bucket: apply 20 kN to the joint and the bolt carries 20 kN. That picture is wrong, and the extent to which it is wrong is the most useful number in fastener design.

02 · Two springs, and the load-sharing constant

Tightening a bolt stretches it and compresses the members it clamps. Both are elastic, so both are springs, and they are loaded in opposition: an external load that stretches the bolt further simultaneously lets the members expand back toward their free length. The two share the load in proportion to their stiffnesses.

Key relation — load sharing in a preloaded joint

\[ C=\frac{k_b}{k_b+k_m}, \qquad F_b=F_i+CP, \qquad F_m=F_i-(1-C)P \]

\( C \) is the fraction of an external load \( P \) that reaches the bolt. Since \( F_b - F_m = P \) always, the two expressions are one statement: whatever the members stop carrying, the bolt picks up.

For an M12 bolt through a 40 mm steel grip, \( k_b \approx 436 \) and \( k_m \approx 2361\ \mathrm{MN/m} \), giving \( C = 0.156 \). A 20 kN external load raises bolt tension by 3.1 kN and reduces clamp force by 16.9 kN. The bolt barely notices. What the joint actually spends under load is not bolt strength — it is clamp force.

03 · The two ways a preloaded joint fails

Because clamp force is the currency, the first failure mode is separation: when \( F_m \) reaches zero the members part, and from that instant the bolt carries the entire external load directly. Every protective property in the paragraph above vanishes at once. The separation load is \( P_0 = F_i/(1-C) \), which for the worked joint is 44.9 kN against an applied 20 kN — a margin of 2.25.

The second is bolt yielding, checked against the proof strength: \( n = (S_pA_t-F_i)/(CP) \), here 4.05. Note which of the two governs. The bolt is the strong part of this joint by a wide margin; separation arrives first, and a designer worrying about bolt strength while ignoring clamp force is watching the wrong number. Preload is normally set at about 75% of proof load for a reusable connection precisely to push separation as far away as possible without yielding the bolt on assembly.

04 · Fatigue, and why preload is the cheapest fix available

Fluctuating loads are where the stiffness ratio pays for itself. For a load cycling between zero and \( P \), the bolt's stress amplitude is \( \sigma_a = CP/2A_t \) — the external amplitude scaled by \( C \). In the worked joint that is 18.5 MPa. Remove the preload and the bolt would see \( P/2A_t = 118.7\ \mathrm{MPa} \) instead, larger by a factor of exactly \( 1/C = 6.4 \).

This is worth stating plainly, because it inverts an intuition. Tightening a bolt more makes it last longer under cyclic load. The mean stress rises, which Lesson 4 says is damaging, but the amplitude falls by far more, and fatigue answers to amplitude first. The overwhelming majority of fatigue failures in bolted joints are loose-joint failures.

The same reasoning explains why gaskets are treated with suspicion in dynamically loaded joints. A soft gasket collapses \( k_m \), which drives \( C \) up, which routes the external load into the bolt:

Joint construction\( C \)Bolt stress amplitude
Steel on steel0.15618.5 MPa
Gasketed, \( k_m/4 \)0.42550.4 MPa
Soft gasket, \( k_m/10 \)0.64977.0 MPa

A soft gasket has multiplied the bolt's fatigue loading fourfold without a single number in the load specification changing. This is the design fact behind a rule most plants enforce by habit: use the hardest, thinnest gasket the sealing duty permits, and never substitute a softer one because it seals more easily.

Closing. A preloaded joint is two springs in opposition, and \( C \) says how the load divides between them. Design for clamp force, check separation before bolt strength, and treat any change that softens the members as a change to the bolt's fatigue life.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Torque–preload and the nut factor (Manufacturing Processes II L10) — \( T=KdF_i \) and why \( K \) is an assumption; this lesson takes preload as achieved and asks what it does.
  • Axial stiffness (Strength of Materials L1) — \( k=AE/L \), the model for the bolt spring.
  • Springs in parallel (Physics I) — two elements sharing a common deflection share load in proportion to stiffness.
  • Goodman criterion (Lesson 4) — the fatigue check applied to the bolt, with preload supplying a large mean stress.
  • Proof strength — the fastener-specific allowable, below yield, that grade markings are defined against.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

How an external load actually divides

Problem. An M12 × 1.75 grade 8.8 bolt (\( S_p=600\ \mathrm{MPa} \)) clamps a 40 mm grip of steel members. Take \( E=207\ \mathrm{GPa} \), \( k_b=A_tE/l \), and the Wileman member stiffness \( k_m=EdA e^{Bd/l} \) with \( A=0.78715 \), \( B=0.62873 \). The joint carries an external load of 20 kN per bolt, and preload is set at 75% of proof load. Find: \( A_t \), \( C \), the preload, and the bolt and member loads under the external load.

Solution. (1) \( A_t=\frac{\pi}{4}(12-0.9382\times1.75)^2 = \frac{\pi}{4}(10.358)^2 = 84.3\ \mathrm{mm^2} \). (2) \( k_b = (84.3\times10^{-6})(207\times10^9)/0.040 = 436\ \mathrm{MN/m} \). (3) \( k_m = (207\times10^9)(0.012)(0.78715)e^{0.62873(0.012)/0.040} = 1.955\times10^9 \times e^{0.1886} = 2361\ \mathrm{MN/m} \). (4) \( C = 436/(436+2361) = 0.156 \). (5) \( F_i = 0.75(84.3\times10^{-6})(600\times10^6) = 37{,}920\ \mathrm{N} \). (6) \( F_b = 37{,}920+0.156(20{,}000) = 41{,}038\ \mathrm{N} \); \( F_m = 37{,}920-0.844(20{,}000) = 21{,}038\ \mathrm{N} \).

Answer: \( A_t=84.3\ \mathrm{mm^2} \), \( C=0.156 \), \( F_i=37.9 \), \( F_b=41.0 \), \( F_m=21.0\ \mathrm{kN} \). Sanity check: \( F_b-F_m = 41{,}038-21{,}038 = 20{,}000\ \mathrm{N} \), exactly the applied load — the check that catches almost any arithmetic slip in this calculation. Note the physical result: a 20 kN load added 3.1 kN to the bolt and removed 16.9 kN of clamp force. The joint spends clamp force, not bolt strength.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Which failure arrives first

Problem. For the same joint (\( C=0.156 \), \( F_i=37{,}920\ \mathrm{N} \), \( A_t=84.3\ \mathrm{mm^2} \), \( S_p=600\ \mathrm{MPa} \), \( P=20\ \mathrm{kN} \)) find: the load at which the joint separates, the factor of safety against separation, and the factor of safety against bolt yielding. State which governs.

Solution. (1) Separation when \( F_m=0 \): \( P_0 = F_i/(1-C) = 37{,}920/0.844 = 44{,}923\ \mathrm{N} \). (2) \( n_{sep} = P_0/P = 44{,}923/20{,}000 = 2.25 \). (3) Yielding when \( F_b = S_pA_t = (600\times10^6)(84.3\times10^{-6}) = 50{,}580\ \mathrm{N} \): \( n_{yield} = (50{,}580-37{,}920)/(0.156\times20{,}000) = 12{,}660/3{,}118 = 4.05 \).

Answer: \( P_0=44.9\ \mathrm{kN} \), \( n_{sep}=2.25 \), \( n_{yield}=4.05 \) — separation governs. Sanity check: the bolt is nearly twice as far from yielding as the joint is from opening, which is the normal state of a properly preloaded steel joint and the reason clamp force is the design variable. A useful cross-check on \( n_{sep} \): at \( P=P_0 \) the member load should compute to zero, and indeed \( 37{,}920-0.844(44{,}923)=0 \).

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

What preload buys under cyclic load

Problem. The 20 kN external load on the same joint fluctuates between zero and its full value. Find: the bolt's stress amplitude, and the amplitude the same bolt would see if the joint were assembled with no effective preload so that the bolt carried the whole external load. Express the improvement as a ratio.

Solution. (1) Preloaded: the bolt's share of the load range is \( CP = 0.156(20{,}000) = 3{,}118\ \mathrm{N} \), so the force amplitude is \( CP/2 = 1{,}559\ \mathrm{N} \) and \( \sigma_a = 1{,}559/(84.3\times10^{-6}) = 18.5\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (2) Unpreloaded: the bolt takes all of \( P \), so \( \sigma_a = 10{,}000/(84.3\times10^{-6}) = 118.7\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (3) Ratio \( 118.7/18.5 = 6.4 \).

Answer: 18.5 MPa preloaded against 118.7 MPa unpreloaded — a factor of 6.4. Sanity check: that ratio is exactly \( 1/C = 1/0.156 = 6.4 \), which it must be, since preload divides the bolt's share of the load range by \( C \) and changes nothing else about the amplitude. The counter-intuitive conclusion is sound: tightening the bolt raises its mean stress but cuts its amplitude sixfold, and fatigue answers to amplitude first. Loose joints, not overtightened ones, are what break bolts.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

In a preloaded bolted joint, the stiffness constant \( C=k_b/(k_b+k_m) \) represents

\( C \) partitions the external load: the bolt takes \( CP \) and the members give up \( (1-C)P \) of clamp force. Preload itself is not divided by \( C \) — it is equal and opposite in the two elements by definition. The torque–preload conversion is governed by the nut factor \( K \), an entirely separate quantity covered in Manufacturing Processes II. Because members are typically several times stiffer than the bolt, \( C \) is usually small, around 0.1 to 0.2 for steel-on-steel.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

A joint has \( F_i = 30\ \mathrm{kN} \) and \( C = 0.20 \). Under a 25 kN external load, the clamp force remaining between the members is

\( F_m = F_i - (1-C)P = 30 - 0.8(25) = 30 - 20 = 10\ \mathrm{kN} \). The 5 kN answer subtracts the full external load, which would be right only if the members were infinitely stiff. The bolt meanwhile rises to \( 30+0.2(25)=35\ \mathrm{kN} \) — that is the last option, the bolt load rather than the clamp force. Note how close this joint is to separating: 10 kN of clamp force left from 30, at only 25 kN of a load that would separate it at 37.5 kN.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

Once an external load exceeds the separation load \( P_0 \),

Separation is the point at which clamp force reaches zero. Beyond it there is nothing for the members to give up, so the sharing mechanism ends and the bolt takes the whole load — with the fatigue amplitude jumping by the factor \( 1/C \) that preload had been buying. This is why separation, not bolt yield, is normally the governing check: crossing it does not merely reduce a margin, it removes the protective behaviour the joint was designed around.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Replacing a thin hard gasket with a thick soft one, all loads unchanged, will

A soft gasket lowers \( k_m \), and since \( C=k_b/(k_b+k_m) \), lowering \( k_m \) raises \( C \) — routing more of the external load into the bolt. Cutting \( k_m \) to a quarter takes \( C \) from 0.156 to 0.425 and the bolt's amplitude from 18.5 to 50.4 MPa. The gasket does not absorb load in any protective sense; it makes the joint's stiff element less stiff. Separation load also falls, since \( P_0=F_i/(1-C) \) shrinks as \( C \) grows.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

The tensile stress area of an M12 × 1.75 bolt, from \( A_t=\frac{\pi}{4}(d-0.9382p)^2 \), is closest to

\( 12-0.9382(1.75)=10.358\ \mathrm{mm} \), and \( \frac{\pi}{4}(10.358)^2=84.3\ \mathrm{mm^2} \). The 113 mm² answer is the area of the full 12 mm shank, which overstates capacity by a third — threads remove real material and the stress area exists precisely to account for that. Using the minor diameter alone would give about 76 mm², slightly conservative; the standard formula sits between the two because the failure surface runs through neither extreme exactly.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Preload for a reusable connection is commonly specified as \( 0.75A_tS_p \) rather than a smaller fraction because

Both benefits scale with preload. Separation load is \( F_i/(1-C) \), so more preload directly buys more margin before the joint opens, and staying clamped is what keeps the bolt's fatigue amplitude at \( CP/2A_t \) rather than the far larger unpreloaded value. The 0.75 factor is where those gains stop before assembly stress reaches proof. It does not compensate for nut-factor uncertainty — that scatter is exactly why the remaining 25% margin is needed.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A bolt in a dynamically loaded joint keeps failing in fatigue at the first engaged thread. Of the following, the change most likely to help is

Fatigue answers to amplitude, and amplitude is \( CP/2A_t \) — reduced by keeping the joint clamped and by anything that lowers \( C \), which means stiffer members. Reducing torque moves the joint toward separation and can increase the amplitude several-fold; it is the intuitive answer and the wrong one. A softer gasket raises \( C \) and makes matters worse. A longer bolt lowers \( k_b \) and so lowers \( C \) slightly, which is a genuine if modest help — but it is second-order beside simply having the joint properly tight.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Two joints carry the same external load. Joint A has \( C=0.15 \); joint B, identical but with a much less stiff member stack, has \( C=0.60 \). Compared with A, the bolt in B experiences

Amplitude scales directly with \( C \), so \( 0.60/0.15 = 4 \). The whole of a preloaded joint's fatigue advantage lives in keeping \( C \) small, which means keeping the members far stiffer than the bolt. It is worth noticing that nothing about the bolt itself changed between the two joints — same grade, same size, same load — and its fatigue duty still quadrupled. Joint stiffness is a design variable, not a detail.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the gasket substitution that broke the bolts. A pump discharge flange has sealed without complaint for years on a thin, hard gasket. During a shutdown the specified gasket is unavailable and a softer, thicker one is fitted instead — it seals immediately, at lower torque, and everyone regards this as an improvement. Within four months bolts begin failing one at a time, always fracturing at the first engaged thread, always with the beach marks of fatigue rather than the cup-and-cone of overload. Nothing about the process changed: same pressure, same pump, same pulsation from the same reciprocating source. What changed was \( k_m \). The softer gasket cut member stiffness roughly fourfold, which raised \( C \) from about 0.16 to about 0.43, which raised the bolts' stress amplitude from roughly 18 to roughly 50 MPa — a load the bolts had never seen and were never selected for. The lower torque made it worse, since less preload meant a lower separation load as well. The repair was to return to the specified gasket, not to upgrade the bolts. The transferable point is that a gasket is a structural member of the joint, and substituting one is a stiffness change that silently re-rates every bolt around it.
