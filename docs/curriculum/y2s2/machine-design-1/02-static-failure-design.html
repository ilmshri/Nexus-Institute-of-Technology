Understanding Failure Theories (Tresca, von Mises etc...)THE EFFICIENT ENGINEER

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Apply von Mises to a bracket's worst point: what inputs do you need?
  • Why include Kt in static design of brittle parts but often not ductile ones?

01 · One number from a stress state

Material strength is quoted from a tension test — a single axis, a single number. Real parts carry combined stress: bending with torsion, pressure with axial load, a bolt in tension while a joint shears. A failure theory is the bridge, collapsing a multiaxial stress state into one equivalent stress that may be compared against the tensile yield strength honestly.

02 · Von Mises and Tresca

Key relations — the two static theories

\[ \sigma' = \sqrt{\sigma_x^2 - \sigma_x\sigma_y + \sigma_y^2 + 3\tau_{xy}^2}, \qquad \sigma_{Tresca} = \sigma_{max} - \sigma_{min} \]

Von Mises (distortion energy) predicts yielding of ductile metals most accurately; Tresca (maximum shear) is always the more conservative of the two, by up to about 15%.

Both take the same input and answer the same question, and Tresca never predicts a higher capacity than von Mises. That is why codes fond of conservatism adopt Tresca while a designer chasing mass adopts von Mises — and why quoting a factor of safety without naming the theory is an incomplete statement.

03 · Stress concentrations, counted honestly

Nominal stress is what the average section carries; a hole, a fillet or a keyway multiplies it locally by a geometric factor \( K_t \), so \( \sigma_{max} = K_t \sigma_{nom} \). For a static load on a ductile material, local yielding blunts the peak and redistributes it, so \( K_t \) is commonly set aside. Under fatigue, or in a brittle material, that permission is withdrawn entirely — which is the bridge to Lesson 3, and the reason a shaft's shoulder radius is a design variable rather than a drafting convenience.

04 · The static sizing method

One: resolve the stress state at the worst point — usually a surface, usually at a discontinuity. Two: get the principal stresses, by Mohr's circle or by formula. Three: pick a theory and say which. Four: compare against \( S_y/n \). Five: decide whether \( K_t \) applies — ductile and static, usually not; brittle or fatigue, always. The commonest error in practice is comparing a raw shear stress against a tensile yield strength, which mixes two incompatible quantities and is optimistic by roughly the factor \( \sqrt{3} \).

Closing. A failure theory is a translator, not a truth. Name the one you used, and the number you report becomes checkable.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Principal stresses and Mohr's circle (Strength of Materials) — the transformation itself, not just the formula.
  • Yield strength (Materials I L7) — a tensile-test number being borrowed for a multiaxial state.
  • Factor of safety (Lesson 1) — the allowable stress this lesson compares against.

Glossary

TermEquationWhen to use

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

The same state, two theories

Problem. A shaft surface carries \( \sigma_x = 80\ \mathrm{MPa} \) bending and \( \tau_{xy} = 40\ \mathrm{MPa} \) torsional shear, with \( \sigma_y = 0 \). \( S_y = 250\ \mathrm{MPa} \). Find: the principal stresses, the von Mises and Tresca equivalent stresses, and the factor of safety by each theory.

Solution. (1) Centre \( (80+0)/2 = 40 \); radius \( \sqrt{40^2+40^2} = 56.57\ \mathrm{MPa} \). Principals: \( 96.57 \) and \( -16.57\ \mathrm{MPa} \), with the third equal to zero. (2) Von Mises: \( \sqrt{80^2 + 3(40)^2} = \sqrt{11\,200} = 105.83\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (3) Tresca: \( \sigma_{max}-\sigma_{min} = 96.57-(-16.57) = 113.14\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (4) \( n_{VM} = 250/105.83 = 2.36 \); \( n_{T} = 250/113.14 = 2.21 \).

Answer: 96.6 / −16.6 MPa; \( \sigma' = 105.8 \), \( \sigma_{Tresca} = 113.1 \) MPa; \( n = 2.36 \) or \( 2.21 \). Sanity check: Tresca is 6.9% higher in stress and correspondingly lower in factor of safety, inside the expected 0–15% band. If your two theories ever disagree by more than about 15%, the arithmetic is wrong, not the physics.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

When the concentration counts

Problem. A ductile steel plate with \( S_y = 250\ \mathrm{MPa} \) carries a nominal tensile stress of 40 MPa through a section containing a hole with \( K_t = 2.5 \). Find: the peak local stress, and state whether the part yields (a) under a static load and (b) if the same plate were a brittle casting.

Solution. (1) Peak: \( \sigma_{max} = K_t\sigma_{nom} = 2.5\times40 = 100\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (2) Static, ductile: 100 MPa is below \( S_y = 250 \), so no yielding; and even had it exceeded yield locally, the ductile material would redistribute rather than fracture. (3) Brittle casting: no redistribution is available, so the 100 MPa peak is the stress that must be compared against the material's (much lower) tensile capacity, and \( K_t \) cannot be set aside.

Answer: 100 MPa peak; safe statically in ductile steel; \( K_t \) governs in a brittle casting. Sanity check: the difference is not in the stress — it is 100 MPa in both — but in whether the material can shed the peak. That single distinction is why cast housings crack at fillets that would be unremarkable in a forging.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Shear yield is not tensile yield

Problem. A pin is loaded in pure shear. The material has \( S_y = 250\ \mathrm{MPa} \) in tension. Find: the shear yield strength by the von Mises criterion, and the factor of safety if the pin carries \( 80\ \mathrm{MPa} \) of shear.

Solution. (1) For pure shear, \( \sigma_x=\sigma_y=0 \), so \( \sigma' = \sqrt{3}\,\tau \). Yielding begins when \( \sqrt{3}\,\tau = S_y \), giving \( S_{sy} = S_y/\sqrt{3} = 0.577\times250 = 144.3\ \mathrm{MPa} \). (2) \( n = 144.3/80 = 1.80 \).

Answer: \( S_{sy} = 144.3\ \mathrm{MPa} \), \( n = 1.80 \). Sanity check: comparing the 80 MPa shear directly against the 250 MPa tensile yield would have reported \( n = 3.13 \) — optimistic by exactly \( \sqrt{3} \), and the single most common static-design error in the field. A pin "with a factor of three" can in fact be sitting at 1.8.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

For the same stress state, which statement about the two static failure theories is always true for a ductile metal?

Tresca is the more conservative theory everywhere, so its equivalent stress is never below the von Mises value, and von Mises therefore never reports the larger number. The two agree only in specific states — uniaxial tension being one — and differ by up to about 15% in pure shear, which is the largest gap. The relationship is geometric, set by the shapes of the two yield surfaces, so it does not depend on which ductile metal is involved.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

A pure shear stress \( \tau = 50\ \mathrm{MPa} \) acts alone. What is the von Mises equivalent stress?

With both normal stresses zero the formula reduces to \( \sigma' = \sqrt{3\tau^2} = \sqrt{3}\,\tau = 1.732\times50 = 86.6\ \mathrm{MPa} \). Answering 50 MPa treats shear as if it were directly comparable with a tensile strength, the error this lesson names explicitly. The 100 MPa answer doubles the shear, which is the Tresca result for this state and so belongs to the other theory. The 28.9 MPa answer divides by \( \sqrt{3} \) instead of multiplying, inverting the relation.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

Under a static load on a ductile steel part, why is the stress-concentration factor \( K_t \) commonly set aside?

A ductile material yields locally at the notch, which redistributes load into the surrounding material and blunts the peak — the part tolerates the concentration rather than fracturing at it. \( K_t \) is a purely geometric factor and is routinely 2 to 3, nowhere near unity. The permission has nothing to do with load magnitude, and it is withdrawn entirely under fatigue or in brittle materials, where no redistribution mechanism exists. \( K_t \) applies to any stress type.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

In a plane-stress state with principals \( 96.6 \) and \( -16.6\ \mathrm{MPa} \), what is the third principal stress used in the Tresca calculation?

Plane stress means the out-of-plane principal stress is zero, and it must still be included when identifying the maximum and minimum for Tresca. Here the set is \( \{96.6,\,0,\,-16.6\} \), so the Tresca stress is \( 96.6-(-16.6)=113.2\ \mathrm{MPa} \). Forgetting the zero is harmless when both in-plane principals straddle zero, as here, but becomes a real error when both are the same sign — in that case the zero is the minimum and governs the answer.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A designer reports "the factor of safety is 2.2" without naming a failure theory. What is the most accurate criticism?

The same stress state produced 2.36 by von Mises and 2.21 by Tresca in this lesson's worked example, so a bare factor of safety cannot be checked or compared against another engineer's number. The criticism is one of completeness, not of validity — the figure is perfectly meaningful once the theory is named. Nothing here establishes a minimum permitted value, and the claim that the theory does not matter is contradicted by the two results.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Which combination makes it mandatory to apply the stress-concentration factor in a static strength check?

A brittle material cannot yield locally, so the geometric peak is the stress that actually acts and \( K_t \) must be carried through. Ductile materials under steady load redistribute the peak, which is the standard permission to set \( K_t \) aside; the rate of load application does not change that, since the mechanism is plasticity rather than strain rate. Notch size does not grant an exemption — a small sharp notch typically produces a higher \( K_t \), not a lower one.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

Applying von Mises to a state with \( \sigma_x = 120\ \mathrm{MPa} \), \( \sigma_y = 0 \), \( \tau_{xy} = 0 \) gives an equivalent stress of

Uniaxial tension is the state the tension test itself produces, and any sound failure theory must return the applied stress unchanged there: \( \sqrt{120^2} = 120\ \mathrm{MPa} \). This is the sanity check to run on any failure-theory formula you are unsure you have remembered correctly — feed it uniaxial tension and see whether it hands the number back. The other options apply spurious factors of \( \sqrt{3} \) or one-half that have no term to attach to in this state.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

The shear yield strength of a ductile steel with \( S_y = 300\ \mathrm{MPa} \), by the von Mises criterion, is approximately

Von Mises gives \( S_{sy}=0.577\,S_y = 0.577\times300 = 173\ \mathrm{MPa} \). Using 300 MPa compares a shear stress against a tensile strength, the error worth roughly \( \sqrt{3} \) that this lesson flags. The 150 MPa answer is the Tresca prediction, \( S_y/2 \) — defensible if you say you used Tresca, but it is not the von Mises figure the question asks for. Multiplying by \( \sqrt{3} \) to reach 520 MPa moves in the wrong direction: shear capacity is always below tensile capacity.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — a pin with a factor of three that was really 1.8. A lifting-lug pin on a maintenance davit is checked during a routine review. The original calculation divided the tensile yield strength by the pin's shear stress and reported a factor of safety of 3.1, which passed without comment for years. The reviewer repeats the check through von Mises: shear yield is \( 0.577 S_y \), and the real factor is 1.8. Nothing about the pin changed and no arithmetic was wrong — the original simply compared a shear stress against a tensile strength, two quantities that are not comparable. The lug was not replaced, because 1.8 was still adequate for its duty, but every other shear-loaded item in the same calculation package was rechecked, and two of them were not adequate. The lesson is that an error of kind propagates silently through a document in a way an error of arithmetic rarely does.
