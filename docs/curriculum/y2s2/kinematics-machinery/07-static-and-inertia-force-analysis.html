Static and inertia force analysis

Force transmission through mechanisms; free-body chains under motion. Equivalent offset inertia forces. Bearing loads born from acceleration, not just weight.

Basics of DOM- Rigid Body, Inertia Force and Inertia Torque | D'Alembert's PrincipleRATHIN DUTTA

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Why do accelerating links load bearings beyond gravity?
  • What is an equivalent offset inertia force?

§1 · From motion to force, in one step

Lessons 2 and 3 delivered the kinematics: every link's velocity and acceleration for a given input. This lesson converts them into the numbers that size hardware — pin loads, bearing loads and the torque the motor must actually supply.

The bridge is d'Alembert's principle. A link with mass \( m \) whose centre of mass accelerates at \( \mathbf{a}_G \), while the link itself has angular acceleration \( \alpha \), can be treated as being in static equilibrium if two fictitious quantities are added to its free-body diagram: an inertia force \( -m\mathbf{a}_G \) at the centre of mass, and an inertia couple \( -I_G\alpha \). With those in place the whole apparatus of statics applies unchanged — sum forces to zero, sum moments to zero — on a machine that is plainly not static.

Key relations — d'Alembert and the offset force

\[ \sum\mathbf{F}-m\mathbf{a}_G=0,\qquad \sum M_G-I_G\alpha=0,\qquad e=\frac{I_G\alpha}{m\,a_G} \]

The force and the couple can be combined into a single equivalent offset inertia force of the same magnitude \( ma_G \), displaced a distance \( e \) from the centre of mass — one vector instead of two quantities.

Take a connecting rod of 0.8 kg with \( I_G = 0.0045\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \), whose centre of mass accelerates at 1200 m/s² while the rod's angular acceleration is 4000 rad/s². The inertia force is \( 0.8(1200) = 960 \) N and the inertia couple \( 0.0045(4000) = 18 \) N·m, so the equivalent single force is 960 N applied \( 18/960 = 18.75 \) mm off the centre of mass. That offset is not a detail: it is what decides how the 960 N divides between the two pins at the ends of the rod.

§2 · Static analysis: what the motor owes the load

Strip the inertia away first and ask a purely static question. The four-bar of Lessons 1 and 2 — ground 100, crank 40, coupler 120, rocker 90 mm — resists a 50 N·m torque at the rocker. What torque does the crank need?

The coupler is a two-force member: no load acts along its length, so it can only push or pull along its own line. Its force therefore produces a moment about \( O_4 \) of \( F\,r_4\sin\mu \), where \( \mu \) is the transmission angle. At \( \theta_2 = 60^\circ \), \( \mu = 46.38^\circ \), so

\[ F=\frac{T_4}{r_4\sin\mu}=\frac{50}{0.090\sin 46.38^\circ}=767.4\ \mathrm{N} \]

and the same force acting on the crank at A, at \( 60^\circ - 24.95^\circ = 35.05^\circ \) to it, needs a crank torque of \( 767.4(0.040)\sin 35.05^\circ = 17.63\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \).

Check that against power. Nothing stores or dissipates energy in an ideal linkage, so \( T_2\omega_2 = T_4\omega_4 \) and \( T_2 = 50(3.525)/10 = 17.63\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \). The two routes agree exactly, and they share no arithmetic — one is a moment balance through the coupler, the other a power balance using Lesson 2's velocity ratio. This is the third time in this course that an independent check has been available; it is worth the thirty seconds every time.

§3 · Why the transmission angle is a hardware cost

Read the coupler-force equation again: \( F = T_4/(r_4\sin\mu) \). The duty \( T_4 \) is fixed by the process, and \( r_4 \) by the geometry, so the force in the coupler is governed entirely by \( \sin\mu \) — and \( \mu \) sweeps through the cycle.

The figure plots it. At \( \theta_2=180^\circ \), \( \mu=82.3^\circ \) and the coupler carries 561 N. At \( \theta_2=0 \), \( \mu=29.0^\circ \) and it carries 1148 N — 2.05 times as much, for exactly the same useful output. Every pin, every bearing and the coupler section itself are sized by that 1148 N. Lesson 1 called a low transmission angle a grading matter; here is the invoice.

It also explains the fatigue duty. The load is not steady at some average: it cycles between 561 and 1148 N once per revolution, so the pins see a fluctuating load with a mean of about 850 N and an amplitude near 300 N, which is a Goodman problem, not a static one.

§4 · Adding the inertia, and reading the bearing load honestly

The full analysis superposes the two. Work link by link, starting from the link whose acceleration is fully known, and carry the pin forces along the chain:

  1. From the kinematics, get \( \mathbf{a}_G \) and \( \alpha \) for every link.
  2. Put the inertia force and couple on each link's free-body diagram.
  3. Solve the links in sequence, transferring the pin force from one to the next.
  4. The crank torque that emerges is the shaking torque; the resultant force on the frame is the shaking force, and it is what the foundation feels.

The scale of the inertia contribution is easy to underrate. A 0.5 kg piston at the 2500 m/s² of Lesson 3 delivers 1250 N through its pin at top dead centre with no gas load at all — larger than the entire static coupler force computed above, from a part that weighs half a kilogram. In fast machinery the inertia terms dominate and the static analysis is the small correction, which is the reverse of the intuition most people bring from statics.

Two practical consequences. First, bearing selection needs the peak load and its direction, both of which move through the cycle; a mean value understates the duty and hides the fact that the load reverses. Second, the shaking force is the reason machinery is bolted down and the reason balancing exists — and balancing is Lesson 10.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Acceleration analysis (Lesson 3) — supplies \( \mathbf{a}_G \) and \( \alpha \) for every link, without which no force can be computed.
  • Transmission angle (Lesson 1) — reappears here as the divisor that sets the coupler force.
  • Two-force members (Statics L3) — why the coupler's force acts along its own line.
  • Moment equilibrium (Statics L2) — applied to a moving machine via d'Alembert.
  • Fluctuating-load fatigue (Machine Design I L4) — the pin load cycles once per revolution, so it is a Goodman problem.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Crank torque, two independent ways

Problem. The four-bar of Lesson 2 — ground 100, crank 40, coupler 120, rocker 90 mm — is at \( \theta_2=60^\circ \), where \( \theta_3=24.95^\circ \), \( \theta_4=71.34^\circ \), \( \mu=46.38^\circ \) and \( \omega_4/\omega_2=0.3525 \). The rocker resists 50 N·m. Find the coupler force and the required crank torque, then check the torque by a power balance.

Solution. (1) The coupler is a two-force member, so its force acts along AB. Its moment about \( O_4 \) is \( F r_4\sin\mu \), which must equal 50 N·m:

\( F = \dfrac{50}{0.090\sin 46.38^\circ} = \dfrac{50}{0.090(0.72396)} = \dfrac{50}{0.065156} = 767.4\ \mathrm{N} \).

(2) The same force acts on the crank at A. The angle between the crank and the coupler is \( 60^\circ - 24.95^\circ = 35.05^\circ \), so

\( T_2 = F r_2\sin 35.05^\circ = 767.4(0.040)(0.57424) = 17.63\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \).

(3) Power check: an ideal linkage neither stores nor dissipates, so \( T_2\omega_2 = T_4\omega_4 \) and \( T_2 = 50(0.3525) = 17.63\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \).

Answer: coupler force 767.4 N, crank torque 17.63 N·m, confirmed by power. Sanity check: the two routes agree to four figures while sharing no arithmetic — one is a moment balance through the coupler geometry, the other uses only the velocity ratio from Lesson 2. Note also that the crank torque is far smaller than the rocker torque, by exactly the velocity ratio, which is what a speed increase from crank to rocker must cost in torque.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

What the worst transmission angle costs

Problem. The same linkage holds the same 50 N·m at the rocker through a whole revolution. Using \( \mu_{\min}=28.955^\circ \) at \( \theta_2=0 \) and \( \mu_{\max}=82.284^\circ \) at \( \theta_2=180^\circ \), find the largest and smallest coupler forces, their ratio, and the mean and amplitude of the pin load.

Solution. (1) At \( \mu_{\min} \): \( F = \dfrac{50}{0.090\sin 28.955^\circ} = \dfrac{50}{0.090(0.48423)} = 1147.6\ \mathrm{N} \).

(2) At \( \mu_{\max} \): \( F = \dfrac{50}{0.090\sin 82.284^\circ} = \dfrac{50}{0.090(0.99098)} = 560.6\ \mathrm{N} \).

(3) Ratio \( = 1147.6/560.6 = 2.05 \).

(4) Mean \( = (1147.6+560.6)/2 = 854.1\ \mathrm{N} \); amplitude \( = (1147.6-560.6)/2 = 293.5\ \mathrm{N} \).

Answer: 1147.6 N and 560.6 N, a factor of 2.05; the pins see \( 854 \pm 294 \) N. Sanity check: the output has not changed at any point in the cycle — the machine does exactly the same work — yet the hardware must be sized for twice the force at one position. That is the whole practical content of the transmission angle. And because the load cycles once per revolution, the pins carry a fluctuating load, so the bushes are a fatigue and fretting problem rather than a static bearing-pressure one.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Inertia force, inertia couple, and the offset (with a US customary check)

Problem. A connecting rod has mass 0.8 kg and \( I_G = 0.0045\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \). At the instant considered its centre of mass accelerates at 1200 m/s² and the rod's angular acceleration is 4000 rad/s². Find the inertia force, the inertia couple and the offset of the equivalent single force. Then express the force in pounds-force, and compare it with the 1250 N a 0.5 kg piston delivers at 2500 m/s².

Solution. (1) Inertia force \( = m a_G = 0.8(1200) = 960\ \mathrm{N} \), directed opposite to \( \mathbf{a}_G \).

(2) Inertia couple \( = I_G\alpha = 0.0045(4000) = 18.0\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \), opposing \( \alpha \).

(3) Offset \( e = \dfrac{I_G\alpha}{m a_G} = \dfrac{18.0}{960} = 0.01875\ \mathrm{m} = 18.75\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(4) \( 960\ \mathrm{N} \div 4.448 = 215.8\ \mathrm{lbf} \).

(5) The piston delivers \( 0.5(2500) = 1250\ \mathrm{N} \), 30% more than the rod, from a part of 0.5 kg against the rod's 0.8 kg.

Answer: 960 N (215.8 lbf), 18.0 N·m, offset 18.75 mm; the lighter piston contributes the larger force.

Sanity check: the comparison is the useful part. Mass is a poor guide to inertia loading because acceleration varies so much more than mass does across a mechanism — the piston is 60% of the rod's mass and sees more than twice its acceleration. The offset also deserves a second look: 18.75 mm on a rod a few hundred millimetres long is small, but it is what splits the 960 N unequally between the two pins, and ignoring it puts the wrong number into each bearing.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

d'Alembert's principle allows a moving link to be analysed by statics provided that

Adding \( -m\mathbf{a}_G \) at the centre of mass and \( -I_G\alpha \) about it converts the dynamic problem into an equilibrium problem that the usual force and moment sums solve. Speed is irrelevant — the faster the machine, the larger those added terms and the more they dominate. Friction is a separate modelling choice, and the link is emphatically not in equilibrium; the principle is a bookkeeping device that lets it be treated as if it were.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

The equivalent offset inertia force replaces a force and a couple with a single force displaced by

Moving a force sideways by \( e \) generates a couple \( F e \), so to reproduce \( I_G\alpha \) with a force \( ma_G \), the offset must be \( e = I_G\alpha/(ma_G) \) — 18.75 mm in the worked rod. The point of the substitution is that a single displaced vector can be carried directly into a graphical or free-body solution, where a separate couple is easy to lose.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A four-bar coupler carries \( F = T_4/(r_4\sin\mu) \). Halving \( \sin\mu \) at constant output torque

\( \sin\mu \) is in the denominator, so halving it doubles the force in the coupler and in both its pins — while the useful output is unchanged. In the worked linkage that is very nearly what happens between the best and worst positions: 561 N against 1148 N, a factor of 2.05. Output power is set by the load and the speed and is untouched by geometry.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

In an ideal frictionless linkage, the crank torque required for an output torque \( T_4 \) is

Power in equals power out, so \( T_2\omega_2 = T_4\omega_4 \) and \( T_2 = T_4\,\omega_4/\omega_2 \) — \( 50(0.3525) = 17.63 \) N·m for the worked case. Because the ratio varies through the cycle, so does the required crank torque, which is precisely the fluctuating torque a flywheel is fitted to smooth.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A 0.5 kg piston accelerating at 2500 m/s² delivers a pin force of

\( F = ma = 0.5(2500) = 1250 \) N, from inertia alone and before any gas load is added. It is larger than the entire static coupler force in the worked four-bar, from a part weighing half a kilogram — the standard demonstration that in fast machinery the inertia terms dominate and the static analysis is the correction.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

An ideal four-bar drives a constant output torque at a constant input speed. The torque the crank must supply

\( T_2 = T_4\,\omega_4/\omega_2 \), and Lesson 2 showed that ratio sweeping from 0.0014 to 0.838 in this linkage. A constant load therefore demands a crank torque that rises and falls once per revolution even with a perfectly steady motor and no friction. That fluctuation, together with the inertia terms, is what a flywheel is fitted to absorb — Lesson 9.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

The shaking force of a mechanism is

It is the resultant of all the inertia effects, delivered into the frame through the bearings, and it is what a foundation and its holding-down bolts actually feel. It is the quantity balancing exists to reduce (Lesson 10). The shaking torque is its rotational partner, and is what a flywheel smooths (Lesson 9).

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A bearing is selected using the average of the load over the cycle rather than the peak. The consequence is

Bearing capacity is set by peak contact pressure, and life by the load spectrum, so averaging discards exactly the information that matters — in the worked linkage the peak is 1148 N against a mean of 854 N, 34% higher. Averaging also hides the reversal: a load that swings between two values fatigues and frets a bush in a way a steady load does not.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the bushes that only wore on one machine. Two identical conveyor drives, same duty and same drive motor, used four-bar linkages built by different fabricators to the same nominal stroke. One ran for years on its original pins. The other consumed coupler bushes at roughly three times the rate, and the maintenance record blamed lubrication.

Measurement of the two linkages found link lengths within a few millimetres of each other but in a different combination, and that was enough. With the output torque fixed by the process, the coupler force is \( F = T_4/(r_4\sin\mu) \), so the peak load through the pins is set by the worst transmission angle in the cycle. Working the geometry of the poor machine gave \( \mu_{\min}=29.0^\circ \) against the good machine's much healthier figure: at 50 N·m of rocker torque, that is a peak coupler force of 1148 N instead of 561 N, once every revolution, for the life of the machine. Nothing was wrong with the lubricant or the bush material; the geometry was asking for twice the load.

Two practices follow. A linkage's bearing duty is a geometry calculation, not a load calculation: the same output torque produces very different pin forces depending on proportions, and the number to compute is the peak over the cycle. And when two nominally identical machines wear at different rates, comparing the link lengths costs nothing and settles whether the difference is in the maintenance or in the drawing.
