Cams

Follower motion laws — uniform, harmonic, cycloidal — and their acceleration signatures. Profile synthesis and pressure angle. Why cam wear patterns encode their motion law. Filling-machine cams as the local example.

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Why does cycloidal motion beat harmonic for high-speed cams?
  • What does pressure angle limit in a cam-follower?

§1 · The cam is the motion, written down

A linkage produces the motion its proportions allow. A cam produces the motion you specify: its profile is the displacement schedule, machined into steel. That is the trade — complete freedom over the follower's motion, paid for with a higher pair, line contact, contact stress and wear, in place of the linkage's comfortable pins.

Design starts from the displacement diagram: follower lift against cam angle, laid out as a sequence of rises, dwells and returns that the machine's timing demands. The dwells are usually why the cam was chosen at all — no linkage holds an output perfectly still for a third of a revolution. What has to be decided is what happens during a rise, and that choice is called the motion law.

The lift is a given: 20 mm here, over a rise of \( \beta = 90^\circ \), at 300 rev/min so \( \omega = 31.42 \) rad/s. Every motion law delivers the same 20 mm over the same 90°. They differ entirely in the derivatives, and the derivatives are what break the machine.

§2 · Three laws, and what their derivatives say

Uniform (constant-velocity) motion is the naive answer: divide the lift evenly across the rise, \( v = h\omega/\beta = 0.400 \) m/s throughout. Its acceleration is zero in the middle and infinite at both ends, where the follower is asked to reach full speed instantly. It exists in practice only with its corners rounded, which means it is not really this law at all.

Simple harmonic motion, \( s=\tfrac{h}{2}\left(1-\cos\frac{\pi\theta}{\beta}\right) \), is the sine wave's projection and is smooth in the middle. Cycloidal motion, \( s=h\left(\frac{\theta}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sin\frac{2\pi\theta}{\beta}\right) \), is a constant-magnitude acceleration vector rotating at constant speed, projected on the axis.

Key relations — peak velocity and acceleration

\[ \text{SHM:}\quad v_{\max}=\frac{\pi h\omega}{2\beta},\qquad a_{\max}=\frac{\pi^2h\omega^2}{2\beta^2} \]

\[ \text{Cycloidal:}\quad v_{\max}=\frac{2h\omega}{\beta},\qquad a_{\max}=\frac{2\pi h\omega^2}{\beta^2} \]

Both cycloidal peaks are exactly \( 4/\pi = 1.273 \) times the harmonic ones, for any lift, any rise angle and any speed.

For the worked cam: harmonic gives 0.628 m/s and 39.48 m/s²; cycloidal gives 0.800 m/s and 50.26 m/s². On peak numbers alone the harmonic law wins, and at low speed it is chosen for exactly that reason.

§3 · Why cycloidal wins anyway, and what wear tells you

Look at the ends of the rise rather than the middle. Harmonic acceleration begins at \( +39.48 \) m/s² the instant the rise starts — a step, from the zero of the preceding dwell. A step in acceleration is infinite jerk, \( da/dt \). No real follower train is infinitely stiff, so it responds to that step by ringing: the follower, its spring and its linkage form a spring-mass system, and a step excites every mode it has. The result is vibration, noise, and contact stress well above the value the steady analysis predicted.

Cycloidal acceleration leaves zero smoothly and returns to zero smoothly. Its jerk is finite everywhere. It pays 27% more peak acceleration — a quantity that can simply be designed for — to eliminate a quantity that cannot. Above a few hundred rev/min, that is not a close decision.

The exchange shows in wear. A cam cut to a harmonic law and run fast wears in two narrow bands at the ends of the rise, where the step lands and the follower hammers. A cycloidal cam of the same duty wears more evenly and most at mid-rise, where its acceleration and hence its contact force genuinely peak. A worn cam is a record of its own acceleration history, and reading that record is a legitimate diagnostic: bands at the transitions mean the motion law, or its manufacture, has a discontinuity in it.

§4 · Pressure angle, base circle, and keeping the follower on the cam

The cam pushes the follower along the contact normal, which is not the direction the follower moves. The angle between them is the pressure angle \( \phi \), and it does to a cam what the transmission angle does to a linkage.

Key relation — pressure angle, radial roller follower

\[ \tan\phi=\frac{ds/d\theta}{R_p+s} \]

\( R_p \) is the prime-circle radius and \( s \) the lift at that instant. Practice keeps \( \phi \le 30^\circ \) on a translating follower.

The numerator belongs to the motion law and the denominator to the cam's size. Harmonic motion on this cam has \( ds/d\theta = \pi h/2\beta = 20 \) mm/rad at mid-rise, so with \( R_p = 40 \) mm and \( s = 10 \) mm, \( \phi = 21.8^\circ \) — comfortable. Shrink the prime circle to 25 mm and \( \phi = 29.7^\circ \), on the limit. Grow it to 60 mm and \( \phi = 15.9^\circ \). The base circle is the pressure-angle control, and a cam that is too small is the usual cause of a cam that jams. Cycloidal motion, with its steeper mid-rise \( ds/d\theta = 2h/\beta = 25.5 \) mm/rad, gives \( \phi = 27.0^\circ \) on the same 40 mm prime circle — the same trade as before, appearing in a second place.

One more check closes the design. The cam can only push; the follower is returned by a spring, and the spring must hold contact through the whole negative-acceleration phase or the follower lifts off and lands again — follower jump, which destroys cams. The requirement is \( F_{\text{spring}} \ge m\,a_{\max} \) with margin. A 1.2 kg follower on the cycloidal law needs \( 1.2 \times 50.26 = 60.3 \) N at the worst point, so a spring giving about 78 N there carries a 30% margin. Note that the harmonic law would have needed only 47.4 N: the 27% acceleration penalty is paid twice, once in contact stress and once in the spring that has to be carried against it for the whole life of the machine.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Higher pairs (Lesson 1) — the cam-follower contact removes one freedom, which is what lets one input position a follower arbitrarily.
  • Acceleration and inertia force (Lesson 3) — the follower's acceleration is what the spring and the contact stress have to carry.
  • Simple harmonic motion (Physics I) — the projection argument behind the harmonic law and its derivatives.
  • Differentiation of a specified displacement (Mathematics I) — every result here is \( s(\theta) \) differentiated once and twice.
  • Transmission angle (Lesson 1) — the pressure angle is the same idea for a higher pair.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Harmonic against cycloidal, same duty

Problem. A cam lifts a follower 20 mm over a 90° rise and runs at 300 rev/min. Find the peak velocity and peak acceleration for simple harmonic motion and for cycloidal motion, and state the ratio between them.

Solution. \( \omega = 2\pi(300)/60 = 31.416 \) rad/s, \( \beta = 90^\circ = \pi/2 = 1.5708 \) rad, \( h = 0.020 \) m.

(1) SHM: \( v_{\max} = \frac{\pi h\omega}{2\beta} = \frac{\pi(0.020)(31.416)}{2(1.5708)} = \frac{1.9739}{3.1416} = 0.628\ \mathrm{m/s} \).

(2) SHM: \( a_{\max} = \frac{\pi^2h\omega^2}{2\beta^2} = \frac{9.8696(0.020)(987.0)}{2(2.4674)} = \frac{194.82}{4.9348} = 39.48\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \).

(3) Cycloidal: \( v_{\max} = \frac{2h\omega}{\beta} = \frac{2(0.020)(31.416)}{1.5708} = 0.800\ \mathrm{m/s} \).

(4) Cycloidal: \( a_{\max} = \frac{2\pi h\omega^2}{\beta^2} = \frac{6.2832(0.020)(987.0)}{2.4674} = \frac{124.02}{2.4674} = 50.26\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \).

Answer: SHM 0.628 m/s and 39.48 m/s²; cycloidal 0.800 m/s and 50.26 m/s²; both cycloidal peaks are \( 4/\pi = 1.273 \) times the harmonic ones. Sanity check: the ratio is exactly \( 4/\pi \) in both velocity and acceleration, and it is independent of \( h \), \( \beta \) and \( \omega \) — the constants cancel. That is worth remembering as a design fact: choosing cycloidal over harmonic always costs 27%, never more, and buys finite jerk. For reference, uniform motion would give the lowest peak velocity of all, \( h\omega/\beta = 0.400 \) m/s, and infinite acceleration at both ends.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Pressure angle and the base circle

Problem. The cam above uses a radial translating roller follower on a prime circle of radius \( R_p = 40 \) mm, with harmonic motion. Find the pressure angle at mid-rise, where \( ds/d\theta \) is greatest and the lift is \( h/2 \). Then find what \( R_p = 25 \) mm and \( R_p = 60 \) mm would give, and say which designs are acceptable.

Solution. For SHM the slope peaks at mid-rise: \( ds/d\theta = \frac{\pi h}{2\beta} = \frac{\pi(20)}{2(1.5708)} = 20.0\ \mathrm{mm/rad} \), and there \( s = h/2 = 10 \) mm.

(1) \( R_p = 40 \): \( \tan\phi = \frac{20.0}{40+10} = 0.4000 \), so \( \phi = 21.80^\circ \) ✓.

(2) \( R_p = 25 \): \( \tan\phi = \frac{20.0}{25+10} = 0.5714 \), so \( \phi = 29.74^\circ \) — on the 30° limit.

(3) \( R_p = 60 \): \( \tan\phi = \frac{20.0}{60+10} = 0.2857 \), so \( \phi = 15.95^\circ \) ✓.

Answer: 21.8°, 29.7° and 16.0°; the 40 and 60 mm cams are comfortable and the 25 mm cam is at the limit with nothing in hand. Sanity check: the motion law appears only in the numerator and the cam size only in the denominator, so the two are independent controls. Switching this cam to cycloidal motion raises the slope to \( 2h/\beta = 25.5 \) mm/rad and, on the 40 mm prime circle, the pressure angle to 27.0° — still acceptable, but the 27% penalty has now appeared in a second place. The general fix for a high pressure angle is a bigger cam, which is also the general reason cams are larger than a first sketch suggests.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Spring sizing against follower jump (with a US customary check)

Problem. The follower assembly on the cycloidal cam weighs 1.2 kg. Find the spring force needed at the worst point to keep contact, apply a 30% margin, and convert the result to pounds-force. Then state what happens if the spring is 40 N instead.

Solution. (1) The cam can only push, so the spring alone supplies the force during the negative-acceleration phase. The requirement is \( F \ge m\,a_{\max} = 1.2(50.26) = 60.3\ \mathrm{N} \).

(2) With a 30% margin, \( F = 1.3(60.3) = 78.4\ \mathrm{N} \).

(3) \( 78.4\ \mathrm{N} \times \frac{1\ \mathrm{lbf}}{4.448\ \mathrm{N}} = 17.6\ \mathrm{lbf} \).

(4) With 40 N the spring can decelerate only \( a = 40/1.2 = 33.3\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \), well short of the 50.26 the cam demands. The follower leaves the cam surface, flies for part of the cycle, and lands on the profile with an impact the design never accounted for.

Answer: 78.4 N (17.6 lbf); a 40 N spring allows follower jump. Sanity check: the same follower on the harmonic law would need only \( 1.2(39.48) = 47.4 \) N before margin, so the 40 N spring would still be marginal but far closer. Two consequences worth naming: the jump condition scales as \( \omega^2 \), so a cam that is safe at 300 rev/min is not automatically safe at 400; and the spring is not a return device that can be sized by feel — it is the component holding a higher pair together.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

Cycloidal motion is preferred over simple harmonic for high-speed cams because

Cycloidal acceleration starts and ends at zero, so there is no step and the jerk stays finite. Harmonic acceleration steps from zero to its peak the instant the rise begins, which is infinite jerk and excites every mode of the follower train. Cycloidal is worse on both peaks — velocity and acceleration are each \( 4/\pi \) times the harmonic values — and needs a larger cam for the same pressure angle. It wins on the derivative that cannot be designed around.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

For the same lift, rise angle and speed, the cycloidal peak acceleration exceeds the harmonic by a factor of

\( \frac{2\pi h\omega^2/\beta^2}{\pi^2h\omega^2/2\beta^2} = \frac{4}{\pi} = 1.273 \). Every parameter cancels, so the 27% penalty is a property of the two motion laws and nothing else — it does not shrink at low lift or long rise. The same factor governs the peak velocities.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

Uniform (constant-velocity) follower motion is unusable in its pure form because

Constant velocity means the follower must reach full speed instantly at the start and stop instantly at the end: an acceleration step of infinite height, and therefore an infinite inertia force. In practice the transitions are blended with arcs or a smooth law, which is really a different motion law with a constant-velocity segment in the middle. Its peak velocity is actually the lowest of the three laws, at \( h\omega/\beta \).

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

A cam has a pressure angle of 34° at mid-rise. The most direct fix is to

\( \tan\phi = (ds/d\theta)/(R_p+s) \): the cam's size is the denominator, so enlarging the prime circle lowers the angle directly — the worked cam drops from 29.7° to 16.0° going from a 25 to a 60 mm prime circle. Speed does not appear in the formula at all. A flat-faced follower does sidestep the pressure angle, but it changes the contact geometry and brings its own curvature constraint, so it is a redesign rather than the direct fix.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Follower jump occurs when

A cam can only push. Through the negative-acceleration phase the spring is the only thing decelerating the follower, so it must supply at least \( ma_{\max} \) — 60.3 N for a 1.2 kg follower at 50.26 m/s². Below that the follower separates and returns as an impact. Because the requirement scales with \( \omega^2 \), jump appears when a machine is sped up, never when it is slowed down.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A cam is found worn in two narrow bands at the start and end of its rise, with the mid-rise region comparatively unmarked. This points to

Wear follows contact force, and contact force follows acceleration. Bands at the transitions rather than at mid-rise say the load is spiking where the acceleration steps — the signature of a harmonic or uniform law run too fast, or of a manufacturing discontinuity at the blend. A pressure-angle problem or excessive spring force would show as heavier, broader wear where the pressure angle or the lift peaks, which is mid-rise.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

The same cam and follower are run at 400 rev/min instead of 300. The spring force needed to prevent jump increases by a factor of about

Peak acceleration goes as \( \omega^2 \), so the factor is \( (400/300)^2 = 1.78 \). The 60.3 N requirement becomes 107 N, and the 78.4 N spring chosen with a 30% margin at 300 rev/min is now below the bare minimum. Speed increases on cam-driven machinery have to be checked against the jump condition before anything else.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

The dwell is usually the reason a cam is chosen rather than a linkage because

A linkage output passes through zero velocity instantaneously at a toggle, but it does not stay there; holding a follower genuinely still for a specified arc of the cycle is what a cam does and a four-bar cannot. The price is the higher pair — line contact, high contact stress and wear — where the linkage enjoys surface contact at every joint.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the filling machine that got faster. A rotary filling machine used a cam to raise each container against the filling head, hold it through the fill, and lower it. Throughput was raised by running the cam shaft from 300 to 400 rev/min, with no other change: the fill volume was corrected in software and the machine passed its trials. Within weeks the cam began showing bright bands and pitting at the two ends of the rise, and containers were occasionally being struck rather than lifted.

Two things changed at once, and both scale as \( \omega^2 \). The peak follower acceleration on this cam's cycloidal rise, \( 2\pi h\omega^2/\beta^2 \), rose by \( (400/300)^2 = 1.78 \) — from 50.3 to 89.4 m/s² — so the 1.2 kg follower assembly now needed \( 1.2 \times 89.4 = 107 \) N to stay on the cam, against a spring chosen to deliver about 78 N. The follower was leaving the profile during the deceleration and landing on it, which is precisely what produces localised bands rather than distributed wear, and what turns a controlled lift into an impact.

Two practices follow. A cam machine's speed is limited by its spring at least as often as by its motor, and the jump check — \( F_{\text{spring}} \ge ma_{\max} \) with margin — must be repeated at the new speed before a rate increase is accepted. And wear patterns are evidence: bands at the transitions of a rise say the follower is losing and regaining contact there, which is a different failure from the even wear of an honestly loaded cam and calls for a different fix.
