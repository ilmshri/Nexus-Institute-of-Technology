Position and velocity analysis

Graphical and analytical position solutions. Velocity by instant centers and by vector loops. Reading mechanism speed ratios without simulation.

Instantaneous Centers of Rotation and Kennedy's TheoremENGINEERING EDUCATOR ACADEMY

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Locate instant centers of a four-bar: what do they give you instantly?
  • Why does a mechanism's speed ratio vary through its cycle?

§1 · Closing the loop

A mechanism with \( M=1 \) has one input, so every other position must follow from it. The machinery for extracting them is one equation: go round the closed chain and arrive back where you started. Treat each link as a vector and the four-bar becomes

Key relation — vector loop closure

\[ r_2e^{i\theta_2} + r_3e^{i\theta_3} - r_4e^{i\theta_4} - r_1 = 0 \]

Real and imaginary parts give two scalar equations. With \( r_1 \ldots r_4 \) and the input \( \theta_2 \) known, they determine \( \theta_3 \) and \( \theta_4 \).

Two solutions satisfy those equations — the open and crossed circuits, mirror images across the line from the crank pin to the far pivot. A physical linkage is assembled into one of them and stays there; it cannot jump circuits without being taken apart. Choosing the wrong root is the standard way a mechanism analysis produces a mechanism nobody built.

Solving by hand is easier as geometry than as algebra. Locate the crank pin A from \( \theta_2 \), measure the distance \( d \) from A to the fixed pivot \( O_4 \), and then A, \( O_4 \) and the coupler pin B form a triangle whose three sides are all known: \( d \), \( r_3 \) and \( r_4 \). Two applications of the cosine rule finish it. At \( \theta_2 = 60^\circ \) on the linkage in the figure, A is at (20.00, 34.64) mm, \( d = 87.18 \) mm, and the triangle gives \( \theta_3 = 24.95^\circ \) and \( \theta_4 = 71.34^\circ \). If \( d \) ever falls outside \( |r_3-r_4| \) to \( r_3+r_4 \), no triangle exists and the linkage cannot be assembled at that crank angle — which is the Grashof condition of Lesson 1 arriving again, this time as a broken square root.

§2 · Velocity, by differentiating the loop

Differentiate the closure equation once with respect to time. The link lengths are constant, so only the angles move, and the result is linear in the unknown angular velocities — which is why velocity analysis is always easier than position analysis.

Key relation — four-bar angular velocities

\[ \omega_3=\frac{r_2\omega_2\sin(\theta_4-\theta_2)}{r_3\sin(\theta_3-\theta_4)}, \qquad \omega_4=\frac{r_2\omega_2\sin(\theta_2-\theta_3)}{r_4\sin(\theta_4-\theta_3)} \]

Both are proportional to \( \omega_2 \): double the input speed and every velocity in the mechanism doubles, while the geometry is untouched.

With \( \omega_2 = 10 \) rad/s the linkage gives \( \omega_3 = -0.905 \) rad/s and \( \omega_4 = 3.525 \) rad/s. The sign on \( \omega_3 \) is information, not an error: the coupler is rotating the opposite way to the crank at this instant. The rocker turns at 0.3525 of the crank speed — at this instant only.

§3 · Instant centres, and the audit they provide

Any two rigid bodies in plane motion have, at each instant, one point where their velocities are equal: the instant centre. If one body is the ground, the instant centre is the point of the moving body that is momentarily at rest, and the body is turning about it as if pinned there. A mechanism of \( N \) links has \( N(N-1)/2 \) of them — six for a four-bar.

Four are free: the four pin joints are instant centres by inspection (\( I_{12}, I_{23}, I_{34}, I_{14} \)). The remaining two come from the Aronhold-Kennedy theorem: the three instant centres shared by any three bodies lie on one straight line. Apply it to links 1, 2 and 4 and \( I_{24} \) must lie on the line \( I_{12}I_{14} \) — the ground line — and also on \( I_{23}I_{34} \), the coupler line. Extend both and they cross.

\( I_{24} \) is the point that links 2 and 4 share, so it has the same velocity considered as a point of either link. As a point of link 2 that velocity is \( \omega_2 \cdot |O_2I_{24}| \); as a point of link 4 it is \( \omega_4 \cdot |O_4I_{24}| \). Equate them:

Key relation — velocity ratio from an instant centre

\[ \frac{\omega_4}{\omega_2}=\frac{|O_2 I_{24}|}{|O_4 I_{24}|} \]

A ratio of two distances measured off a drawing — no trigonometry, no calculus.

In the figure the coupler line extended crosses the ground line 54.44 mm beyond \( O_2 \), so \( |O_2I_{24}| = 54.44 \) and \( |O_4I_{24}| = 154.44 \) mm. Their ratio is 0.3525, which is \( \omega_4/\omega_2 \) to four decimal places. That agreement is what makes the construction worth knowing: it is an independent check on an analytical result, obtainable in seconds, and the two methods share no arithmetic.

§4 · Why the speed ratio will not hold still

A gear pair has one ratio, fixed by tooth counts. A linkage does not, and the instant-centre picture shows why: \( I_{24} \) is not a fixed point. As the crank turns, the coupler line sweeps and the crossing point slides along the ground line, so the ratio of the two distances — and therefore the output speed — changes continuously. Sweeping the linkage in the figure through a full revolution, \( |\omega_4/\omega_2| \) ranges from 0.0014 at \( \theta_2 = 239^\circ \) to 0.838 at \( 342^\circ \).

The near-zero value is a physical event, not a numerical accident: it is the rocker at the end of its swing, momentarily stopped and about to reverse. Those toggle positions occur when the crank and coupler fall into one line, and their locations follow from the same cosine rule used for position. For this linkage the rocker turns between \( \theta_4 = 49.46^\circ \) and \( 114.62^\circ \) — a swing of 65.17°, which is the specification a designer actually cares about. Between those limits the output slows to a stop and accelerates back, and a mechanism whose output velocity is changing is a mechanism generating inertia forces. That is Lesson 3.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Mobility and Grashof (Lesson 1) — the assembly limit reappears here as a cosine rule with no solution.
  • Rigid-body velocity (Dynamics L4) — \( \mathbf{v}_B = \mathbf{v}_A + \boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}_{B/A} \), the relation the loop derivative encodes.
  • Cosine rule (Mathematics I) — the whole position solution, applied twice.
  • Complex-number rotation (Mathematics II) — \( e^{i\theta} \) as a rotation operator, which is what makes the loop equation compact.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Position of a four-bar by the cosine rule

Problem. Ground 100 mm, crank 40 mm, coupler 120 mm, rocker 90 mm, with the crank at \( \theta_2 = 60^\circ \) from the ground line. Locate the crank pin A, find the distance \( d \) from A to the fixed pivot \( O_4 \), and solve the open circuit for \( \theta_3 \) and \( \theta_4 \).

Solution. (1) \( A = (40\cos 60^\circ,\ 40\sin 60^\circ) = (20.00,\ 34.64)\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(2) \( d = \sqrt{(100-20.00)^2+(0-34.64)^2} = \sqrt{6400+1200} = 87.18\ \mathrm{mm} \). Check assembly: \( |120-90| = 30 \le 87.18 \le 210 \) ✓.

(3) In triangle \( AO_4B \), the angle at \( O_4 \) between \( O_4A \) and \( O_4B \) is \( \gamma \), with \( \cos\gamma = \frac{d^2+r_4^2-r_3^2}{2dr_4} = \frac{7600+8100-14400}{2(87.18)(90)} = 0.08295 \), so \( \gamma = 85.24^\circ \).

(4) The direction from \( O_4 \) to A is \( \operatorname{atan2}(34.64,\ 20.00-100) = 156.59^\circ \). The open circuit takes \( \theta_4 = 156.59^\circ - 85.24^\circ = 71.34^\circ \), giving \( B = (100+90\cos 71.34^\circ,\ 90\sin 71.34^\circ) = (128.80,\ 85.27)\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(5) \( \theta_3 = \operatorname{atan2}(85.27-34.64,\ 128.80-20.00) = 24.95^\circ \).

Answer: \( \theta_3 = 24.95^\circ \), \( \theta_4 = 71.34^\circ \). Sanity check: close the loop numerically — \( |AB| = \sqrt{(128.80-20.00)^2+(85.27-34.64)^2} = 120.000 \) mm and \( |O_4B| = 90.000 \) mm, both exact to three decimals. The crossed circuit, taking \( \theta_4 = 156.59^\circ + 85.24^\circ \), is equally valid algebra and describes a linkage that would have to be dismantled to reach.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Velocity two ways, and they must agree

Problem. The same linkage runs at \( \omega_2 = 10 \) rad/s counter-clockwise, with \( \theta_3 = 24.95^\circ \) and \( \theta_4 = 71.34^\circ \) from the previous problem. Find \( \omega_3 \) and \( \omega_4 \) analytically, then locate \( I_{24} \) and confirm \( \omega_4 \) from the drawing.

Solution. (1) \( \omega_3 = \frac{r_2\omega_2\sin(\theta_4-\theta_2)}{r_3\sin(\theta_3-\theta_4)} = \frac{40(10)\sin(11.34^\circ)}{120\sin(-46.39^\circ)} = \frac{78.66}{-86.87} = -0.905\ \mathrm{rad/s} \).

(2) \( \omega_4 = \frac{r_2\omega_2\sin(\theta_2-\theta_3)}{r_4\sin(\theta_4-\theta_3)} = \frac{40(10)\sin(35.05^\circ)}{90\sin(46.39^\circ)} = \frac{229.7}{65.15} = 3.525\ \mathrm{rad/s} \).

(3) \( I_{24} \) lies where the coupler line AB extended meets the ground line \( y=0 \). Parametrising from A to B, \( y \) reaches zero at \( t = \frac{0-34.64}{85.27-34.64} = -0.6842 \), giving \( x = 20.00 - 0.6842(128.80-20.00) = -54.44\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(4) \( |O_2I_{24}| = 54.44 \) mm and \( |O_4I_{24}| = 54.44+100 = 154.44 \) mm, so \( \omega_4/\omega_2 = 54.44/154.44 = 0.3525 \) and \( \omega_4 = 3.525 \) rad/s.

Answer: \( \omega_3 = -0.905 \) rad/s, \( \omega_4 = 3.525 \) rad/s, confirmed by the instant centre. Sanity check: \( I_{24} \) came out negative, meaning it sits outside the linkage on the far side of \( O_2 \) — normal, and the reason instant-centre constructions need generous paper. The two methods share no arithmetic: one is trigonometry on the loop derivative, the other a ratio of two lengths. Agreement to four decimals is therefore a real check, not a restatement.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Rocker swing and the toggle positions (US customary)

Problem. A crank-rocker has ground 4.0 in, crank 1.6 in, coupler 4.8 in, rocker 3.6 in — the same proportions as the worked linkage, scaled. The rocker reaches its extremes when the crank and coupler line up. Find the two extreme rocker angles and the total swing, and state what the output velocity does there.

Solution. At an extreme the crank and coupler are collinear, so the distance \( O_2B \) is either \( r_3+r_2 = 6.4 \) in (crank pointing away) or \( r_3-r_2 = 3.2 \) in (crank folded back). Each case makes a triangle with the ground 4.0 in and the rocker 3.6 in.

(1) Extended: \( \cos\theta_4 = \frac{r_1^2+r_4^2-(r_3+r_2)^2}{2r_1r_4} = \frac{16.00+12.96-40.96}{2(4.0)(3.6)} = \frac{-12.00}{28.8} = -0.4167 \), so \( \theta_4 = 114.62^\circ \).

(2) Folded: \( \cos\theta_4 = \frac{16.00+12.96-10.24}{28.8} = \frac{18.72}{28.8} = 0.6500 \), so \( \theta_4 = 49.46^\circ \).

(3) Swing \( = 114.62^\circ - 49.46^\circ = 65.17^\circ \).

Answer: the rocker swings 65.17°, between 49.46° and 114.62°, and at both limits the output angular velocity is momentarily zero. Sanity check: the angles match the millimetre linkage exactly, as they must — the cosine rule is homogeneous in length, so scaling every link by 0.04 changes no angle. That is also why a linkage can be designed on any convenient scale and manufactured at another. The sweep of the mm version confirms the zero: the minimum of \( |\omega_4/\omega_2| \) over a revolution is 0.0014, found at \( \theta_2 = 239^\circ \), which is a numerical sampling of an exact zero between two sampled degrees.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

A four-bar has how many instant centres?

\( N(N-1)/2 = 4(3)/2 = 6 \) — one for every pair of links, ground included. Four of them are the pin joints, found by inspection; the other two, \( I_{13} \) and \( I_{24} \), have to be constructed with Kennedy's theorem. The count grows quickly: a six-bar has fifteen, which is why the method is used selectively rather than exhaustively.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

The Aronhold-Kennedy theorem states that for any three bodies in plane motion, their three instant centres

They are collinear. That single fact is what makes the construction work: an unknown centre is placed at the crossing of two lines, each drawn through a pair of centres that are already known. For \( I_{24} \) in a four-bar, one line is the ground (from links 1, 2, 4) and the other is the coupler (from links 2, 3, 4).

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

An analysis returns \( \omega_3 = -0.905 \) rad/s while \( \omega_2 \) is positive. This means

A negative angular velocity is a direction, and in a four-bar the coupler routinely reverses its sense of rotation within one revolution of the crank while the crank keeps turning one way. Nothing is wrong. A wrong circuit would show up in the position solution as a linkage that is a mirror image of the one built, and a toggle shows up as an output velocity passing through zero, not as a sign.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Doubling \( \omega_2 \) with the linkage held at the same crank angle

The velocity equations are linear and homogeneous in \( \omega_2 \), so every velocity scales with it while the geometry — and therefore every ratio — is untouched. Quadrupling is the answer for the acceleration terms, which go as \( \omega^2 \); mixing the two up is the most common error in a first dynamic analysis.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Position analysis of a four-bar requires a cosine rule whose argument comes out greater than 1. The correct interpretation is

The triangle formed by the coupler, the rocker and the distance \( d \) does not exist, so the chain cannot close at that crank angle: the linkage physically cannot occupy that position. It is the Grashof result of Lesson 1 arriving as broken arithmetic, and it happens exactly when \( d \) falls outside \( |r_3-r_4| \) to \( r_3+r_4 \). Clamping the cosine hides a real geometric verdict, and speed has nothing to do with position analysis.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

The velocity ratio of a four-bar varies through the cycle because

The ratio equals \( |O_2I_{24}|/|O_4I_{24}| \), and \( I_{24} \) is the crossing of the coupler line with the ground line. As the crank turns, the coupler sweeps and that crossing slides, so the ratio changes continuously — even with a perfectly constant input speed and perfectly rigid, frictionless links. A gear pair, whose contact line keeps a fixed crossing point, is exactly the opposite case.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

At a toggle position of a crank-rocker,

Toggle is the rocker at the end of its swing: it stops and reverses, so its angular velocity passes through zero while the crank continues at full speed. The crank does not stop — in a Grashof crank-rocker it cannot. The transmission angle at those positions is typically at or near its worst, not 90°, and no degree of freedom appears; that is a different phenomenon belonging to special geometries.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Why is an instant-centre construction worth doing when the analytical solution is already available?

The two routes have nothing in common: one differentiates the loop equation, the other measures two distances on a drawing. When they agree to four decimals, as they do for the worked linkage at 0.3525, a whole class of algebra and sign errors has been eliminated. It is not more accurate — drawing tolerance limits it — and it stops at velocity; accelerations need the polygon method of Lesson 3.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the conveyor that indexed short. A packaging line used a crank-rocker to index cartons: the rocker's swing set the stroke, and the stroke was specified at 60° of rocker rotation. After a coupler was replaced following a crash, the line ran but cartons crept out of position over a shift, and the fault was blamed on the servo tuning. It was not the servo; the replacement coupler was 5 mm longer than the drawing.

The swing follows from the extreme positions, where the crank and coupler line up. Working the cosine rule at both extremes for the original set — ground 100, crank 40, coupler 120, rocker 90 mm — gives rocker limits of 49.46° and 114.62°, a swing of 65.17°. Nothing in that calculation involves speed, torque or control gains. It is four lengths and two cosine rules, and it is the number the mechanical design promised the controller. A coupler 5 mm long moves both extremes, and the controller, commanding the same crank rotation, delivers a stroke that no longer matches the pitch. The drift per cycle was under a millimetre, which is precisely why it was chased in software for two days.

Two practices follow. The stroke of a linkage is a consequence of four link lengths, so those lengths carry the tolerance that the stroke specification demands — a coupler is not generic bar stock cut to a tape measure. And when a machine's geometry has been disturbed, the first check is the geometry: extreme positions and swing take a minute to compute from the drawing and settle whether the mechanism can still do what the controller is being asked to correct for.
