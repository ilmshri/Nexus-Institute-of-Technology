Governors and gyroscopic effects

Centrifugal governors as proportional controllers made of iron. Gyroscopic couples on turbine and vehicle bearings. Legacy topics that still explain modern behavior.

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • What does a governor control that a flywheel cannot?
  • Where do gyroscopic couples load a turbine's bearings?

§1 · The problem a flywheel cannot solve

Lesson 9 sized a flywheel to hold the speed steady within a cycle. It said nothing about what the average speed should be, and it cannot: a flywheel is a passive store, and if the load falls away it simply carries on turning faster. Holding the mean speed against a changing load is a control problem, and the governor is that controller, built from iron a century before anyone wrote down a transfer function.

The principle is a feedback loop with no electronics in it. Two balls are carried on arms from a spindle driven by the engine. Rotation throws them outward against gravity; the arms lift a sleeve; the sleeve moves a linkage that closes the fuel or steam valve. Speed rises, balls rise, supply falls, speed falls. The equilibrium of the balls is the measurement, and the sleeve position is the control action.

§2 · The Watt governor, and the inverse square that defeats it

Take the simplest arrangement: two balls of mass \( m \) on arms pivoted on the spindle axis, with nothing else attached. Each ball travels a circle of radius \( r \) at angular speed \( \omega \), so the arm must supply a centripetal force \( m\omega^2r \) horizontally and carry the weight \( mg \) vertically. The arm can only pull along its own length, so the ratio of those two components fixes the arm's angle — and with it the height \( h \) of the ball below the pivot.

Key relation — Watt governor height

\[ \tan\theta=\frac{m\omega^2r}{mg}=\frac{r}{h} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h=\frac{g}{\omega^2}=\frac{895}{N^2}\ \text{metres} \]

with \( N \) in rev/min. The ball mass cancels completely: a heavier ball does not change the height, because it adds to both the centripetal requirement and the weight.

That cancellation is the first surprise and the inverse square is the second. At 60 rev/min, \( h = 0.248 \) m — a governor a quarter of a metre tall, which is workable. At 120 it is 0.062 m, and at 200 it is 0.022 m. The whole travel available to move a valve has shrunk to 22 mm, and the change in that travel for a small speed change is smaller still. Below a certain size the friction in the linkage swamps the signal and the governor stops responding at all.

So the simple Watt governor is a low-speed device, and that is a geometric fact rather than a manufacturing limitation. Every practical governor is a way of defeating it.

§3 · Loading the governor, and the three words that grade it

The Porter governor adds a dead weight \( M \) on the sleeve. The arms now carry the balls' weight and lift the sleeve load, so a much higher rotational speed is needed to reach any given angle — equivalently, at a given speed the balls sit far higher than they would unloaded.

Key relation — Porter governor height

\[ h=\frac{m+M}{m}\cdot\frac{g}{\omega^2} \]

for the arrangement whose upper and lower arms meet on the axis. The factor \( (m+M)/m \) is the design lever, and unlike the Watt case the ball mass now matters.

With a 2 kg ball and a 20 kg sleeve the factor is 11, so the 22 mm of the Watt governor at 200 rev/min becomes 246 mm — usable again. Springs are used the same way in high-speed governors, where the dead weight would be impractically large and its own inertia would make the governor sluggish.

Three properties grade the result. Sensitiveness is the ratio of mean speed to the speed range between the sleeve's extreme positions: a sensitive governor makes a large sleeve movement for a small speed change. Stability requires that each speed correspond to exactly one radius, so the governor settles rather than wanders. And a governor is isochronous when the sleeve moves through its whole travel with no speed change at all — the limiting case of sensitiveness, and a trap: an isochronous governor has no unique equilibrium, so it drives the sleeve hard to one end or the other and the engine hunts, oscillating about the set speed instead of holding it. The cure is deliberate insensitivity plus friction or damping, which is the same conclusion a control engineer reaches about excessive proportional gain, in different words.

§4 · Gyroscopic couples: the reaction to turning a spinning rotor

The last topic of the course is one every rotating machine carries and few analyses include. A spinning rotor has angular momentum \( I\omega \) along its axis. Turn that axis — precess it — and the direction of the momentum vector changes, which requires a torque, and the rotor applies the reaction of that torque to its bearings.

Key relation — gyroscopic couple

\[ C=I\,\omega\,\omega_p \]

\( \omega \) is the spin speed and \( \omega_p \) the precession rate. The couple acts about the axis perpendicular to both, so a rotor precessed in yaw reacts in pitch.

The numbers are not small. A turbine rotor of \( I = 25\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \) at 3000 rev/min has \( \omega = 314.2 \) rad/s. Put it in a ship turning at a leisurely 0.1 rad/s and \( C = 25(314.2)(0.1) = 785\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \). Carried by bearings 2 m apart, that is 393 N up on one and 393 N down on the other — a load that appears only while the vessel turns, reverses when it turns the other way, and is invisible to any analysis done with the ship on a straight course.

The same term explains behaviour that looks like magic elsewhere: a ship's turbine pitching the hull as it turns, a rotor whirling in its clearance, a large flywheel making a vehicle reluctant to change direction. It is also why the flywheel of Lesson 9 is not free — adding inertia to smooth a cycle adds gyroscopic couple to every manoeuvre. Governors and gyroscopes are usually filed as legacy topics; they are better understood as the two places where this course's kinematics reaches directly into how a machine behaves in service.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Flywheels and speed fluctuation (Lesson 9) — the distinction this lesson opens with: a flywheel holds speed within a cycle, a governor sets the mean.
  • Centripetal force (Dynamics L3) — \( m\omega^2r \), one of the two components whose ratio sets the arm angle.
  • Angular momentum (Physics I L10) — \( I\omega \), the vector whose rotation produces the gyroscopic couple.
  • Moment equilibrium (Statics L5) — resolving a couple into a pair of bearing reactions.
  • Balancing (Lesson 10) — the other running load that appears only when the machine is turning.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

The Watt governor's collapse with speed

Problem. Find the ball height of a simple Watt governor at 60, 120 and 200 rev/min. State what happens to the height when the speed doubles, and say why the device is unusable at the highest of the three speeds.

Solution. \( h = g/\omega^2 \) with \( \omega = 2\pi N/60 \).

(1) \( N = 60 \): \( \omega = 6.283\ \mathrm{rad/s} \), \( \omega^2 = 39.48 \), so \( h = 9.81/39.48 = 0.2485\ \mathrm{m} \).

(2) \( N = 120 \): \( \omega = 12.566 \), \( \omega^2 = 157.9 \), so \( h = 0.0621\ \mathrm{m} \).

(3) \( N = 200 \): \( \omega = 20.944 \), \( \omega^2 = 438.6 \), so \( h = 0.0224\ \mathrm{m} \).

(4) Doubling the speed quarters the height, since \( h \propto 1/\omega^2 \): 0.2485 to 0.0621 m from 60 to 120 rev/min is a factor of exactly 4.

Answer: 248.5, 62.1 and 22.4 mm; the height falls as the inverse square of speed, and 22 mm is too small to work with. Sanity check: the shortcut \( h = 895/N^2 \) metres reproduces all three — \( 895/3600 = 0.249 \), \( 895/14\,400 = 0.0622 \), \( 895/40\,000 = 0.0224 \) — which is worth carrying because it needs no unit conversion. The reason 22 mm fails is not the absolute size but the change in it: the sleeve movement available for a 1% speed change is a fraction of a millimetre, which the friction in any real linkage will absorb entirely.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Sizing a Porter governor

Problem. A Porter governor has 2 kg balls and a 20 kg sleeve weight, with upper and lower arms meeting on the axis. Find the ball height at 200 rev/min and compare it with the Watt governor at the same speed. Then find the sleeve mass needed to give a 150 mm height at 250 rev/min with the same 2 kg balls.

Solution. (1) At 200 rev/min, \( g/\omega^2 = 0.02236\ \mathrm{m} \) from the previous problem, and the loading factor is \( \dfrac{m+M}{m} = \dfrac{2+20}{2} = 11 \).

(2) \( h = 11(0.02236) = 0.2460\ \mathrm{m} \), against the Watt governor's 0.0224 m — eleven times as much, which is the factor itself.

(3) At 250 rev/min, \( \omega = 26.18\ \mathrm{rad/s} \) and \( g/\omega^2 = 9.81/685.4 = 0.014313\ \mathrm{m} \).

(4) The required factor is \( 0.150/0.014313 = 10.48 \), so \( \dfrac{2+M}{2} = 10.48 \) and \( M = 2(10.48-1) = 18.96\ \mathrm{kg} \).

Answer: 246 mm at 200 rev/min, eleven times the Watt height; a 18.96 kg sleeve gives 150 mm at 250 rev/min. Sanity check: note that the answer to (2) is exactly the loading factor times the Watt result, because the sleeve load enters as a simple multiplier and nothing else changes. Note also what it costs: the 20 kg sleeve is dead weight that must be accelerated every time the governor responds, which makes a heavily loaded governor slow. That is why high-speed engines use a spring instead — the same multiplying effect with a fraction of the moving mass.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Gyroscopic couple on a turbine's bearings (with a US customary check)

Problem. A turbine rotor has \( I = 25\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} \) and runs at 3000 rev/min in a vessel that turns at 0.10 rad/s. Its bearings are 2.0 m apart. Find the gyroscopic couple and the bearing reactions it produces, express the reaction in pounds-force, and state what happens if the vessel turns twice as fast.

Solution. (1) \( \omega = 2\pi(3000)/60 = 314.16\ \mathrm{rad/s} \).

(2) \( C = I\omega\omega_p = 25(314.16)(0.10) = 785.4\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \).

(3) A couple carried by two bearings 2.0 m apart is a force pair: \( F = C/L = 785.4/2.0 = 392.7\ \mathrm{N} \), one up and one down.

(4) \( 392.7/4.448 = 88.3\ \mathrm{lbf} \).

(5) The couple is linear in \( \omega_p \), so doubling the turn rate to 0.20 rad/s doubles it to 1570.8 N·m and the bearing reactions to 785.4 N.

Answer: 785.4 N·m, giving \( \pm 392.7 \) N (88.3 lbf) at the bearings, and both double if the vessel turns twice as fast. Sanity check: the load exists only while the vessel is turning and reverses when it turns the other way, so it is a transient, fully reversed duty that no steady-state bearing calculation would ever show. Its direction is worth stating carefully too: the couple acts about the axis perpendicular to both the spin and the precession, so a rotor yawed by the vessel's turn reacts in pitch — it tries to lift one end of the machine, not to resist the turn.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

The difference between a flywheel and a governor is that

A flywheel is a passive energy store that smooths the swing within one revolution and has no view about what the average speed should be; if the load falls away it simply speeds up. A governor measures speed and changes the energy supplied, which is a feedback loop. Machines that need both fit both, and confusing them is why a surging engine is sometimes given a bigger flywheel, which cannot help.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

In a simple Watt governor, doubling the ball mass

\( h = g/\omega^2 \) contains no mass at all. The ball mass appears in both the centripetal requirement \( m\omega^2r \) and the weight \( mg \), and cancels when their ratio sets the arm angle. This is why the Watt governor cannot be tuned by changing the balls, and why a sleeve load, which does not cancel, is the standard way to change its behaviour.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A Porter governor's height exceeds the Watt value by the factor

\( h = \frac{m+M}{m}\cdot\frac{g}{\omega^2} \), so a 2 kg ball with a 20 kg sleeve gives 11, not 10 — the balls' own weight is still being carried. Using \( M/m \) instead is the standard slip and lands about 10% low here, more at small \( M/m \).

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

An isochronous governor is one that

Isochronism is the limiting case of sensitiveness: every sleeve position corresponds to the same speed, so there is no unique equilibrium for the governor to settle at. It drives the sleeve to one extreme, overshoots, and drives it to the other — hunting. It sounds like the ideal and is a fault, which is the same lesson a control engineer learns about excessive proportional gain.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Hunting in a governed engine is cured by

Hunting is an over-responsive loop, so the fix is to reduce the response and dissipate energy in it — a small deliberate speed range across the sleeve travel, plus friction or a dashpot. Raising the sensitiveness makes it worse. A larger flywheel smooths fluctuation within a cycle and does nothing about an oscillation of the mean speed, which is a different phenomenon on a much longer timescale.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

The gyroscopic couple on a rotor is

\( C = I\omega\omega_p \) — the rate of change of the angular-momentum vector \( I\omega \) as its direction is swung at \( \omega_p \). \( I\omega \) alone is the momentum, and \( \tfrac12 I\omega^2 \) is the kinetic energy the flywheel of Lesson 9 stores; neither is a couple.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A rotor spinning about a fore-and-aft axis is precessed by a vessel turning in yaw. The gyroscopic couple acts

The couple is perpendicular to both the spin and the precession, so a rotor yawed by the vessel's turn reacts in pitch — it tries to lift one end of the machine rather than to resist the turn. That perpendicular relationship is what makes gyroscopic effects feel counter-intuitive, and it is why the resulting bearing loads appear where nobody was watching.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Fitting a larger flywheel to a vehicle engine to reduce speed fluctuation also

\( C = I\omega\omega_p \) is proportional to \( I \), so more inertia means a larger couple for the same spin and turn rate — the machine becomes more reluctant to change direction and loads its bearings harder when it does. That is the standing cost of the Lesson 9 decision, and the reason flywheel sizing is bounded from above as well as below.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the generator set that surged after an overhaul. A governor-controlled engine driving an alternator was overhauled. The governor linkage was cleaned, re-bushed and freed off, and its response was checked and found much crisper than before. In service the set began surging: the speed swung slowly above and below its set point instead of settling, and the electrical output followed it.

Nothing had failed. The overhaul had raised the governor's sensitiveness by removing the friction that had been damping it, pushing it toward the isochronous condition where a whole range of sleeve positions corresponds to one speed. With no unique equilibrium to settle at, the sleeve is driven to one extreme, the engine overshoots, and the sleeve is driven to the other — which is hunting, and it is a property of the loop rather than of any component in it. The engine was not faulty, the governor was not worn, and the linkage was in better condition than it had been for years.

Two practices follow. Friction in a governor linkage is not purely a defect — part of it is the damping the loop needs, so an overhaul that removes it must restore damping deliberately, with a dashpot or a designed speed droop, rather than assume that freer is better. And a machine that surges is telling you about a control loop, not a mechanism: the useful measurement is the speed range across the full sleeve travel, and if that range is near zero the governor is isochronous and will hunt no matter how well it is made.
