Gear tooth geometry

The involute and why conjugate action matters. Module, pressure angle, and interference. Backlash as both necessity and defect.

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Why does the involute tolerate center-distance error?
  • What does interference between teeth physically destroy?

§1 · Conjugate action, and the one curve that delivers it

A gear pair has one job that a friction wheel cannot do reliably: hold an exact, constant speed ratio while transmitting torque. Constant means constant through the mesh, not on average — a ratio that fluctuates within a tooth pitch is a vibration source, and it will be found in the spectrum of Lesson 6.

The condition is geometric. Where two tooth flanks touch, the common normal to both surfaces must pass through a fixed point on the line of centres — the pitch point. That is the law of gearing, and any pair of profiles satisfying it is called conjugate. Many curves can be made conjugate to some mate; only one is conjugate to itself, so that a gear cut with it meshes correctly with any other gear of the same pressure angle and pitch. That curve is the involute: the path traced by a point on a taut string unwound from a circle — the base circle.

The involute's decisive property follows from that construction. The string is always tangent to the base circle, so the contact normal always lies along one fixed straight line, the line of action, inclined to the common tangent by the pressure angle \( \phi \). The direction in which one tooth pushes another never changes through the mesh, which means the bearing load direction never changes either. A cycloidal tooth is conjugate too, and does not have that property.

§2 · Module, pitch and the standard tooth

Two gears mesh only if their teeth are the same size, so tooth size is standardised as the module \( m \), the pitch diameter per tooth.

Key relations — gear geometry

\[ m=\frac{d}{N},\qquad p=\pi m,\qquad d_b=d\cos\phi,\qquad C=\frac{m(N_1+N_2)}{2} \]

\( p \) is the circular pitch measured on the pitch circle and \( d_b \) is the base-circle diameter. In US customary practice the same idea is inverted as diametral pitch \( P=N/d \) teeth per inch, with \( m = 25.4/P \) mm.

Take \( m=4 \) mm, \( \phi=20^\circ \), a 20-tooth pinion driving a 40-tooth gear. Then \( d_1 = 80 \), \( d_2 = 160 \) and \( C = 120 \) mm; the base circles are \( 80\cos 20^\circ = 75.18 \) and \( 150.35 \) mm; the circular pitch is \( \pi(4) = 12.566 \) mm and the base pitch \( \pi m\cos\phi = 11.809 \) mm. The standard full-depth tooth then adds an addendum of \( 1m \) above the pitch circle and a dedendum of \( 1.25m \) below it, the extra quarter-module giving root clearance.

§3 · Contact ratio: how many teeth are actually working

Contact begins where the addendum circle of the driven gear cuts the line of action and ends where the driver's addendum circle cuts it. The length between is the length of action \( Z \), and dividing it by the base pitch counts how many tooth pairs are in mesh on average.

Key relation — contact ratio

\[ m_c=\frac{Z}{p_b} =\frac{\sqrt{r_{a1}^2-r_{b1}^2}+\sqrt{r_{a2}^2-r_{b2}^2}-C\sin\phi}{\pi m\cos\phi} \]

\( m_c > 1 \) is mandatory — below it, one pair disengages before the next takes over and the drive stops. Practice wants at least 1.2, and 1.4 or more for quiet running.

For the worked pair, \( \sqrt{44^2-37.588^2}=22.873 \), \( \sqrt{84^2-75.175^2}=37.479 \) and \( C\sin\phi = 41.042 \) mm, so \( Z = 19.309 \) mm and \( m_c = 19.309/11.809 = 1.635 \). Read that as: for 63.5% of the time two tooth pairs share the load, and for the rest a single pair carries everything. It is the fractional part that matters. As the second pair engages and disengages the stiffness of the mesh changes abruptly, and that stiffness variation — not the tooth force itself — is the origin of gear-mesh vibration.

§4 · Interference, and what centre distance does not change

The involute exists only outside the base circle; inside it there is no involute to be conjugate with. If a gear's addendum reaches the mating pinion's flank below its base circle, the tip does not roll — it digs, cutting material out of the pinion's root and leaving it weakened. This is interference, and it is worst for a small pinion driven by a large gear.

Key relation — interference limit

\[ N_{\min}=\frac{2k}{\sin^2\phi} \]

\( k=1 \) for a standard full-depth tooth. This is the limit against a rack, the worst case.

The numbers explain a century of standards work. At \( \phi=14.5^\circ \), \( N_{\min}=31.9 \), so a pinion needed 32 teeth — large gears, large centre distances, modest ratios per stage. At \( 20^\circ \), \( N_{\min}=17.1 \) and an 18-tooth pinion is legal. At \( 25^\circ \) it falls to 11.2, which is why high-ratio and compact drives use the steeper angle, accepting higher radial bearing loads and a lower contact ratio in exchange. When a smaller pinion is unavoidable, the cure is profile shift: cut the tooth with the hob withdrawn slightly, which moves the working part of the flank away from the base circle without changing the module or the mating gear.

Finally, the property that makes involute gears manufacturable at all. Move the gears apart and the pitch circles and pressure angle both change — but the base circles do not, because they were cut into the metal. The line of action still runs tangent to the same two base circles, so the speed ratio is still exactly \( N_1/N_2 \). Opening the worked pair by 1 mm raises the operating pressure angle from 20° to 21.26° and drops the contact ratio from 1.635 to 1.395 — a 14.7% loss of overlap — and opens a backlash of \( 2\Delta C\tan\phi = 0.73 \) mm. The ratio, however, is untouched. A tolerance on centre distance is therefore a tolerance on smoothness and noise, not on kinematic accuracy, which is exactly the trade a housing designer needs to know. Some backlash is required in any case, for lubricant and thermal growth; the design task is to control it, not to eliminate it.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Higher pairs (Lesson 1) — gear-tooth contact removes one freedom, the same class as the cam contact of Lesson 4.
  • Instant centres (Lesson 2) — the pitch point is the instant centre of the two gears, which is why the law of gearing is stated there.
  • Rolling and sliding contact (Dynamics L5) — pure rolling occurs only at the pitch point; everywhere else the flanks slide.
  • Involute construction (Mathematics I) — the unwinding-string curve and its tangent property.
  • Contact stress (Machine Design I L3) — what the tooth force does once the geometry has decided where it acts.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Geometry and contact ratio of a gear pair

Problem. A 20-tooth pinion drives a 40-tooth gear, module 4 mm, pressure angle 20°, standard full-depth teeth (addendum \( 1m \)). Find the pitch diameters, centre distance, base-circle radii, base pitch and contact ratio.

Solution. (1) \( d_1 = mN_1 = 4(20) = 80 \) mm and \( d_2 = 4(40) = 160 \) mm, so \( r_1 = 40 \), \( r_2 = 80 \) and \( C = 40+80 = 120 \) mm.

(2) \( r_{b} = r\cos\phi \): \( r_{b1} = 40\cos 20^\circ = 37.588 \) mm and \( r_{b2} = 75.175 \) mm.

(3) Addendum radii \( r_{a1} = 40+4 = 44 \) mm, \( r_{a2} = 80+4 = 84 \) mm.

(4) \( p_b = \pi m\cos\phi = \pi(4)(0.93969) = 11.809 \) mm.

(5) \( Z = \sqrt{44^2-37.588^2} + \sqrt{84^2-75.175^2} - 120\sin 20^\circ = 22.873 + 37.479 - 41.042 = 19.309 \) mm.

(6) \( m_c = 19.309/11.809 = 1.635 \).

Answer: \( d_1=80 \), \( d_2=160 \), \( C=120 \) mm; \( r_{b1}=37.59 \), \( r_{b2}=75.18 \) mm; \( p_b = 11.81 \) mm; \( m_c = 1.635 \). Sanity check: \( m_c \) comfortably exceeds 1, so the drive never runs out of contact, and its fractional part says two pairs share the load 63.5% of the time and one pair carries everything for the remaining 36.5%. The single-pair interval is the one that sizes the tooth. As an independent check on the arithmetic, \( Z \) must be smaller than \( C\sin\phi = 41.04 \) mm, the whole tangent length between the base circles — and 19.31 mm is 47% of it, which is typical for standard teeth.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Interference and the choice of pressure angle

Problem. Find the minimum pinion tooth count against a rack for standard full-depth teeth at 14.5°, 20° and 25°. A drive needs a 14-tooth pinion. State which options make that legal and what each costs.

Solution. \( N_{\min}=2k/\sin^2\phi \) with \( k=1 \).

(1) \( \phi=14.5^\circ \): \( \sin\phi = 0.25038 \), \( N_{\min} = 2/0.062690 = 31.90 \) → 32 teeth.

(2) \( \phi=20^\circ \): \( \sin\phi = 0.34202 \), \( N_{\min} = 2/0.116978 = 17.10 \) → 18 teeth.

(3) \( \phi=25^\circ \): \( \sin\phi = 0.42262 \), \( N_{\min} = 2/0.178606 = 11.20 \) → 12 teeth.

(4) A 14-tooth pinion is illegal at 20° and legal at 25°. The alternatives are to cut it at 20° with a positive profile shift, or to reduce the addendum (lower \( k \)), since \( N_{\min} \) is proportional to \( k \).

Answer: 32, 18 and 12 teeth; a 14-tooth pinion needs 25°, a profile shift, or a stub tooth. Sanity check: each route has a price. Going to 25° raises the radial component of the tooth force by \( \tan 25^\circ/\tan 20^\circ = 1.28 \), a 28% increase in the separating load the bearings carry, and lowers the contact ratio. A profile shift keeps the module and the mating gear but changes the tooth thickness and needs the pair designed together. A stub tooth reduces the contact ratio directly, since a shorter addendum shortens the length of action. The inverse-square dependence on \( \sin\phi \) is what makes the 14.5° standard so restrictive, and why it was retired.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Centre-distance error, and a US customary pair

Problem. (a) The 20/40-tooth pair above is assembled with its centres 121 mm apart instead of 120. Find the operating pressure angle, the new contact ratio and the backlash, and say what happens to the speed ratio. (b) Separately, find the contact ratio of an 18/36-tooth pair of diametral pitch 6 per inch at 20°.

Solution (a). (1) The base circles are cut into the metal and cannot change, so \( C\cos\phi \) is invariant: \( \cos\phi' = \frac{120\cos 20^\circ}{121} = \frac{112.763}{121} = 0.93192 \), giving \( \phi' = 21.26^\circ \).

(2) The two square-root terms are properties of the gears alone and are unchanged at 22.873 and 37.479 mm, so \( Z' = 22.873+37.479-121\sin 21.26^\circ = 60.352-43.881 = 16.471 \) mm.

(3) \( m_c' = 16.471/11.809 = 1.395 \), down from 1.635 — a 14.7% loss.

(4) \( B \approx 2\Delta C\tan\phi = 2(1)(0.36397) = 0.73 \) mm.

(5) The speed ratio is still exactly \( 40/20 = 2 \), because it is set by the base circles.

Solution (b). \( d_1 = 18/6 = 3.000 \) in, \( d_2 = 6.000 \) in, \( C = 4.500 \) in; \( r_{b1} = 1.5\cos 20^\circ = 1.40954 \), \( r_{b2} = 2.81908 \) in; addendum \( 1/P = 0.16667 \) in so \( r_{a1} = 1.66667 \), \( r_{a2} = 3.16667 \) in; \( p_b = (\pi/6)\cos 20^\circ = 0.49202 \) in. \( Z = 0.88937+1.44242-1.53909 = 0.79270 \) in, so \( m_c = 1.611 \).

Answer: (a) \( \phi'=21.26^\circ \), \( m_c=1.395 \), backlash 0.73 mm, ratio unchanged at 2:1. (b) \( m_c = 1.611 \). Sanity check: part (a) is the practical reason involute teeth won: a housing bore tolerance degrades smoothness and adds backlash but cannot introduce a ratio error, so a gearbox does not need a precision centre distance to keep time. Part (b) confirms the unit system is cosmetic — \( P=6 \) per inch is \( m = 25.4/6 = 4.233 \) mm, close to the 4 mm pair, and the contact ratio comes out at 1.611 against 1.635, the small difference coming from 18 and 36 teeth rather than 20 and 40.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

Conjugate action requires that the common normal at the point of tooth contact

That is the law of gearing, and it is what makes the angular-velocity ratio constant instant by instant rather than merely on average. The involute satisfies it by keeping the contact normal along one fixed straight line, the line of action, tangent to both base circles — so the direction of the tooth force, and hence of the bearing load, never changes through the mesh.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

Two gears will mesh correctly only if they share the same

Module fixes tooth size and pressure angle fixes the flank shape, so any two involute gears with both in common will mesh — the involute is conjugate to itself, which is precisely why gears are stocked as catalogue items rather than cut in matched pairs. Tooth counts set the ratio and must differ for a reduction. Face width and material affect capacity, not whether the teeth engage.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A gear pair is computed to have a contact ratio of 0.94. This means

A contact ratio below 1 means there are instants with no tooth pair in contact at all: the drive breaks and re-engages every pitch, which is an impact load and, in the strict sense, not a gear drive. Above 1 the mesh is continuous, and the fractional part gives the proportion of time a second pair is sharing the load — 63.5% for the worked pair at 1.635. Practice wants at least 1.2, and more for quiet running.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Interference in a gear pair physically

There is no involute inside the base circle, so a mating tip that reaches below it cannot roll conjugately — it digs. In cutting this shows as undercutting by the hob, which thins the tooth root exactly where the bending stress is highest; in a running pair it shows as scoring. It is avoided by keeping the pinion above \( 2k/\sin^2\phi \) teeth, by profile shift, or by a shorter addendum.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Moving to a 25° pressure angle from 20° allows a smaller pinion. The principal penalty is

The tooth force acts along the line of action, so its radial component goes as \( \tan\phi \): moving from 20° to 25° raises the separating load by \( \tan 25^\circ/\tan 20^\circ = 1.28 \), a 28% increase carried by the bearings and the shafts. The length of action also shortens relative to the base pitch, so the contact ratio falls and the mesh runs less smoothly. The ratio and the module are untouched — those depend on tooth counts and tooth size.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A gearbox housing is bored 1 mm wide, opening a 120 mm centre distance to 121 mm. The speed ratio

The base circles were cut into the metal and do not move, and the line of action stays tangent to both, so the ratio remains exactly \( N_2/N_1 \). What does change is the operating pressure angle (20° to 21.26° here), the contact ratio (1.635 to 1.395) and the backlash (0.73 mm). That insensitivity of ratio to centre distance is the involute's great manufacturing advantage.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

Backlash in a gear pair is

Zero backlash means the teeth are pinched: no room for an oil film, no room for the housing and gears to grow at different rates, and a jammed drive at temperature. Some is therefore designed in, by tooth-thickness allowance or by centre distance, and specified with a tolerance. It matters most in reversing and positioning drives, where the lost motion at reversal is the backlash, and there it is minimised with anti-backlash arrangements rather than by removing the clearance.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Pure rolling between two involute teeth occurs

At the pitch point the two flanks have equal velocities, so there is rolling alone; that point is the instant centre of the two gears. Away from it the contact slides, with the sliding velocity growing toward tip and root. That is why scuffing and pitting appear on the flanks above and below the pitch line rather than on it, and why the pitch line is often the last polished band on a worn tooth.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the replacement pinion that would not run quietly. A drive pinion was replaced on a gearbox after a bearing failure. The new pinion had the correct tooth count and the correct module, the gears meshed, and the ratio was verified on the shafts. The unit ran, but noisily, and a vibration survey found a strong component at tooth-passing frequency that had not been there before.

The pinion had been supplied at 25° pressure angle in place of the original 20°. Nothing in a quick check catches this: module and tooth count fix the pitch diameters, so the centre distance still fits and the shafts still turn at the right ratio. What changes is the flank shape. Two gears of different pressure angle are not conjugate to each other, so contact no longer holds a fixed line of action, the ratio fluctuates within each pitch, and the fluctuation appears exactly at tooth-passing frequency. The steeper pinion also raises the radial separating force by \( \tan 25^\circ/\tan 20^\circ = 1.28 \), which is 28% more load into the bearing that had just been replaced.

Two practices follow. Pressure angle belongs on the spares specification alongside module and tooth count — three numbers, not two, and the third is the one that cannot be measured with a caliper on the shop floor. And a noise complaint that arrives with a new component is a geometry question first: a spectrum peaking at tooth-passing frequency points at the mesh itself, not at the bearings, and the mesh is defined by the pair's shared geometry rather than by either gear alone.
