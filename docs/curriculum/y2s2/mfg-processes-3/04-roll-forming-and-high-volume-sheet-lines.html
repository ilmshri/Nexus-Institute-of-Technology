Roll forming and high-volume sheet lines

Progressive roll forming of profiles; line balancing and tooling. Coil handling, leveling, and slitting upstream. The continuous logic of Kirby-style panel production.

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Why does roll forming beat brake bending at volume?
  • What does the flower pattern encode?

§1 · Continuous instead of discrete

A press brake makes one bend at a time on one part at a time. A roll former makes every bend at once, on a strip that never stops: coil feeds through a line of roll stands, each pair of rolls adding a few degrees to the developing profile, until the section leaves the last stand complete and a flying shear cuts it to length.

The consequence is that complexity stops costing time. On a brake, doubling the number of bends roughly doubles the cycle. On a roll former, doubling the number of bends means more stands and more tooling, but the line still runs at the same metres per minute, so the output per hour is unchanged. Time is bought once, in capital.

That is exactly the shape of a break-even calculation. Take a six-bend profile 3 m long. On a brake at 6 s a bend plus 20 s of handling, the cycle is 56 s, so 64.3 parts an hour; at a machine-and-operator rate of 60 an hour that is 0.933 a part, on tooling costing 4,000. Roll formed at 30 m/min, a 3 m part occupies the line for 6 s, so 600 parts an hour; at 90 an hour that is 0.150 a part, on tooling costing 60,000.

Key relation — the process crossover

\[ N^{*}=\frac{C_{\text{roll}}-C_{\text{brake}}}{c_{\text{brake}}-c_{\text{roll}}} \]

Fixed cost difference over variable cost difference. Here \( (60{,}000-4{,}000)/(0.933-0.150)=71{,}500 \) parts.

So the honest answer to "why does roll forming beat brake bending at volume" is that it does not, below about seventy thousand parts of this profile — and above it, it wins by a widening margin, because the line is 9.3 times faster and the tooling has already been paid for. Two riders matter as much as the number. Roll-forming tooling is profile-specific, so the crossover has to be reached on one profile rather than on a family of them; and changing profile means re-rolling the whole line, which is hours, so a roll former with a mixed order book loses on changeover what it gains on speed.

§2 · The flower pattern, and the strain nobody sees on the drawing

Superimpose the profile's cross-section as it appears at each stand and the result opens like a flower. That drawing — the flower pattern — is the roll former's real design document, and it encodes three things the part drawing does not.

The first is the angle schedule: how many degrees each stand adds. Mild steel tolerates 15 to 30 degrees a stand; high-strength grades need 3 to 8, because they have less ductility to spend and far more springback to fight. A 90° leg is therefore a 4-stand job in mild steel and a 12-stand job in a high-strength grade, and the difference is visible in the flower before any roll is cut.

The second is the material path, which is where the real design work is. As a leg rises, its outer edge has to travel farther than the web does, because it follows a longer route through the same machine length.

Key relation — longitudinal edge strain

\[ \varepsilon=\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{h}{L}\right)^{2}}-1\ \approx\ \frac{h^{2}}{2L^{2}} \]

\( h \) is how far the edge rises and \( L \) the forming length over which it rises. The strip is continuous, so that extra length has to come from stretching the edge — and if \( \varepsilon \) exceeds the yield strain, it does not spring back.

Raise a 40 mm leg over one 400 mm stand pitch and \( \varepsilon=0.499\% \). Mild steel yields at \( Y/E=0.121\% \). The edge is being strained four times past yield, so it lengthens permanently, and a permanently longer edge on a strip of fixed length has nowhere to go: it buckles into the ripple called edge wave. Invert the relation and the design rule falls out — keeping \( \varepsilon\le\varepsilon_y \) needs \( L\ge814 \) mm, which at a 300 mm stand pitch means spreading that leg over three stands instead of one.

The third thing the flower encodes is where the strip is gripped. A section is driven by friction on the surfaces the rolls touch, so the pattern has to keep a flat, driven region all the way through; a profile that closes early loses its drive and stops feeding.

§3 · Everything upstream of the first stand

A roll-forming line is longer before the forming than after it, and most of what goes wrong happens there.

Coil arrives with coil set — a residual curvature from being wound. A leveller removes it by bending the strip alternately over a set of offset rolls, working it past yield in both directions until it forgets its history. That only works if the rolls are small enough to yield the strip through most of its thickness: the surface strain of bending over a roll of radius \( R \) is \( t/2R \).

Key relation — leveller roll size

\[ \varepsilon_{\text{surface}}=\frac{t}{2R}\ \ge\ k\,\varepsilon_y \]

Reaching five times yield in 1.5 mm mild steel needs \( R\le124 \) mm, that is rolls of 248 mm diameter or less. A leveller with rolls too large flattens the strip visually and leaves the residual stress that later makes the finished profile bow.

Slitting cuts a wide master coil into strips of working width with rotary knives, and everything Lesson 1 said about clearance applies: too little double-shears the edge, too much leaves a heavy burr. Two extra consequences belong to slitting specifically. The burr has a direction, and a burr rolled to the outside of a bend is a crack starter. And a slit strip carries camber — a gentle curve in its own plane from asymmetric residual stress — which the roll former converts faithfully into a bowed profile.

Downstream the line is balanced by its slowest element, which is rarely the forming. Cut-off, punching stations and packing all take their turn as the constraint, and a line running below its rated speed is usually waiting on one of them rather than on the rolls.

§4 · What roll forming holds, and what it cannot do

Roll-formed sections hold their cross-section well — typically \( \pm0.5 \) mm on section dimensions and \( \pm1^{\circ} \) on angles — because every part passes through the same rolls. What they hold badly is everything along the length: bow (curvature in the plane of the web), camber (curvature in the plane of the flanges) and twist. Those come from asymmetric strain history, which is why the flower pattern is drawn symmetrically wherever the part allows it, and why a deliberately asymmetric profile is a harder tooling job than its drawing suggests.

End flare is the characteristic defect. While the strip is continuous, each section is restrained by the material ahead of and behind it; the moment the shear cuts, the free ends lose that restraint and spring open. The usual answers are to cut before forming and accept slightly worse length control, to cut after forming and trim a short scrap end, or to add a final restraining stand close to the shear.

Three things the process cannot do, and it is worth knowing them before a profile is designed around it. A section that changes along its length is impossible in ordinary roll forming, because every stand sees the same shape pass through it. Parts shorter than the forming length cannot be produced conventionally, since the piece must be gripped by several stands at once. And a profile that closes and reopens loses the driven surfaces it needs, so closed sections are formed to nearly closed and then seam-welded rather than folded shut.

Which returns to the same decision as \( \S1 \). Brake bending is right for low volume, high mix, short parts and geometry that varies; roll forming is right for one long profile in quantity. The crossover is a number, it is computable before either tool is ordered, and it moves with the profile — more bends push it toward roll forming, because bends cost time on a brake and only capital on a line.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Where does the line beat the brake?

Problem. A six-bend profile 3 m long is quoted two ways. On a press brake each bend takes 6 s and handling adds 20 s per part, at a machine-and-operator rate of 60 per hour, on tooling costing 4,000. Roll formed, the line runs at 30 m/min at a rate of 90 per hour, on tooling costing 60,000. Find each route's parts per hour and cost per part, the break-even quantity, and what happens to it if the profile gains two more bends.

Solution. (1) Brake cycle \( =6(6)+20=56\ \mathrm{s} \), so \( 3600/56=64.3 \) parts per hour and \( 60/64.3=0.933 \) per part.

(2) Roll forming: at 30 m/min the line moves 0.5 m/s, so a 3 m part occupies it for 6 s — 600 parts per hour, and \( 90/600=0.150 \) per part. The line is \( 600/64.3=9.33 \) times faster.

(3) \( N^{*}=(60{,}000-4{,}000)/(0.933-0.150)=56{,}000/0.783=71{,}489 \) parts.

(4) Check: at that quantity both routes cost \( 60{,}000+71{,}489(0.150)=70{,}723 \) and \( 4{,}000+71{,}489(0.933)=70{,}723 \). Equal.

(5) Two more bends add \( 12\ \mathrm{s} \) to the brake cycle, making it 68 s: 52.9 parts an hour and 1.134 per part. The roll-formed rate is unchanged, though the tooling rises — say to 70,000 for two more stands. Then \( N^{*}=(70{,}000-4{,}000)/(1.134-0.150)=67{,}073 \) parts: the crossover falls despite the extra tooling.

Answer: 64.3 against 600 parts an hour, 0.933 against 0.150 per part, break-even 71,489; adding two bends lowers the crossover to about 67,000. Sanity check: the direction of the last result is the important one — bends cost time on a brake and only capital on a line, so a more complex profile favours roll forming even though its tooling gets dearer. Note what the number does not include: roll-forming tooling is profile-specific, so the quantity must be reached on one profile, and a changeover costs hours of line time that this arithmetic never sees.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Why the leg edge waves, and how far to spread it

Problem. A 40 mm leg is raised from flat to vertical in a single 400 mm stand pitch, in mild steel with \( Y=250 \) MPa and \( E=207 \) GPa. Find the longitudinal strain forced on the leg edge, compare it with the yield strain, and say what defect results. Then find the forming length that keeps the edge elastic and the number of 300 mm stand pitches it implies.

Solution. (1) While the web travels \( L=400 \) mm along the line, the edge travels the hypotenuse of that length and its own rise: \( \sqrt{L^2+h^2} \).

(2) \( \varepsilon=\sqrt{1+(40/400)^2}-1=\sqrt{1.01}-1=0.004988 \), that is 0.499%.

(3) The yield strain is \( \varepsilon_y=Y/E=250/207{,}000=0.001208 \), or 0.121%.

(4) \( 0.499/0.121=4.13 \): the edge is strained four times past yield, so it lengthens permanently.

(5) A strip edge that is permanently longer than the strip has nowhere to go and buckles — edge wave, the ripple along the free edge of the finished section.

(6) Setting \( \varepsilon=\varepsilon_y \): \( L=h/\sqrt{(1+\varepsilon_y)^2-1}=40/0.04916=813.6\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(7) At a 300 mm stand pitch that is \( 813.6/300=2.71 \), so the leg must be raised over three stands, not one.

Answer: 0.499% against a 0.121% yield strain, 4.1 times over, producing edge wave; the bend needs 814 mm of forming length, three stands at a 300 mm pitch. Sanity check: the strain goes as \( h^2/2L^2 \), so it is far more sensitive to the leg height than to the pitch — doubling the leg to 80 mm would need four times the forming length, about 3.3 m, which is most of a line. That is the real reason deep-legged profiles need many stands, and it is decided on the flower pattern before any roll is cut rather than discovered on the line.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Size the leveller

Problem. A 1.5 mm mild-steel coil (\( Y=250 \) MPa, \( E=207 \) GPa) arrives with coil set. To erase it, the leveller must strain the strip surface to five times its yield strain. Find the maximum leveller roll radius and diameter, and state what happens if larger rolls are used. Then find the roll radius needed for 3 mm sheet at the same criterion, and comment.

Solution. (1) Bending a strip of thickness \( t \) over a radius \( R \) strains its surface by \( \varepsilon=t/2R \).

(2) \( \varepsilon_y=250/207{,}000=0.001208 \), so the target is \( 5\varepsilon_y=0.006039 \).

(3) \( R=t/(2\varepsilon)=1.5/(2\times0.006039)=124.2\ \mathrm{mm} \), a diameter of 248.4 mm. Rolls must be that size or smaller, since a smaller radius gives more strain.

(4) With larger rolls the strain never reaches five times yield, so only the outer fibres yield and the core keeps its memory: the strip leaves the leveller looking flat and retains the residual stress that later shows up as bow or twist in the formed profile.

(5) For 3 mm at the same criterion, \( R=3/(2\times0.006039)=248.4\ \mathrm{mm} \) — the radius scales directly with thickness.

Answer: \( R\le124.2 \) mm (248.4 mm diameter) for 1.5 mm strip, and 248.4 mm radius for 3 mm; larger rolls leave residual stress that reappears as bow or twist. Sanity check: the linear scaling is worth noticing, because it says a leveller built for thin gauge is over-specified for thick and a leveller built for thick gauge simply cannot level thin — which is why lines carry roll cassettes rather than one set. Note also what levelling does not fix: camber from slitting is an in-plane curve, and rolls that bend the strip through its thickness do nothing about it.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

On a roll-forming line, adding two more bends to a profile

The line runs at metres per minute regardless of how many stands it has, so complexity is paid for in capital rather than in time. On a brake the same two bends add 12 s to every cycle. That asymmetry is why extra bends push the break-even quantity down, toward roll forming, even though the tooling gets dearer.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A flower pattern is

It is the roll former's design document: every stand's section drawn on one set of axes, opening progressively. It encodes the angle schedule, the path the material takes — which is where edge strain is checked — and which surfaces stay flat enough to drive the strip.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Raising a 40 mm leg over a 400 mm forming length strains the leg edge about

\( \varepsilon=\sqrt{1+(40/400)^2}-1=0.499\% \). The edge must travel the hypotenuse while the web travels the base, and on a continuous strip that extra length can only come from stretching. Bending is transverse, but roll forming is bending while moving, which is what creates the longitudinal component.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Edge wave in a roll-formed section is caused by

A permanently lengthened edge on a strip of fixed length has nowhere to go and buckles into a ripple. The cure is geometric rather than mechanical: spread the same bend over more forming length. Keeping \( \varepsilon\le\varepsilon_y \) for a 40 mm leg needs 814 mm, or three stands at a 300 mm pitch.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

A leveller erases coil set by

The strip must be worked past yield in both directions until its residual curvature is erased, and that needs \( t/2R \) to reach several times the yield strain: rolls of 248 mm diameter or less for 1.5 mm mild steel. Larger rolls yield only the outer fibres, leaving a strip that looks flat and later bows.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

End flare on roll-formed lengths occurs because

While the strip is continuous, each section is held in shape by the material ahead of and behind it. Cutting removes that, and the free ends spring toward their unrestrained shape. The usual answers are to cut before forming, to trim a short scrap end, or to add a restraining stand close to the shear.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Which of these is not achievable by conventional roll forming?

Every stand sees the same shape pass through it, so the section is constant along the length by construction. Long lengths, in-line punching and nearly closed sections completed by welding are all routine — the last because a fully closed profile would lose the driven flat surfaces the line needs to feed it.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the profile that waved along one edge. A panel line was retooled for a new section with a 40 mm return leg, and the first production run came off with a visible ripple along that leg on every length. The rolls were checked, reset and checked again; the strip supplier was asked to confirm the coil's flatness; nothing changed.

The defect was designed in, not set up wrong. The flower pattern raised the leg from flat to vertical between two stands 400 mm apart, which forces the leg edge to travel \( \sqrt{400^2+40^2} \) while the web travels 400 — a longitudinal strain of 0.499% against a yield strain of 0.121%. The edge was being stretched four times past yield on every metre of strip, so it left the line permanently longer than the material beside it and buckled. No roll setting can absorb that, because the extra length is real. Keeping the edge elastic needs 814 mm of forming length, which at the line's 300 mm stand pitch meant raising the leg over three stands instead of one.

Two practices follow. Check the edge-strain criterion on the flower pattern before any roll is cut — it costs one line of arithmetic per leg and it is the commonest reason a new profile misbehaves. And when a defect appears identically on every part from the first metre onward, treat the design as the suspect rather than the setup: setup errors drift, wander and respond to adjustment, while a geometric impossibility does none of those things.
