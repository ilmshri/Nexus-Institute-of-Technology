Blow molding, thermoforming, and film

Bottles, trays, and films — the packaging trio. Parison control and wall-thickness distribution. Food-grade material and hygiene constraints introduced.

§1 · Blow moulding: the wall is decided before the mould closes

Extrusion blow moulding makes a hollow part in one step. A parison — a tube of melt — is extruded downward from an annular die; the two mould halves close on it, pinching it shut at top and bottom; and air inflates it against the cavity. It is fast, the tooling is cheap compared with injection moulding, and it is how nearly every plastic bottle is made.

The wall thickness, though, is not decided by the mould. It is decided by the parison, because inflation conserves mass.

Key relation — blow-up ratio and wall thinning

\[ \mathrm{BUR}=\frac{D_{\text{bottle}}}{D_{\text{parison}}},\qquad t_{\text{wall}}=\frac{t_{\text{parison}}}{\mathrm{BUR}} \]

A thin-walled tube expanded to a larger diameter keeps the same material per unit length, so its wall thins in proportion to how far it expanded.

A 40 mm parison with a 3.0 mm wall blown into a 100 mm bottle has \( \mathrm{BUR}=2.5 \) and ends with a 1.20 mm wall. Simple enough — until the bottle stops being a cylinder. A real bottle has a narrow neck, a wide body and often a waist, so the blow-up ratio varies along its height, and with a fixed die gap the wall varies inversely.

The diagram works a bottle running from a 30 mm neck through a 100 mm body to a 60 mm waist. At a constant 2.5 mm die gap the body ends at exactly the 1.0 mm target, the waist at 1.67 mm and the neck at 3.33 mm — a threefold variation, with most of the material sitting where the bottle needs it least and the thinnest section carrying the load.

The cure is parison programming: a servo moves the die mandrel during extrusion so the gap tracks the blow-up ratio the melt is about to meet. Here the gap must run from 0.75 mm at the neck to 2.5 mm at the body — a 3.3:1 range, executed over the few seconds the parison takes to extrude. That is the whole answer to why parison control sets bottle wall thickness: the mould only decides the shape, and every gram of wall was allocated before the mould closed.

Two other parison effects have to be managed with it. Sag: the parison hangs under its own weight, so its top thins while its bottom thickens. Swell: the melt is viscoelastic, so it leaves the die larger than the gap, exactly as in Lesson 6 — and both effects change with melt temperature and extrusion rate, which is why blow-moulding process settings are far less transferable between machines than moulders expect.

§2 · Thermoforming: the sheet is the budget

Thermoforming heats a sheet to its softening range and forms it over or into a mould with vacuum, air pressure, or a mechanical plug. The tooling is a fraction of an injection mould's cost and the cycle is short, which is why trays, lids and blister packs are made this way.

The same conservation argument applies, in area rather than in circumference.

Key relation — areal draw ratio

\[ \mathrm{DR}=\frac{A_{\text{part}}}{A_{\text{sheet}}},\qquad \bar t=\frac{t_{\text{sheet}}}{\mathrm{DR}} \]

The sheet's area is fixed by the frame; whatever surface the part needs comes from stretching it, and the average wall thins in proportion.

A 200 × 150 mm tray 60 mm deep needs \( 30{,}000 \) mm² of base plus \( 2(200+150)(60)=42{,}000 \) mm² of wall, so \( \mathrm{DR}=72{,}000/30{,}000=2.4 \), and a 1.2 mm sheet gives a 0.50 mm average wall.

Average is the operative word. The sheet touches the cool mould progressively, and material that has touched stops stretching, so the last regions to arrive — the corners of a deep draw — carry all the remaining strain. A corner drawing at a local ratio of 4.0 instead of the average 2.4 finishes at 0.30 mm, 40% below the average, and that corner is where the tray fails. Hence the practical depth limit of about half the width for straight vacuum forming, and hence plug assist: a plug pushes the sheet's centre down first, carrying material toward the corners before the vacuum finishes the job, which roughly doubles the achievable depth.

§3 · Film: thickness and orientation from the same two ratios

Blown film extrudes a thin tube upward through an annular die and inflates it into a bubble that is cooled, collapsed and wound. Two ratios set everything: the blow-up ratio, how far the bubble expands, and the take-up ratio, how much faster the nip rolls pull than the melt leaves.

Key relation — film thickness

\[ h_{\text{film}}=\frac{h_{\text{die}}}{\mathrm{BUR}\times\mathrm{TUR}} \]

Mass conservation again, now in two directions at once. A 1.2 mm die gap at \( \mathrm{BUR}=2.5 \) and \( \mathrm{TUR}=20 \) gives a 24 µm film.

The two ratios do something else as well, and it is what separates a good film from a bad one. The blow-up stretches the melt across the machine direction and the take-up stretches it along it, so their ratio sets how balanced the film's orientation is. At \( \mathrm{TUR}/\mathrm{BUR}=8 \) the film is strongly machine-oriented: strong and stiff along the web, and easy to tear along it too, because the molecules are aligned to split apart. A film meant to resist tearing is run at a higher blow-up ratio and a lower take-up ratio for the same thickness.

Cast film takes the other route: a flat die onto a chilled roll, which gives better clarity and much better gauge uniformity but essentially no transverse orientation, so it tears easily across the web. Neither is better; they fail differently, and the packaging decides which failure matters.

§4 · Food contact: what actually constrains the material

Packaging that touches food is regulated on what can move out of it, and that constraint reaches back into the resin, the additives and the process.

Key relations — the two migration limits

\[ m\le10\ \mathrm{mg/dm^{2}}\ \text{of contact area},\qquad m\le60\ \mathrm{mg/kg}\ \text{of food} \]

Both apply, so the binding one depends on the pack's surface-to-mass ratio. They coincide at \( A/m=6\ \mathrm{dm^{2}/kg} \); above that the mass limit binds, below it the area limit does.

A 500 ml bottle with 3.5 dm² of contact area has \( A/m=7\ \mathrm{dm^{2}/kg} \), so the mass limit binds: 30 mg of total migration is permitted, not the 35 mg the area limit would allow. Small packs, with more surface per gram of contents, are the demanding case — which is why a single-serve pot is harder to qualify than a drum of the same material.

That limit is what governs the recipe. Plasticisers, slip agents, antioxidants, stabilisers and colourants are all mobile small molecules, so a food-contact grade is chosen as much for what has been left out of it as for its mechanical properties. Recycled content is restricted for the same reason: its history is not known, so what might migrate out of it is not known either.

The second constraint is barrier, and it is a series-resistance problem identical in form to the thermal one. Polyethylene is a good moisture barrier and a poor oxygen barrier; EVOH is an excellent oxygen barrier and is ruined by moisture. So they are coextruded, with the EVOH buried between polyethylene layers, and the permeabilities add as resistances in series:

Key relation — permeation in series

\[ \frac{h_{\text{tot}}}{P_{\text{eff}}}=\sum_i\frac{h_i}{P_i} \]

Five micrometres of EVOH a thousand times less permeable than the polyethylene around it, in a 200 µm wall, cuts oxygen transmission 26-fold — from 2.5% of the wall thickness.

The third constraint is the machine rather than the material: hygienic design, meaning surfaces that can be cleaned and drained, no crevices or dead legs where product can sit, and materials that survive the cleaning chemicals. And the fourth is documentation — resin lot traceability, additive declarations and a declaration of compliance for the finished pack — which is Metrology & Quality Control's say-it, do-it, prove-it obligation appearing in another industry with different paperwork and identical logic.

What this lesson assumes

  • Resistances in series (Heat Transfer L2) — permeation through a multilayer film has the identical algebra.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Programme the parison

Problem. A bottle is blown from a 40 mm parison. Its neck is 30 mm across, its body 100 mm and its waist 60 mm, and the target wall is 1.0 mm everywhere. (a) Find the blow-up ratio at each of the three sections. (b) With the die gap held at 2.5 mm, find the finished wall at each and the ratio between thickest and thinnest. (c) Find the die gap the programmer must command at each section. (d) Say why the neck is the section that suffers.

Solution. (a) \( \mathrm{BUR}=D/40 \): neck \( 30/40=0.75 \), body \( 100/40=2.50 \), waist \( 60/40=1.50 \).

(b) \( t=t_{\text{parison}}/\mathrm{BUR}=2.5/\mathrm{BUR} \): neck \( 2.5/0.75=3.33\ \mathrm{mm} \), body \( 2.5/2.50=1.00 \), waist \( 2.5/1.50=1.67 \). Thickest over thinnest is \( 3.33/1.00=3.33 \).

(c) To land on 1.0 mm the gap must be \( t_{\text{target}}\times\mathrm{BUR} \): neck \( 1.0(0.75)=0.75\ \mathrm{mm} \), body \( 1.0(2.50)=2.50 \), waist \( 1.0(1.50)=1.50 \).

(d) The neck barely expands, so it barely thins, so it keeps almost all the parison's wall. It finishes three times too thick, which is wasted material and a slower cycle — the whole part waits for its thickest section to cool.

Answer: BUR 0.75 / 2.50 / 1.50; fixed-gap walls 3.33 / 1.00 / 1.67 mm, a 3.33:1 range; programmed gaps 0.75 / 2.50 / 1.50 mm; the neck suffers because it expands least. Sanity check: the required gap is simply the target wall times the local blow-up ratio, which means the programme is the bottle's diameter profile scaled — a satisfying result, because it says the mandrel's motion is the bottle's silhouette. Note the two costs of not programming: the extra material is paid on every bottle forever, and the thick neck sets the cooling time for the whole part by the square law of Lesson 7.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Thin a tray, and find where it fails

Problem. A tray 200 × 150 mm and 60 mm deep is thermoformed from 1.2 mm sheet. Find the part's surface area, the areal draw ratio and the average finished wall. Then find the wall at a corner that draws at a local ratio of 4.0, express it as a percentage below the average, and give two ways to improve it.

Solution. (1) Base area \( =200(150)=30{,}000\ \mathrm{mm^2} \).

(2) Wall area \( =2(200+150)(60)=42{,}000\ \mathrm{mm^2} \), so the part surface is \( 72{,}000\ \mathrm{mm^2} \).

(3) The sheet contributes only its own footprint, 30,000 mm², so \( \mathrm{DR}=72{,}000/30{,}000=2.4 \).

(4) Average wall \( =1.2/2.4=0.50\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(5) At a corner drawing 4.0: \( 1.2/4.0=0.30\ \mathrm{mm} \), which is \( 1-0.30/0.50=40\% \) below the average.

(6) Two improvements: plug assist, pushing the sheet's centre down first so material is carried toward the corners before the vacuum finishes, which roughly doubles the achievable depth-to-width; and reducing the draw — a shallower tray, a larger corner radius, or draft on the walls, all of which lower the local ratio directly.

Answer: 72,000 mm² of surface, DR 2.4, average wall 0.50 mm; the corner finishes at 0.30 mm, 40% thin; plug assist or a gentler draw fixes it. Sanity check: the average is a budget and the corner is where the budget is overspent, which is why a thermoformed part is specified on its minimum wall rather than its average. Note that the depth term dominates: the walls contribute 42,000 mm² against the base's 30,000, so halving the depth would take DR from 2.4 to 1.7 and the average wall from 0.50 to 0.71 mm without touching the sheet.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Qualify the pack

Problem. (a) A 500 ml bottle presents 3.5 dm² of contact area to 0.5 kg of food. Given migration limits of 10 mg/dm² of area and 60 mg/kg of food, find which limit binds and the permitted total migration. Find the surface-to-mass ratio at which the two limits coincide. (b) A 200 µm wall is to be built as 195 µm of polyethylene plus 5 µm of EVOH whose permeability is a thousand times lower. Find the reduction in oxygen transmission against a 200 µm monolayer, and the EVOH's share of the wall.

Solution. (a1) Area limit: \( 3.5\times10=35\ \mathrm{mg} \) permitted.

(a2) Mass limit: \( 0.5\times60=30\ \mathrm{mg} \) permitted.

(a3) Both apply, so the smaller binds: 30 mg, set by the mass limit.

(a4) The two coincide when \( 10A=60m \), that is \( A/m=6\ \mathrm{dm^2/kg} \). This pack is at \( 3.5/0.5=7 \), above the crossover, so the mass limit binds — and smaller packs, with more surface per gram, sit further above it.

(b1) Permeation resistances add in series: \( h_{\text{tot}}/P_{\text{eff}}=\sum h_i/P_i \). Writing the polyethylene permeability as \( P \), the structure is \( 195/P+5/(P/1000)=195/P+5000/P=5195/P \).

(b2) A 200 µm monolayer is \( 200/P \).

(b3) The ratio of resistances is \( 5195/200=26.0 \), so transmission falls 26-fold.

(b4) The EVOH is \( 5/200=2.5\% \) of the wall.

Answer: the mass limit binds at 30 mg, and the limits cross at 6 dm²/kg; 2.5% of the wall as EVOH cuts oxygen transmission 26-fold. Sanity check: both results say the same thing in different currencies — a small amount of the right material in the right place dominates the outcome. Note the direction of the pack-size effect: it is the small pack that is hard to qualify, which is counter-intuitive until you notice that surface scales as the square of a length and contents as the cube. And note that the barrier calculation is the thermal series-resistance formula with permeability where conductivity would be, so the intuition transfers whole.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

In extrusion blow moulding the finished wall thickness is set mainly by

Inflation conserves mass, so \( t=t_{\rm parison}/\mathrm{BUR} \). The mould decides the shape; every gram of wall was allocated when the parison was extruded. Blowing pressure only has to be enough to reach the cavity, and more of it does not redistribute material.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

With a fixed die gap, a bottle's neck comes out much thicker than its body because

Thinning is inversely proportional to blow-up ratio, and a 30 mm neck from a 40 mm parison has \( \mathrm{BUR}=0.75 \) — it barely expands at all. On the worked bottle the neck finishes at 3.33 mm against the body's 1.00, a 3.3:1 variation that is wasted material and a longer cycle.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

Parison programming works by

The gap is commanded as \( t_{\rm target}\times\mathrm{BUR}(z) \), so the mandrel's motion is effectively the bottle's diameter profile — 0.75 to 2.5 mm on the worked bottle. Pressure and temperature cannot redistribute material once the parison exists.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

A 200 × 150 mm tray 60 mm deep thermoformed from 1.2 mm sheet has an average finished wall of

The part's surface is \( 30{,}000+42{,}000=72{,}000\ \mathrm{mm^2} \) against a 30,000 mm² sheet footprint, so \( \mathrm{DR}=2.4 \) and the average wall is \( 1.2/2.4=0.50 \) mm. The 0.30 mm figure is the corner, which draws at about 4.0.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

In a deep thermoformed part the thinnest wall is normally

Sheet that touches the cool mould stops stretching, so all the remaining strain is carried by whatever has not touched yet — the corners of a deep draw. At a local ratio of 4.0 against an average 2.4 the corner is 40% thin, which is why the part is specified on minimum rather than average wall, and why plug assist exists.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Blown film from a 1.2 mm die gap at \( \mathrm{BUR}=2.5 \) and \( \mathrm{TUR}=20 \) is

\( h=h_{\rm die}/(\mathrm{BUR}\times\mathrm{TUR})=1.2/50=0.024\ \mathrm{mm} \). The same two ratios also set orientation: at \( \mathrm{TUR}/\mathrm{BUR}=8 \) the film is strongly machine-oriented, so it is stiff along the web and tears easily along it.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A 500 ml pack presents 3.5 dm² to 0.5 kg of food. Against limits of 10 mg/dm² and 60 mg/kg, the permitted migration is

Both apply and the smaller binds: \( 0.5\times60=30 \) mg against the area limit's 35. The two coincide at \( A/m=6\ \mathrm{dm^2/kg} \) and this pack is at 7, so it sits on the mass side — and smaller packs sit further over, which is why a single-serve pot is harder to qualify than a drum.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Five micrometres of EVOH in a 200 µm polyethylene wall, at a thousandth the permeability, cuts oxygen transmission by about

Resistances add in series: \( 195/P+5000/P=5195/P \) against a monolayer's \( 200/P \), so a factor of 26. It is the thermal series-resistance formula with permeability in place of conductivity, and it shows how a 2.5% layer can dominate a structure — provided it is buried where moisture cannot reach it.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the bottle that was lightweighted on the wrong section. A bottle was running 10% over its target weight, and the obvious response was taken: the die gap was reduced until the weight came right. The bottles then failed top-load and drop tests, and the failures were in the body, not the neck — the section that had not been thought about at all.

The gap is a single setting acting on a wall that varies. With a fixed 2.5 mm gap this bottle's neck finished at 3.33 mm and its body at 1.00 mm, because thinning is inversely proportional to the local blow-up ratio and the 30 mm neck barely expands while the 100 mm body expands 2.5 times. The excess weight was almost all in the neck. Reducing the gap uniformly took it out of the body as well, where there was none to spare, so the section carrying the load lost what the section carrying nothing kept.

Two practices follow. Lightweight against the wall distribution, not the total weight: the question is always which section is heavy, and the answer is computable from the diameter profile before any bottle is blown. And where a machine has parison programming, treat the programme as part of the product specification rather than a machine setting — it is the only control that can move material along the bottle, and a single gap number cannot do the job it is being asked to do.
