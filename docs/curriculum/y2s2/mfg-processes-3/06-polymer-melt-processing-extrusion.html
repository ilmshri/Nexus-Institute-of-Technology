Polymer Extrusion - Single Screw Extruder vs. Twin Screw ExtruderPOLYMERWORLD

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • What does die swell reveal about polymer melts?
  • Why is cable insulation extruded over a moving conductor rather than moulded?

§1 · The screw is a pump that melts on the way

A single-screw extruder does three jobs with one rotating part. Solid pellets are dragged forward by the feed section, compacted and melted in the compression section as the channel depth shallows, and pumped at a steady rate through the metering section into the die. Almost all the melting energy is mechanical — the barrel heaters mostly hold temperature rather than supply it — which is why extruder output and melt temperature are coupled to screw speed and cannot be set independently.

The metering section is where the pumping happens, and it does two opposite things at once. The barrel surface moving relative to the screw drags melt forward; the pressure the die imposes pushes melt back down the channel.

Key relations — the screw characteristic

\[ Q_{\text{drag}}=\tfrac12\pi^{2}D^{2}NH\sin\phi\cos\phi,\qquad Q_{\text{back}}=\frac{\pi DH^{3}\sin^{2}\phi}{12\eta L}\,\Delta P \]

so \( Q=A-B\,\Delta P \). Drag flow depends on screw speed and channel depth; back flow depends on the cube of the depth and inversely on viscosity, which is why a shallow metering section makes a stiff pump.

For a 60 mm screw at 60 rev/min with a 3 mm metering depth, a square-pitch helix angle of 17.66° and a melt viscosity of 500 Pa·s, \( A=15.40\ \mathrm{cm^3/s} \) and \( B=1.30\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m^3/(s\,Pa)} \) — so 40 MPa of die pressure costs about a third of the drag flow.

§2 · Where the machine actually operates

The die is the other half of the system, and it behaves like a resistance: the more melt you push through it, the more pressure it takes.

Key relation — the die characteristic and the operating point

\[ Q=\frac{K}{\eta}\Delta P,\qquad K=\frac{\pi R^{4}}{8L_d},\qquad \Delta P^{*}=\frac{A}{B+K/\eta} \]

\( K \) is the die's conductance and \( R^{4} \) is why die size dominates it. Setting the two characteristics equal gives the one pressure and flow at which the machine runs.

Put a \( \varnothing5 \) mm die with a 20 mm land on that screw: \( K=7.67\times10^{-10} \), and the operating point is 9.26 MPa at 14.20 cm³/s — 47.5 kg an hour in polyethylene. Change to a \( \varnothing4 \) mm die and \( K \) falls by \( (4/5)^{4}=0.41 \), so the pressure climbs to 20.3 MPa while the output drops only to 12.76 cm³/s.

That asymmetry — the pressure more than doubles and the output falls a tenth — is the practically important reading of the diagram. A single-screw extruder is a stiff pump, so anything that restricts the flow path shows up in the pressure gauge long before it shows up in the throughput. A blinding screen pack, a die chilling at one edge and a degraded melt all announce themselves the same way, which is why head pressure is the number an extruder operator watches.

§3 · Die swell, and what it tells you about the melt

Extrude through a 4 mm die and the strand that emerges is not 4 mm. It is larger — often 20 to 50% larger — and it got that way in the first few millimetres after the die lip.

Key relation — the swell ratio

\[ B=\frac{D_{\text{extrudate}}}{D_{\text{die}}},\qquad B\approx1+(B_0-1)\,e^{-t/\lambda},\qquad t=\frac{L_d}{v} \]

\( \lambda \) is the melt's relaxation time and \( t \) the residence time in the die land. Swell falls as the melt is given time to forget.

What die swell reveals is that a polymer melt is viscoelastic, not a viscous liquid. In the converging entry to the die the chains are stretched and oriented; in the land they begin to relax; on exit, whatever elastic strain is left recovers, and the extrudate expands. A Newtonian fluid does not do this at all.

Which makes swell a design variable rather than a nuisance. At 14.20 cm³/s through a \( \varnothing5 \) die the melt velocity in the land is 0.72 m/s, so a 20 mm land gives 27.7 ms of relaxation — about a quarter of a 100 ms relaxation time, so most of the elastic strain survives and the swell is 1.38. Lengthen the land to 100 mm and the residence time reaches 138 ms, more than one relaxation time, and the swell falls to 1.13. The other levers work the same way: higher temperature shortens \( \lambda \), higher shear rate raises \( B_0 \), and a long land costs pressure because \( K\propto1/L_d \).

The practical consequence is that the die is never the product size. A 5 mm strand at a swell of 1.25 needs a 4 mm die; and since the die is cut before the material is fully characterised, first articles are a measurement exercise rather than a formality.

§4 · Downstream, and why cable is extruded rather than moulded

What leaves the die is hot, soft and oversize. Everything after it is a line: a sizing device fixing the outside dimension while the skin is still forming, a cooling bath, a haul-off that pulls the product through at a controlled speed, and a cutter or coiler.

The haul-off is a process variable, not just a conveyor. Pulling faster than the melt emerges draws the extrudate down, and the area ratio is the square of the diameter ratio: taking a 5 mm free extrudate to a 3 mm product is a drawdown of \( (5/3)^{2}=2.78 \). So the finished size is set by the die, the swell and the haul-off together, and only one of the three is fixed in steel.

The cooling bath's length is a heat-transfer calculation, not a guess. For a 3 mm strand of thermal diffusivity \( 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2/s} \) cooled in water, the centre reaches within 5% of the bath temperature at a Fourier number of about 0.60, so \( t=0.60R^{2}/\alpha=13.5\ \mathrm{s} \) — which at a line speed of 1 m/s means 13.5 metres of bath. Double the line speed and the bath doubles; that is why extrusion halls are long.

All of which explains the question about cable. Insulation is applied by pulling the conductor through a crosshead die that turns the melt through 90° and wraps it round the moving wire. It is done that way for one decisive reason and two supporting ones. The decisive one is continuity: a cable is kilometres long and every joint in its insulation is an electrical weak point, so a process with a defined part length is disqualified before cost is discussed.

The supporting reasons are rate and control. A 1.4 mm conductor insulated to 2.8 mm carries \( \frac{\pi}{4}(2.8^{2}-1.4^{2})=4.62\ \mathrm{mm^{2}} \) of polymer, so at 300 m/min the line consumes 23.1 cm³/s, or 77 kg an hour — a continuous demand no batch process serves. Our 60 mm extruder at 14.20 cm³/s would run the same cable at 184 m/min, and that arithmetic is the coupling that matters: output and line speed are one variable. A 1% drift in extruder output is a 1% error in wall thickness, which is why the wall is measured continuously in line rather than on samples, and why the insulation wall — not the conductor, which arrives already sized — is what the process actually controls.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Viscosity, laminar flow and pressure-driven flow in a duct (Fluid Mechanics L1 and L7) — the die characteristic is Poiseuille flow with \( K=\pi R^4/8L_d \).
  • Pump and system characteristics meeting at an operating point (Fluid Mechanics L4) — the same construction, with a screw in place of a pump.
  • Transient conduction and the Fourier number (Heat Transfer L3–L4) — the cooling-bath length.
  • Polymer structure, \( T_g \) and viscoelasticity (Materials Science II L8) — why a melt stores elastic strain and swells.
  • Continuous versus batch economics (Lesson 4) — the argument that disqualifies moulding for cable.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Find the operating point, then change the die

Problem. A 60 mm single-screw extruder runs at 60 rev/min with a 3 mm metering depth, a 600 mm metering length, a square-pitch helix angle of 17.66° and a melt viscosity of 500 Pa·s, giving \( A=15.40\ \mathrm{cm^3/s} \) and \( B=1.30\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m^3/(s\,Pa)} \). Find the operating pressure and output with a \( \varnothing5 \) mm die of 20 mm land, then repeat for a \( \varnothing4 \) mm die, and say what the comparison tells you about the machine.

Solution. (1) The die's conductance is \( K=\pi R^{4}/8L_d \). For \( R=2.5 \) mm and \( L_d=20 \) mm, \( K=\pi(2.5\times10^{-3})^{4}/(8\times0.02)=7.67\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m^3} \).

(2) The two characteristics are \( Q=A-B\Delta P \) and \( Q=(K/\eta)\Delta P \). Setting them equal, \( \Delta P^{*}=A/(B+K/\eta) \).

(3) \( K/\eta=7.67\times10^{-10}/500=1.534\times10^{-12} \), so \( \Delta P^{*}=1.540\times10^{-5}/(1.30\times10^{-13}+1.534\times10^{-12}) =9.26\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{Pa}=9.26\ \mathrm{MPa} \).

(4) \( Q=(K/\eta)\Delta P^{*}=1.42\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}=14.20\ \mathrm{cm^3/s} \), which in polyethylene at 930 kg/m³ is 47.5 kg an hour.

(5) For \( R=2.0 \) mm, \( K \) falls by \( (4/5)^{4}=0.410 \) to \( 3.14\times10^{-10} \), so \( K/\eta=6.28\times10^{-13} \) and \( \Delta P^{*}=1.540\times10^{-5}/(7.58\times10^{-13})=20.3\ \mathrm{MPa} \).

(6) \( Q=6.28\times10^{-13}(2.03\times10^{7})=1.276\times10^{-5}=12.76\ \mathrm{cm^3/s} \).

(7) The pressure rose by a factor of 2.19 while the output fell by only 10%.

Answer: 9.26 MPa at 14.20 cm³/s on the \( \varnothing5 \) die; 20.3 MPa at 12.76 cm³/s on the \( \varnothing4 \). The screw is a stiff pump. Sanity check: \( K\propto R^{4} \) makes the die by far the more sensitive half of the system, so a 20% smaller radius is a 59% smaller conductance — and the screw absorbs almost all of that in pressure rather than in flow. That is the practical reading: head pressure is a sensitive early indicator of a blinding screen pack or a chilling die, and throughput is a late one. Note also that neither the screw nor the die sets the output alone; only their intersection does.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Size the die for the strand you want

Problem. A 5.0 mm strand is required. (a) If the melt's swell ratio is 1.25, what die opening is needed? (b) For the extruder above running 14.20 cm³/s through a \( \varnothing5 \) die, find the melt velocity in the land, and use \( B=1+(B_0-1)e^{-t/\lambda} \) with \( B_0=1.5 \) and \( \lambda=100 \) ms to find the swell at a 20 mm land and at a 100 mm land. (c) Give one cost of the longer land.

Solution. (a) The extrudate is \( B \) times the die, so the die must be \( 5.0/1.25=4.0\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(b1) Velocity in the land: \( v=Q/\pi R^{2}=1.42\times10^{-5}/(\pi(2.5\times10^{-3})^{2}) =1.42\times10^{-5}/1.963\times10^{-5}=0.723\ \mathrm{m/s} \).

(b2) A 20 mm land gives \( t=0.020/0.723=27.7\ \mathrm{ms} \), so \( t/\lambda=0.277 \) and \( B=1+0.5e^{-0.277}=1+0.5(0.758)=1.38 \).

(b3) A 100 mm land gives \( t=138\ \mathrm{ms} \), \( t/\lambda=1.383 \), and \( B=1+0.5e^{-1.383}=1+0.5(0.251)=1.13 \).

(c) The land is a resistance: \( K=\pi R^{4}/8L_d \), so five times the land is a fifth of the conductance — the operating pressure rises steeply and the output falls, exactly as in the previous problem's die change.

Answer: a 4.0 mm die for a 5.0 mm strand at \( B=1.25 \); melt velocity 0.723 m/s, swell 1.38 at a 20 mm land and 1.13 at 100 mm; the longer land costs pressure and output. Sanity check: the swell falls toward 1 as the residence time passes one relaxation time, which is the right limit — given long enough the melt forgets its history entirely and there is nothing elastic left to recover. Note what this means for tooling: the die opening depends on the material's relaxation behaviour, the land length and the throughput, none of which is known precisely before the first trial, so a production die is normally cut small and opened.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Design the line around the cable

Problem. A 1.4 mm conductor is to be insulated to a 2.8 mm outside diameter. Find the insulation's cross-sectional area, the volumetric and mass rates at a line speed of 300 m/min in polyethylene at 930 kg/m³, and the line speed the 14.20 cm³/s extruder above would support. Then find the cooling-bath length for a 3 mm strand at 1 m/s, taking \( \alpha=10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2/s} \) and a Fourier number of 0.60.

Solution. (1) \( A=\frac{\pi}{4}(2.8^{2}-1.4^{2})=\frac{\pi}{4}(7.84-1.96)=\frac{\pi}{4}(5.88) =4.62\ \mathrm{mm^{2}} \).

(2) At 300 m/min \( =5 \) m/s: \( Q=4.62\times10^{-6}(5)=2.31\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}=23.1\ \mathrm{cm^3/s} \).

(3) Mass rate \( =2.31\times10^{-5}(930)(3600)=77.3\ \mathrm{kg/h} \).

(4) Our extruder gives \( 1.42\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^3/s} \), so the line speed it supports is \( 1.42\times10^{-5}/4.62\times10^{-6}=3.07\ \mathrm{m/s}=184\ \mathrm{m/min} \).

(5) Cooling: \( t=\mathrm{Fo}\,R^{2}/\alpha=0.60(1.5\times10^{-3})^{2}/10^{-7} =0.60(22.5)=13.5\ \mathrm{s} \).

(6) At 1 m/s that is 13.5 m of bath; at the cable line's 5 m/s it would be 67 m.

Answer: 4.62 mm² of insulation, 23.1 cm³/s and 77.3 kg/h at 300 m/min; the extruder supports 184 m/min; the bath is 13.5 m at 1 m/s. Sanity check: output and line speed are one variable, not two — which is the whole reason a cable line is speed-controlled from the extruder rather than the other way round, and why a 1% drift in output is a 1% wall-thickness error rather than a 1% length error. Note the bath result too: cooling time scales with \( R^{2} \) but bath length scales with speed as well, so doubling the line speed doubles the hall while doubling the strand diameter quadruples the time.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

In a single-screw extruder, most of the energy that melts the polymer comes from

Viscous dissipation and the work of dragging and compacting the solids do most of the melting; the barrel heaters largely hold temperature. That is why melt temperature rises with screw speed and cannot be set independently of output — a coupling that surprises anyone expecting an oven.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

In the screw characteristic \( Q=A-B\Delta P \), the coefficient \( B \) is proportional to

\( Q_{\text{back}}=\pi DH^{3}\sin^{2}\phi\,\Delta P/12\eta L \) — back flow is a pressure-driven flow in a shallow channel, so it goes as \( H^{3} \). A shallow metering section therefore makes a stiff pump whose output barely moves with die pressure, which is usually what an extruder wants.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

An extruder's actual output is set by

The screw's output falls with pressure and the die's rises with it, so exactly one pressure satisfies both: \( \Delta P^{*}=A/(B+K/\eta) \). It is the same construction as a pump meeting a system curve. The haul-off sets the drawdown, not the throughput.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Changing from a \( \varnothing5 \) mm die to a \( \varnothing4 \) mm die on the same screw

\( K\propto R^{4} \), so a 20% smaller radius is a 59% smaller conductance: the pressure goes 9.26 to 20.3 MPa while the output goes 14.20 to 12.76 cm³/s. The screw is a stiff pump, which is why head pressure is a sensitive early warning of a restriction and throughput is not.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Die swell shows that a polymer melt is

Chains stretched and oriented in the die's converging entry recover whatever elastic strain survives the land, so the extrudate expands. A purely viscous fluid shows no swell at all. Everything that gives the melt time to relax — a longer land, a higher temperature, a lower rate — reduces it.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Lengthening the die land from 20 mm to 100 mm takes the swell ratio from 1.38 to 1.13 because

At 0.723 m/s the land time goes from 27.7 to 138 ms against a 100 ms relaxation time, so \( e^{-t/\lambda} \) falls from 0.76 to 0.25. The cost is conductance: \( K\propto1/L_d \), so five times the land is a fifth of the conductance and a much higher operating pressure.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

Cable insulation is extruded rather than moulded principally because

Continuity disqualifies any process with a defined part length before cost is discussed. Rate and control support it — a 2.8 mm cable at 300 m/min consumes 77 kg an hour continuously — but the decisive argument is that a joint in insulation is a defect, not an inconvenience.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

On a cable line, a 1% drift in extruder output produces

Output and line speed are one variable: the wall is whatever the melt rate divided by the line speed makes it. That is why the wall is gauged continuously in line rather than on samples, and why the process controls the insulation rather than the conductor, which arrives already sized.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the die that was cut to the drawing. A cable line was retooled for a new insulation wall, and the crosshead die was cut to the finished outside diameter on the reasonable assumption that the die makes the part. First trials produced insulation consistently oversize by roughly a quarter, the haul-off was increased to pull it down, and the wall then varied along the length as the drawdown fought the swell.

The die was the wrong size by design. A polymer melt is viscoelastic: chains oriented in the die's entry recover on exit, so the extrudate leaves larger than the opening by the swell ratio \( B \), here about 1.25. A die intended to produce a 5.0 mm outside diameter had to be cut at \( 5.0/1.25=4.0 \) mm. Compensating with haul-off instead does something different — it draws the extrudate down while it is still molten, which orients it further and leaves the wall sensitive to every small variation in output, which is exactly the instability that was then observed.

Two practices follow. Size an extrusion die from the product size and the measured swell of that material at that rate and temperature, and treat the first article as the measurement that establishes \( B \) rather than as an inspection. And keep the two levers separate: swell is set by the die geometry and the melt's relaxation, drawdown by the haul-off, and using one to correct the other buys a dimension at the cost of stability.
