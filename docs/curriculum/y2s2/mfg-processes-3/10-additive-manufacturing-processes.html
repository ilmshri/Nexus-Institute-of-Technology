Additive manufacturing processes

PBF, DED, material extrusion, and binder jetting with their materials and defect signatures. Post-processing realities. Where AM fits a maintenance organization: fixtures and unobtainable spares.

§1 · Four families, and what actually separates them

Every additive process builds by adding material in layers. What distinguishes them is how the material is placed and how it is bonded, and those two choices decide everything else — resolution, density, anisotropy, residual stress and what has to happen after the build.

Powder bed fusion spreads a thin powder layer and melts selected regions with a laser or electron beam. It reaches full density and near-wrought properties in metals, which is why it makes functional parts; the melt pool also leaves steep thermal gradients, so parts carry substantial residual stress and need supports that anchor them against it. Directed energy deposition feeds powder or wire into a melt pool on a moving head, so it builds fast and large, adds material to existing parts, and resolves badly — it is a near-net process that assumes machining afterwards. Material extrusion lays down a thermoplastic bead; it is cheap and accessible, and each layer bonds to the last by a weld rather than by being bulk material. Binder jetting prints liquid binder onto powder, so there is no melt pool at all: no residual stress, no supports, but a fragile green part that must be debound and sintered, with the shrinkage that implies.

Those distinctions explain a ranking that surprises people, because the best process for surface finish is not the best for strength. Binder jetting uses the finest powder and never melts it, so it gives the smoothest as-built surfaces; powder bed fusion is coarser as-built because partly melted particles stick to the skin, but it is the only family that reaches near-wrought strength; and material extrusion is worst on both counts. Ranked on finish it is binder jetting, then powder bed fusion, then extrusion; ranked on strength honesty it is powder bed fusion, then binder jetting, then extrusion by a long way.

§2 · Layer height buys two things at once, in opposite directions

Layer height is the one parameter every additive process shares, and it sets both the surface and the schedule.

Key relation — staircase roughness

\[ R_a=\frac{h\cos\theta}{4} \]

\( h \) is the layer height and \( \theta \) the surface's angle to the build plate. The steps form a sawtooth of peak-to-valley \( h\cos\theta \) measured normal to the intended surface, and a sawtooth's \( R_a \) is a quarter of its peak-to-valley.

At a 45° surface: 200 µm layers give \( R_a=35\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), 50 µm give 8.8, and 20 µm give 3.5. Both ends of the curve are degenerate — a vertical wall has no steps and a flat top is a single layer — and the as-built roughness is always worse than the geometry predicts, because partly melted powder and the bead's own profile add to it.

Build time runs the other way. Each layer costs a fixed recoat or travel time plus a scan time proportional to the area, so

Key relation — build time

\[ t=\frac{H}{h}\left(t_{\text{recoat}}+\frac{A_{\text{layer}}}{v\,\text{hatch}}\right) \]

Everything is divided by the layer height, so time is inversely proportional to it while roughness is directly proportional.

A 50 mm tall part with 4,000 mm² of scanned area per layer, a laser at 800 mm/s on a 0.11 mm hatch and 8 s of recoat, takes 45.5 s of scanning per layer. At \( h=50\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) that is 1,000 layers and 14.8 hours; at \( h=100\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) it is 500 layers and 7.4 hours. Doubling the layer height exactly halves the build and exactly doubles the staircase roughness — a clean trade, and the first decision on any build.

The third property layer height does not fix is strength, and this is where honesty matters. Powder bed fusion in metal reaches 95–100% of wrought strength across the layers and 85–95% along the build direction, closing to near-parity after hot isostatic pressing. Binder jetting after sintering reaches 90–97% density and is nearly isotropic. Material extrusion is different in kind: with a bulk polymer at 50 MPa, a printed part might reach 45 MPa in the plane of the layers and 15 MPa across them — a factor of three, because the interlayer bond is a weld between beads that were never simultaneously molten. Orientation on the build plate is therefore a structural decision, not a packing one.

§3 · What each family assumes will happen afterwards

The commonest costing error in additive manufacturing is quoting the print. Post-processing is routinely 30 to 70% of a metal part's cost, and each family assumes a different chain of it.

Powder bed fusion in metal assumes: stress relief on the plate before cutting the part free, because releasing it first lets the residual stress warp it; separation from the plate by wire EDM or bandsaw; support removal, which is manual and often the single largest labour item; hot isostatic pressing if fatigue matters; machining of every feature with a real tolerance, since the process holds roughly \( \pm0.1 \) mm at best; a surface treatment; and internal inspection, usually computed tomography, because porosity cannot be seen.

Powder bed fusion in polymer assumes depowdering, bead blasting and often dyeing or sealing — but no supports, since the surrounding powder holds the part. Material extrusion assumes support removal and, if the surface matters, sanding or vapour smoothing. Directed energy deposition assumes machining, always. And binder jetting assumes the longest chain of all: depowdering a fragile green part, curing, debinding and sintering.

Key relation — designing for sinter shrinkage

\[ L_{\text{green}}=\frac{L_{\text{final}}}{1-s} \]

At \( s=18\% \) linear the green part is 1.22 times the finished size, and the volume shrinks by \( 1-(1-s)^{3}=45\% \). The shrinkage must be compensated in the model and it is not perfectly isotropic, which is what limits binder jetting's accuracy.

§4 · Where additive actually belongs in a plant

Additive manufacturing has near-zero tooling cost and a per-part cost that barely falls with quantity. Every process in this course so far has the opposite shape: expensive tooling, cheap parts. So the crossover sits at low quantities, and it is computable exactly as in Lesson 4.

A bracket printed for 85 a part against one machined for 20 a part on a 3,000 fixture crosses at \( 3{,}000/(85-20)=46 \) parts. Below 46, print it; above, do not. That single number disposes of most of the enthusiasm and most of the scepticism at once.

Two applications in a maintenance organisation clear that bar comfortably. The first is fixtures, jigs, gauges and end-of-arm tooling: quantity one, geometry bespoke to a machine that already exists, loads modest, and lead time the thing that actually matters. The second is unobtainable spares. When a part for out-of-production equipment has a twelve-week lead time and the plant loses 8,000 a day while it waits, printing it in five days saves 79 days — 632,000 — and the part could cost twenty times a conventional one without the arithmetic becoming close.

Three cautions belong with that, and they are what separate a working programme from a press release. Reverse-engineering a legacy part needs its material and heat treatment identified, not just its geometry, and a scan gives only the second. A printed part in a pressure-containing or safety-critical duty needs qualification the plant may not be able to perform, which is Metrology & Quality Control's problem in a new setting. And the anisotropy of \( \S2 \) must be oriented against the actual load — a spare printed in the convenient direction rather than the loaded one is a part that looks right and is three times weaker where it matters.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Choose the layer height

Problem. A part 50 mm tall is built with 4,000 mm² of scanned area per layer, a laser at 800 mm/s on a 0.11 mm hatch spacing, and 8 s of recoat per layer. (a) Find the scan time per layer and the build time at 50 µm and at 100 µm layers. (b) Find the staircase roughness at a 45° surface for each. (c) State the trade in one sentence, and say what the relation predicts for a vertical wall and why the real part will not match it.

Solution. (a1) Scan rate \( =v\times\text{hatch}=800(0.11) =88\ \mathrm{mm^2/s} \), so \( t_{\text{scan}}=4000/88=45.5\ \mathrm{s} \) per layer.

(a2) At \( h=50\ \mu\mathrm{m} \): \( 50/0.05=1000 \) layers, each costing \( 8+45.5=53.5\ \mathrm{s} \), so \( 53{,}455\ \mathrm{s}=14.85\ \mathrm{h} \).

(a3) At \( h=100\ \mu\mathrm{m} \): 500 layers, \( 26{,}727\ \mathrm{s}=7.42\ \mathrm{h} \).

(b) \( R_a=h\cos45^{\circ}/4 \): at 50 µm, \( 50(0.7071)/4=8.8\ \mu\mathrm{m} \); at 100 µm, \( 17.7\ \mu\mathrm{m} \).

(c) Doubling the layer height exactly halves the build and exactly doubles the staircase roughness, because time goes as \( 1/h \) and roughness as \( h \).

(d) At a vertical wall \( \theta=90^{\circ} \), so \( \cos\theta=0 \) and the relation predicts no staircase at all — correctly, since the layers stack directly on one another. The real wall is still rough, because partly melted powder sticks to the skin and the bead has its own profile; the formula gives the geometric contribution only.

Answer: 45.5 s of scan per layer; 14.85 h and 7.42 h; 8.8 and 17.7 µm of staircase \( R_a \); doubling \( h \) halves time and doubles roughness; a vertical wall has no staircase but is not smooth. Sanity check: the two effects are exactly reciprocal because both are linear in \( h \), which makes this the cleanest trade in the process — there is no layer height that is better on both counts, only one that suits the part. Note that the recoat term is what makes tall thin parts expensive: it is paid per layer regardless of how little is scanned, so a 50 mm tall matchstick costs nearly what a 50 mm tall block does.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Orient the part, and compensate the shrinkage

Problem. (a) A polymer with 50 MPa bulk strength is printed by material extrusion, reaching 90% of bulk in the plane of the layers and 30% across them. Give both strengths and the ratio, and say what it means for how the part is placed on the plate. (b) The same geometry is to be binder jetted instead, with 18% linear sintering shrinkage. Find the scale factor for the green part and the volumetric shrinkage. (c) Name the post-processing each of the two routes assumes.

Solution. (a1) In plane: \( 0.9(50)=45\ \mathrm{MPa} \). Across the layers: \( 0.3(50)=15\ \mathrm{MPa} \).

(a2) The ratio is \( 45/15=3.0 \). The weak direction is the build direction, because each layer is welded to the last rather than being continuous material.

(a3) So orientation on the plate is a structural decision: the part must be laid so its principal tensile load runs in the layer plane, not across it. A part oriented for packing density or for support convenience can be three times weaker than the same geometry oriented for load.

(b1) The green part must be larger by \( 1/(1-s) \): \( 1/(1-0.18)=1.22 \), so every dimension is scaled 1.22 times.

(b2) Volumetric shrinkage \( =1-(1-0.18)^{3}=1-0.551=45\% \).

(c) Material extrusion assumes support removal and, if the surface matters, sanding or vapour smoothing. Binder jetting assumes depowdering a fragile green part, curing, debinding and sintering — a far longer chain, and the sintering is where the accuracy is lost, because the shrinkage is not perfectly isotropic.

Answer: 45 and 15 MPa, a ratio of 3, so the load must lie in the layer plane; the green part is scaled 1.22× against a 45% volumetric shrinkage; and the two routes assume completely different post-processing. Sanity check: the 45% volumetric figure is the one that surprises — an 18% linear shrinkage sounds modest until it is cubed, and it is why binder-jetted parts distort in sintering rather than merely getting smaller. Note that both halves of this problem are the same lesson: an additive part's properties and dimensions are consequences of how it was built, so the build decision has to be made with the drawing in hand rather than afterwards.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

When is printing it the right answer?

Problem. (a) A bracket can be printed for 85 a part with no tooling, or machined for 20 a part on a 3,000 fixture. Find the crossover quantity and state the rule it gives. (b) A pump component for out-of-production equipment has a twelve-week lead time. The plant loses 8,000 a day while the equipment is down, and the part could be printed in five days. Find the saving, and comment on how much the printed part's own cost matters. (c) Give three cautions before printing a plant spare.

Solution. (a1) Printing costs \( 85N \); machining costs \( 3000+20N \).

(a2) Equal at \( 85N=3000+20N \), so \( N^{*}=3000/65=46 \) parts.

(a3) The rule: below about 46 parts, print it; above, do not. Additive has near-zero tooling and a per-part cost that does not fall with quantity, which is the opposite shape to every other process in this course.

(b1) Twelve weeks is 84 days; printing takes 5, so 79 days are saved.

(b2) \( 79\times8000=632{,}000 \).

(b3) Against that, the part's own cost is almost irrelevant — it could cost twenty times a conventional one without the arithmetic becoming close. The variable that matters is lead time, not unit cost, which is why the spares case is strong where the production case usually is not.

(c) Three cautions: the legacy part's material and heat treatment must be identified, and a geometric scan gives neither; a pressure-containing or safety-critical duty needs qualification the plant may not be able to perform; and the build direction must be oriented against the real load, or the part is three times weaker exactly where it is loaded.

Answer: 46 parts; 632,000 saved on the spare, against which unit cost hardly signifies; and material identification, qualification and orientation are the three things to settle first. Sanity check: the two parts of this problem point in opposite directions and both are right — additive loses badly on repeat production and wins overwhelmingly on lead time, so the question is never "is printing cheaper" but "what is the cost of waiting". Note that the cautions are not administrative: printing the right geometry in the wrong material or the wrong orientation produces a part that fits, looks correct, and fails.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

Ranked on as-built surface finish, best first, the three families are

Binder jetting uses the finest powder and never melts it, so nothing sticks to the skin. Powder bed fusion is coarser as-built because partly melted particles adhere. Material extrusion is worst, with both the largest layers and a visible bead profile. The ranking on strength is different — powder bed fusion first — which is the point.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

Doubling the layer height from 50 to 100 µm on a fixed part

Build time goes as \( 1/h \) and staircase roughness as \( h \), so the trade is exactly reciprocal: 14.85 h and 8.8 µm become 7.42 h and 17.7 µm. There is no layer height that is better on both counts, only one that suits the part.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

A material-extrusion part in a 50 MPa polymer reaches about 45 MPa in the layer plane. Across the layers it reaches about

Roughly 30% of bulk, because the interlayer bond is a weld between beads that were never simultaneously molten — a factor of three below the in-plane strength. Orientation on the plate is therefore a structural decision, and a part placed for packing density can be three times weaker where it is loaded.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

Metal powder bed fusion parts are stress relieved

The plate is what restrains the part against the residual stress the melt pool built in. Cut it free first and the stress releases as distortion. It is the first item in the post-processing chain, and getting the order wrong scraps the part before anything else can be done.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A binder-jetted part sintering at 18% linear shrinkage loses what fraction of its volume?

\( 1-(1-0.18)^{3}=1-0.551=45\% \). The linear figure sounds modest until it is cubed, and the shrinkage is not perfectly isotropic — which is where binder jetting's accuracy is lost, and why the green part is modelled 1.22 times oversize.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Post-processing on a metal powder-bed part typically accounts for

Stress relief, plate separation, support removal, optional hot isostatic pressing, machining of every real tolerance, surface treatment and internal inspection. Quoting the print alone is the commonest costing error in additive manufacturing, and support removal is often the single largest labour item.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A bracket costs 85 to print with no tooling, or 20 to machine on a 3,000 fixture. The crossover is at

\( 85N=3000+20N \) gives \( N=3000/65=46 \). Additive has near-zero tooling and a per-part cost that does not fall with quantity — the opposite shape to every other process in this course — so it wins at low volume and loses at high.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

Before printing a replacement for an out-of-production plant part, the item most easily overlooked is

A scan gives geometry and nothing else. Printing the right shape in the wrong alloy or condition produces a part that fits, looks correct and fails — and the failure is usually attributed to the process rather than to the missing specification. Qualification and build orientation are the other two things to settle first.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the printed bracket that failed in the direction nobody checked. A maintenance team printed a replacement mounting bracket in a filled polymer to keep a conveyor running while a machined part was on order. It was modelled correctly and printed on the flat, which was the orientation that packed best on the plate and needed the least support. It broke in service inside a shift, at a load well below what the material's datasheet allowed.

The datasheet described the bulk polymer. A material-extrusion part is not bulk polymer: each layer is welded to the one below it by a bond formed between beads that were never simultaneously molten, so with a 50 MPa bulk material a printed part reaches about 45 MPa in the plane of the layers and about 15 MPa across them — a factor of three. Laid flat, the bracket's principal tensile load ran straight across the layers, into the weakest axis it had. Standing it up would have put the same load in the layer plane, cost more support material and a longer build, and produced a part three times stronger.

Two practices follow. Treat build orientation as a structural decision made with the load case in hand, never as a packing or support decision — it is the additive equivalent of the rolling direction in Lesson 2, and it has a larger effect. And when a printed part is used in service, record its orientation with the drawing: the geometry alone does not define the part, and a reprint in a different orientation is a different component.
