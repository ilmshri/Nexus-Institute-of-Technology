Deep drawing and stretch forming

Drawability and the limiting draw ratio; blankholder force and defect map — wrinkling versus tearing. Redraws and ironing. Cans and cookware as engineered products.

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • What limits the drawing ratio in one hit?
  • Wrinkling versus tearing: which control fixes each?

§1 · Two stresses, two failures, one control

Deep drawing pushes a flat blank through a die with a punch, turning a disc into a cup. Follow the material and the mechanics divide cleanly in two. The flange — here the part of the blank still outside the die, not the bent-up leg Lesson 2 called by the same name — is being pulled toward a smaller circumference, so it is in circumferential compression. The wall already drawn in is carrying the whole force of dragging that flange, so it is in tension.

Each of those stresses has its own failure. Compress a thin annulus in its own plane and it buckles: the flange throws up radial wrinkles, which are then dragged into the wall and locked in. Tension the wall past its strength and it tears, always at the same place — just above the punch nose radius, where the section is thinnest and no longer supported by the punch.

One control governs both, in opposite directions. The blankholder clamps the flange between two plates. Increase its force and the flange is held flat, so wrinkling stops — but the flange now has to be dragged out from under a heavier clamp, so the friction adds to the wall tension and tearing arrives sooner. Decrease it and the reverse happens. That is the whole answer to which control fixes which defect: they are the same control, and it has a window rather than a setting.

Everything else in the process is a way of widening that window. Lubricant reduces the friction the blankholder buys. A generous die corner radius reduces the bending and unbending work the wall has to carry. Draw beads add local restraint exactly where a non-round flange would otherwise flow in too freely. And the window narrows as the draw gets deeper, until at some ratio it closes.

§2 · How deep is one draw allowed to be

Three ratios decide whether a cup can be drawn in one hit, and all three are checked before anything is tooled.

Key relations — the feasibility set

\[ \mathrm{DR}=\frac{D_b}{D_p}\le2.0,\qquad r=\frac{D_b-D_p}{D_b}\le0.5,\qquad \frac{t}{D_b}\ge0.01 \]

The first two say the same thing twice — \( r=1-1/\mathrm{DR} \) — and the third is about wrinkling: a blank too thin for its diameter buckles before it draws, whatever the blankholder does.

A \( \varnothing150 \) blank in 2 mm sheet drawn to a \( \varnothing80 \) cup gives \( \mathrm{DR}=1.875 \), \( r=0.467 \) and \( t/D_b=0.0133 \). All three pass, but the first two only just, which is a useful thing to know before the first part is pressed rather than after.

Where does the limit of about 2 come from? The wall has to carry the force that deforms the flange, so the limit is a competition between the work of drawing and the strength of the wall. The ideal work of reducing a blank to a cup gives a limiting ratio of \( e=2.72 \) for a perfectly efficient, frictionless process. Real drawing loses work to friction under the blankholder, to friction over the die radius, and to bending the material into the die and straightening it again, which puts the efficiency around 0.7.

Key relation — the limiting draw ratio

\[ \mathrm{LDR}=e^{\,\eta} \]

With \( \eta\approx0.7 \), \( \mathrm{LDR}=2.01 \) — which is where the rule of thumb comes from. Excellent lubrication and generous radii push \( \eta \) toward 0.8 and the LDR to 2.23.

Material matters too, through normal anisotropy \( \bar R \): the ratio of width strain to thickness strain in a tensile test. A sheet with \( \bar R>1 \) resists thinning more than it resists narrowing, so its wall survives more tension before tearing. Ordinary steel sits near \( \bar R=1 \) and reaches an LDR near 2.0; a dedicated deep-drawing grade at \( \bar R\approx1.8 \) reaches about 2.4. That is a material specification bought specifically to change a process limit, which is unusual enough to be worth remembering.

§3 · Force, redraws, and ironing

The drawing force peaks early in the stroke, when the flange is largest, and falls as the flange is consumed.

Key relations — drawing and blankholder force

\[ F=\pi D_p t\,\mathrm{TS}\left(\frac{D_b}{D_p}-0.7\right),\qquad F_h=0.015\,Y\pi\!\left[D_b^{2}-(D_p+2.2t+2R_d)^{2}\right] \]

The \( -0.7 \) is empirical and absorbs the friction and bending losses; the bracket in \( F_h \) is the flange area still under the blankholder as the draw begins.

For the \( \varnothing80 \) cup: \( F=\pi(80)(2)(340)(1.175)=200.8\ \mathrm{kN} \), which is 22.6 US tons. With an 8 mm die radius the blankholder needs \( F_h=146.3\ \mathrm{kN} \) — 73% of the drawing force, high because this blank is large relative to its punch. The press must supply both at once: 347 kN, 39.0 US tons. A press sized on the drawing force alone is 42% short, which is a specification error rather than a process one.

When one draw is not enough, redraw. Ratios multiply, so a sequence of gentle draws reaches what a single hit cannot: 1.875, then 1.455, then 1.310 takes \( \varnothing150 \) down to \( \varnothing42 \) at an overall ratio of 3.57. The redraws are deliberately gentler because each starts from material the previous draw work-hardened, and if the ductility runs out an intermediate anneal is cheaper than the scrap. Cup height follows from area constancy alone, \( h=(D_b^2-D_p^2)/4D_p \), so the sequence in the diagram runs 50.3, 88.5 and 123.4 mm tall on the same blank.

Ironing is a different operation that looks similar. The gap between punch and die is set smaller than the sheet thickness, so the wall is squeezed and lengthens while its diameter stays put. Volume is conserved in the wall, so \( h'=h\,t/t' \): thinning the 50.3 mm wall from 2.0 to 1.2 mm makes it 83.9 mm tall. This is how a beverage can gets a wall far thinner than its base — the base is never ironed — and it is why an ironed cup has a wall of controlled thickness while a drawn one does not.

One consequence of anisotropy shows up here as scrap. If a sheet's properties vary within its plane, the cup rim draws up unevenly and forms ears, which must be trimmed. The trim allowance is designed into the blank diameter, so planar anisotropy is paid for in material on every part.

§4 · Reading the window

The two failures bound a process window, and both bounds can be estimated rather than discovered on the press. The lower bound is the blankholder pressure below which the flange buckles, and it rises steeply with the draw ratio because a deeper draw means a wider, more slender flange:

Key relation — minimum blankholder pressure

\[ p_{\min}=c\left[(\beta-1)^{3}+\frac{0.5\,D_p}{100\,t}\right]\mathrm{TS}, \qquad c\approx2\text{--}3\times10^{-3} \]

\( \beta=\mathrm{DR} \). The cube is what makes the window close: at \( \beta=1.5 \) the flange needs 0.33 MPa, at 1.875 it needs 0.89, and at 2.2 it needs 1.97.

For the worked cup, 0.89 MPa over a 12,645 mm² flange is 11.2 kN — far below the 146 kN the blankholder formula asks for, and the gap between those two numbers is the window. The upper bound is where the added friction pushes the wall past its strength, and it falls as the draw ratio rises for the same reason the lower bound climbs. Somewhere the two meet, and that meeting point is the LDR.

Four practical corollaries follow. A part that wrinkles and a part that tears are opposite symptoms of the same maladjusted control, so the first diagnostic question is which one appeared, not what to tighten. A die corner radius that is too small tears (excessive bending work) and one that is too large wrinkles (unsupported flange), which is the same trade in a different variable; \( R_d\approx4 \) to \( 10t \) is the usual range. A punch nose radius below about \( 4t \) concentrates the thinning and moves the tear to a predictable place. And because the wall is drawn in tension rather than bent, springback is small in the wall and concentrated in the flange — so a drawn part with a retained flange carries both this lesson's problems and the last one's.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Tensile strength, yield, work hardening and anisotropy (Materials Science I L5, Strength of Materials L2) — the wall's capacity, the redraw's ductility budget and \( \bar R \).
  • Buckling of a compressed member (Strength of Materials L10) — wrinkling is a flange buckling in its own plane.
  • Friction and the effect of lubrication in forming (Manufacturing Processes I L7–L9) — the loss that sets the drawing efficiency.
  • Blanking and the strip layout that produces the disc (Lesson 1) — the blank comes from there, trim allowance included.
  • Springback (Lesson 2) — small in a drawn wall, real in a retained flange.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Can it be drawn in one hit, and what press does it need?

Problem. A \( \varnothing150 \) mm blank in 2 mm sheet (\( \mathrm{TS}=340 \) MPa, \( Y=250 \) MPa) is to be drawn into a \( \varnothing80 \) mm cup over an 8 mm die radius. Check the three feasibility ratios, find the cup height, and find the drawing force, the blankholder force and the total press force, in kilonewtons and US tons.

Solution. (1) \( \mathrm{DR}=150/80=1.875 \), which is at or below 2.0 — passes.

(2) \( r=(150-80)/150=0.467 \), at or below 0.5 — passes.

(3) \( t/D_b=2/150=0.0133 \), at or above 0.01 — passes. All three, but the first two narrowly.

(4) Area is conserved, so \( h=(D_b^2-D_p^2)/4D_p=(22500-6400)/320=50.3\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(5) \( F=\pi D_p t\,\mathrm{TS}(\mathrm{DR}-0.7)=\pi(80)(2)(340)(1.175) =200{,}811\ \mathrm{N}=200.8\ \mathrm{kN} \).

(6) \( D_p+2.2t+2R_d=80+4.4+16=100.4 \), so \( F_h=0.015(250)\pi[22500-10080]=146{,}318\ \mathrm{N}=146.3\ \mathrm{kN} \).

(7) Total \( =200.8+146.3=347.1\ \mathrm{kN} \), which is \( 347{,}128/4.4482/2000=39.0 \) US tons. The drawing force alone would be 22.6 US tons.

Answer: feasible in one draw; \( h=50.3 \) mm; 200.8 kN drawing plus 146.3 kN blankholder, 347.1 kN or 39.0 US tons total. Sanity check: the blankholder is 73% of the drawing force here, which is high but expected — the flange area is largest exactly when the draw ratio is near its limit. A press specified on the 22.6 tons of drawing force would be 42% short, and it is a specification error rather than a process one, so it survives every trial on paper. Note also how close the ratios are to their limits: at \( \varnothing75 \) instead of 80 the DR would be 2.0 and one hit would be marginal.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Three draws, and then ironing

Problem. The same \( \varnothing150 \) blank is to become a \( \varnothing42 \) cup. Show that one draw cannot do it, propose a three-stage sequence through \( \varnothing80 \) and \( \varnothing55 \), and give each stage's ratio and each cup's height. Then find how tall the first cup's wall becomes if it is ironed from 2.0 mm to 1.2 mm, and say why the base is unaffected.

Solution. (1) One hit would need \( \mathrm{DR}=150/42=3.571 \), far above the limit of about 2 — impossible.

(2) Stage 1: \( 150\to80 \), \( \mathrm{DR}=1.875 \). Stage 2: \( 80\to55 \), \( \mathrm{DR}=1.455 \). Stage 3: \( 55\to42 \), \( \mathrm{DR}=1.310 \).

(3) Ratios multiply: \( 1.875\times1.455\times1.310=3.571 \), the overall reduction required.

(4) Heights from area constancy, all referred to the original blank: \( h=(22500-D_p^2)/4D_p \). At 80: 50.3 mm. At 55: 88.5 mm. At 42: 123.4 mm.

(5) The redraws are deliberately gentler than the first because each starts from material the previous draw work-hardened; if ductility runs out, an intermediate anneal restores it.

(6) Ironing conserves the wall's volume while holding its diameter, so \( h't'=ht \) and \( h'=50.3(2.0/1.2)=83.9\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(7) The base never passes between punch and die, so it keeps its original 2 mm.

Answer: one draw is impossible at DR 3.57; three stages of 1.875, 1.455 and 1.310 give cups 50.3, 88.5 and 123.4 mm tall; ironing the first cup's wall to 1.2 mm makes it 83.9 mm tall with a 2 mm base. Sanity check: the heights had to grow as the diameters shrink, because the same disc of material is being redistributed — and they can be computed from the diameter alone without knowing anything about the tooling, which is what makes area constancy so useful early in a design. Note what ironing buys and costs: a wall of controlled thickness far thinner than the base, at the price of a separate operation and much higher wall tension, which is why an ironed cup is drawn first and ironed second rather than both at once.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Where the window is, and why it closes

Problem. Using \( p_{\min}=c[(\beta-1)^3+0.5D_p/100t]\mathrm{TS} \) with \( c=3\times10^{-3} \), \( D_p=80 \) mm, \( t=2 \) mm and \( \mathrm{TS}=340 \) MPa, find the minimum blankholder pressure at drawing ratios of 1.5, 1.875 and 2.2. Convert the 1.875 case to a force on the flange. Then use \( \mathrm{LDR}=e^{\eta} \) to find the limiting ratio at efficiencies of 0.7 and 0.8, and say what physically raises \( \eta \).

Solution. (1) The bracket's second term is fixed: \( 0.5(80)/(100\times2)=0.20 \).

(2) \( \beta=1.5 \): \( (0.5)^3+0.2=0.325 \), so \( p_{\min}=3\times10^{-3}(0.325)(340)=0.33\ \mathrm{MPa} \).

(3) \( \beta=1.875 \): \( (0.875)^3+0.2=0.870 \), so \( p_{\min}=0.89\ \mathrm{MPa} \).

(4) \( \beta=2.2 \): \( (1.2)^3+0.2=1.928 \), so \( p_{\min}=1.97\ \mathrm{MPa} \) — six times the 1.5 case, from a cube.

(5) Flange area \( =\frac{\pi}{4}(150^2-80^2)=12{,}645\ \mathrm{mm}^2 \), so at \( \beta=1.875 \) the minimum hold is \( 0.887\times12{,}645=11.2\ \mathrm{kN} \).

(6) \( \mathrm{LDR}=e^{0.7}=2.01 \) and \( e^{0.8}=2.23 \).

(7) \( \eta \) rises with anything that removes lost work: better lubrication under the blankholder and over the die radius, a more generous die corner radius (less bending and unbending), and a lower blankholder force — which is available only if wrinkling allows.

Answer: 0.33, 0.89 and 1.97 MPa; 11.2 kN of hold on the flange at DR 1.875; LDR 2.01 at \( \eta=0.7 \) and 2.23 at 0.8. Sanity check: the cube is the whole story of why deep draws are hard — the floor of the window climbs steeply while its ceiling falls, and the two meet at the LDR. Note the direction of each cure: everything that raises \( \eta \) also lowers the friction the blankholder buys, so lubrication widens the window from both sides at once, which is why it is the cheapest fix on the list and the first one to check when a drawn part starts failing.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

Wrinkles in a drawn cup originate in

The flange is being pulled toward a smaller circumference, so it is compressed in its own plane and buckles like any slender compressed member. The wall is in tension and fails the other way, by tearing just above the punch nose radius where the section is thinnest and no longer supported.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

A drawn cup is tearing near the punch nose. The first correction to try is

Tearing means the wall is carrying too much tension, and the blankholder is what puts most of it there through friction. Reducing the hold or lubricating better both lower it. Increasing the hold is the cure for the other failure, and a smaller die radius adds bending work and makes tearing worse.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

The one-hit limiting draw ratio of about 2 comes from

A perfectly efficient process would reach \( e=2.72 \); friction under the blankholder and over the die radius, plus bending and unbending into the die, bring the efficiency to about 0.7, giving \( e^{0.7}=2.01 \). Excellent lubrication and generous radii reach \( \eta=0.8 \) and an LDR of 2.23.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

A \( \varnothing150 \) blank is drawn to a \( \varnothing80 \) cup. Its height is about

Area is conserved, so \( h=(D_b^2-D_p^2)/4D_p=(22500-6400)/320=50.3 \) mm. The same relation gives 88.5 mm at \( \varnothing55 \) and 123.4 mm at \( \varnothing42 \) — a useful check that needs nothing about the tooling.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

A \( \varnothing42 \) cup from a \( \varnothing150 \) blank requires an overall ratio of 3.57. This is achieved by

1.875 × 1.455 × 1.310 = 3.571. Ratios multiply, and the redraws are deliberately gentler because each starts from material work-hardened by the last; an intermediate anneal restores ductility if it runs out. Ironing changes wall thickness, not diameter.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Ironing a cup wall from 2.0 mm to 1.2 mm at constant diameter makes the wall

The wall's volume is conserved at constant diameter, so \( h'=h\,t/t' =h(2.0/1.2)=1.67h \) — 50.3 mm becomes 83.9 mm. The base never passes between punch and die, so it keeps its original thickness, which is exactly how a beverage can has a wall far thinner than its bottom.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

Normal anisotropy \( \bar R \) above 1 raises the limiting draw ratio because the sheet

\( \bar R \) is the ratio of width strain to thickness strain. A sheet that prefers to narrow rather than thin keeps a thicker, stronger wall for the same draw, so it tears later: \( \bar R\approx1 \) gives an LDR near 2.0 and a dedicated deep-drawing grade at 1.8 reaches about 2.4. Planar variation in \( \bar R \) is a separate problem — it makes ears.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

The minimum blankholder pressure rises with \( (\beta-1)^3 \). The consequence is that

The floor of the window climbs as a cube — 0.33 MPa at \( \beta=1.5 \), 0.89 at 1.875, 1.97 at 2.2 — while the ceiling set by tearing falls, and where they meet is the LDR. It is why a draw at 1.9 is a different proposition from one at 1.5, and why lubrication, which widens the window from both sides, is the first thing to check.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the press that was sized on the wrong force. A shop quoted a drawn stainless cup, \( \varnothing80 \) from a \( \varnothing150 \) blank in 2 mm sheet, and sized the job for its 25-tonne press on a drawing-force calculation of about 22.6 US tons. On the first production attempt the press stalled part-way down the stroke, and the parts that did come out carried heavy wrinkles in the flange.

Both symptoms came from the same omission. The blankholder force is carried by the same press stroke as the drawing force, and for this geometry it is \( 146 \) kN against the drawing force's \( 201 \) kN — a total of 347 kN, or 39.0 US tons. The press had 64% of what the job needed. What happened next made it worse: with the press struggling, the blankholder pressure was reduced to get the stroke to complete, which took the flange below the \( 0.89 \) MPa that \( \beta=1.875 \) requires and produced exactly the wrinkles that were then blamed on the material.

Two practices follow. Size a drawing press on the sum of the drawing and blankholder forces, because they act together and the blankholder can exceed half the total on a blank this large relative to its punch. And treat a blankholder setting as a process parameter with a floor and a ceiling, both computable before the job runs — reducing it to rescue a stroke is trading a tearing margin for a wrinkling failure, and doing it at the machine hides the real problem, which was a press two-thirds the size the part needed.
