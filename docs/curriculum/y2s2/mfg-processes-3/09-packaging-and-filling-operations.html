Packaging and filling operations

Filling, capping, labeling, date-coding, and case packing as a machine system; line speed, accumulation, and the V-curve logic that protects the filler. Hygienic design and changeover discipline. The KDD-style dairy line read as one machine.

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Why is the filler the protected center of a packaging line, and what does the V-curve of speeds around it do?
  • Name two hygienic-design features that separate food-line equipment from generic machinery.
  • Why does changeover reduction (SMED) buy more capacity on packaging lines than almost anywhere else?

§1 · A line is one machine with many ways to stop

A packaging line is a sequence: depalletiser, rinser, filler, capper, labeller, date coder, case packer, palletiser. Each is a competent machine on its own. Rigidly coupled, they are a single machine that stops whenever any one of them does, and the arithmetic of that is unforgiving.

Key relation — machines in series

\[ A_{\text{line}}=\prod_i A_i \]

With no buffer between them, every stoppage anywhere stops everything. Eight machines at 98% availability give \( 0.98^{8}=85\% \); at 95% each they give \( 0.95^{8}=66\% \).

Two conclusions follow immediately. Adding a station is never free — a ninth machine at 98% costs another 2% of the line whatever it does. And chasing the availability of one machine from 97 to 98% is worth far less than it looks, because the product is dominated by whichever machine is worst.

The engineering answer is accumulation: conveyor buffers that hold containers between machines so a short stoppage in one is absorbed rather than propagated. The buffer needed is simply rate times duration. A filler running 600 bottles a minute needs 600 containers of accumulation to absorb a one-minute capper stop, and at an 80 mm bottle pitch that is 48 metres of single-file conveyor — which is why lines carry mass-flow tables and spiral accumulators instead of straight runs, and why the accumulation is often the largest thing in the hall.

§2 · The V-curve, and which machine gets protected

Accumulation only helps if the machines around it are capable of catching up. That requirement is what shapes the whole line, and it produces the pattern every packaging engineer draws first.

Key rule — the V-curve

The filler is rated slowest, and every other machine is rated faster, by more the further it sits from the filler. Plotted in line order, the rated speeds form a V with the filler at its lowest point.

On a 600 bottles-a-minute line: rinser 660, depalletiser 720 upstream; capper 660, labeller 700, coder 740, case packer 780 downstream — 10% at the neighbours rising to 20–30% at the ends.

The logic is directional. Upstream, a faster machine keeps the accumulation ahead of the filler full, so the filler is never starved. Downstream, a faster machine keeps the accumulation behind it empty, so the filler is never blocked. Both conditions say the same thing: the filler runs.

Why the filler? Because it is the most expensive machine on the line, the one whose hygiene requirements are strictest, the hardest to change over, and usually the one whose speed the whole investment was sized around. Protecting it converts the line's availability from the product of everyone's toward the filler's own — from 85% to something approaching 98%, a factor of 1.15 in output for no extra filler at all.

The honest qualification is that accumulation is finite, so real lines land between the two figures, and the gap is closed by buffer size rather than by machine quality. Measured properly, the result appears as overall equipment effectiveness: availability times performance times quality. A line at 0.90 × 0.95 × 0.995 is at 85.1%, so a nominal 600 bottles a minute delivers 510 — and the three factors are worth separating, because they have entirely different cures.

§3 · Hygienic design: what makes food machinery different

A packaging machine touching food is built to rules that ordinary machinery is not, and the rules are all consequences of one requirement: everything that touches product must be cleanable without being dismantled.

Drainability. No horizontal surfaces that hold liquid, no upward-facing channels, frames sloped rather than flat-topped. Water that cannot leave is water that grows something.

No dead legs. A blind branch off a pipe is not swept by the cleaning flow, and product sits in it. The rule of thumb is that a branch may not exceed about 1.5 pipe diameters; a 100 mm branch on a 50 mm line is \( L/D=2.0 \) and fails it. Dead legs are the commonest hygienic-design defect, because they arrive later — as an added instrument tapping, a capped-off future connection, a pressure gauge on a stub.

Crevice-free construction and surface finish. Joints are welded and ground rather than bolted; seals sit in grooves the flow can sweep. Product-contact surfaces are specified at \( R_a\le0.8\ \mu\mathrm{m} \), and the reason is the one Metrology & Quality Control gave: a surface is a profile, not a number. At \( R_a\ 3.2\ \mu\mathrm{m} \) the valleys are four times deeper, and bacterial cells are of order 1 µm — comfortably smaller than the valleys they would be sheltering in, and beyond the reach of the cleaning flow.

Key relation — cleaning in place

\[ v\ge1.5\ \mathrm{m/s}\ \text{in product pipework}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho vD}{\mu}\ \text{well into turbulence} \]

A 50 mm line at 1.5 m/s carries 10.6 m³/h at \( \mathrm{Re}=75{,}000 \). The turbulence is the point: laminar flow slides past a soiled wall, and only turbulent flow scours it.

Cleaning itself trades four variables — time, temperature, chemical concentration and mechanical action — so a shortfall in one is bought back with another. A CIP cycle that cannot reach 1.5 m/s in some branch has lost its mechanical action there, and no amount of caustic compensates, because the caustic never arrives.

§4 · Changeover: where the capacity actually is

Packaging lines run many product variants — sizes, flavours, labels, languages — and each change means new format parts at almost every machine: infeed screws and starwheels sized to the bottle, capping chucks, label plates, case-packer grippers. A changeover is therefore not one task but a dozen parallel ones, and the line is down for all of them.

That is why changeover reduction pays better here than almost anywhere else, and the method is SMED: separate the work that genuinely requires the line stopped — internal — from the work that does not — external — then convert as much internal to external as possible.

Fetching format parts, staging them at each machine, pre-setting adjustable stops on a bench, pre-heating a sealing jaw: all external, all commonly done with the line stopped because nobody separated them. A 120-minute changeover with 70 minutes of that character becomes 50 minutes by moving the work off the critical path, and 35 with the remaining internal work streamlined — quick-release clamps instead of bolts, colour-coded and captive parts, and settings that are positioned rather than adjusted.

Key relations — what a changeover is worth

\[ \Delta t\times n_{\text{changeovers}}\times\text{rate}\ \text{bottles},\qquad \mathrm{EOQ}\propto\sqrt{S} \]

The first is the capacity released. The second is the quieter prize: batch size scales as the square root of setup cost, so a cheaper changeover makes smaller batches economic.

At two changeovers a day, 120 minutes down to 35 releases 170 minutes — 102,000 bottles a day, and 17.7% of a 960-minute working day. And because the economic batch size goes as \( \sqrt{S} \), cutting the setup from 120 to 35 minutes makes economic batches \( \sqrt{35/120}=0.54 \) of their former size: 46% smaller, with the inventory, the warehouse space and the obsolescence risk falling with them. That second effect is usually worth more than the first and is almost never in the business case.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Why the line is slower than any machine on it

Problem. A bottling line has eight machines in series with no accumulation. (a) Find the line availability if each machine is 98% available, and if each is 95%. (b) A ninth machine is added at 98%; what does the line become? (c) With perfect accumulation protecting the filler, the line would tend toward the filler's own 98%; express the gain over part (a) as a factor. (d) Find the accumulation needed, in containers and in metres of single-file conveyor at an 80 mm pitch, to absorb a 60-second stoppage on a 600 bottle-a-minute line.

Solution. (a) Independent machines in series multiply: \( 0.98^{8}=0.851 \), so 85.1%. At 95% each, \( 0.95^{8}=0.663 \) — 66.3%.

(b) \( 0.98^{9}=0.834 \), so the ninth machine costs about 1.7 points whatever it does. Adding a station is never free.

(c) \( 0.98/0.851=1.152 \) — a 15% gain in output from buffering alone, with no better machines.

(d) 600 bottles a minute is 10 a second, so a 60-second stop needs \( 10\times60=600 \) containers of buffer. At 80 mm pitch that is \( 600\times0.08=48\ \mathrm{m} \) of single-file conveyor.

Answer: 85.1% and 66.3%; a ninth machine takes it to 83.4%; buffering is worth a factor of 1.15; and 600 containers, 48 m of conveyor, absorbs a one-minute stop. Sanity check: the product is dominated by the worst machine, so raising a good machine from 97 to 98% is worth far less than fixing a poor one — which is why line improvement starts with a stoppage Pareto rather than with the newest equipment. Note the size of the buffer answer too: 48 m of conveyor for one minute of protection is why accumulation is usually the largest thing in the hall and why it is designed as mass-flow tables and spirals rather than straight runs.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Lay out the V-curve, then read the OEE

Problem. A line is built around a 600 bottle-a-minute filler. (a) Give sensible rated speeds for a rinser and depalletiser upstream and a capper, labeller, coder and case packer downstream, and say what rule you applied. (b) Explain, separately for upstream and downstream, why each machine must be faster. (c) The line runs at 90% availability, 95% performance and 99.5% quality; find the OEE and the actual output in bottles a minute.

Solution. (a) Rate every machine above the filler, by more the further it sits from it: rinser 660 and depalletiser 720 upstream; capper 660, labeller 700, coder 740 and case packer 780 downstream. That is roughly +10% at the neighbours rising to +20–30% at the ends.

(b1) Upstream, a faster machine keeps the accumulation ahead of the filler full, so the filler is never starved.

(b2) Downstream, a faster machine keeps the accumulation behind the filler empty, so the filler is never blocked.

(b3) Both conditions have the same purpose: the filler runs. It is protected because it is the most expensive machine, the most hygiene-critical, the hardest to change over and the one the investment was sized around.

(c) \( \mathrm{OEE}=0.90\times0.95\times0.995=0.851 \), so \( 600\times0.851=510 \) bottles a minute.

Answer: 720 / 660 / 600 / 660 / 700 / 740 / 780 bpm; upstream prevents starving and downstream prevents blocking; OEE 85.1%, so 510 bpm actual. Sanity check: the V is not symmetric in purpose even though it looks symmetric on the chart — the two sides solve opposite problems, and a line built with the downstream machines merely matched to the filler will block it every time a case packer hiccups. Note that the OEE of 85.1% happens to equal the eight-machine series product from the previous problem: the losses are the same losses, counted once in machine terms and once in time terms.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Price a changeover, and then the batch

Problem. A line changes over twice a day, taking 120 minutes each time. A SMED study finds 70 minutes of the work is external in character — fetching, staging, pre-setting — and streamlining the rest brings the internal work to 35 minutes in total. (a) Find the daily minutes released and the extra bottles at 600 a minute, and express it as a percentage of a 960-minute working day. (b) Economic batch size goes as the square root of setup cost; find the change in economic batch size. (c) Say why changeover reduction pays better on packaging lines than on most single machines.

Solution. (a1) Saving per changeover: \( 120-35=85 \) minutes.

(a2) Two a day: \( 2\times85=170 \) minutes released.

(a3) At 600 bottles a minute, \( 170\times600=102{,}000 \) bottles a day.

(a4) As a share of the day: \( 170/960=17.7\% \) more running time.

(b) \( \mathrm{EOQ}\propto\sqrt{S} \), so the batch scales by \( \sqrt{35/120}=\sqrt{0.292}=0.540 \) — economic batches become 46% smaller, taking inventory, warehouse space and obsolescence risk with them.

(c) A packaging line is a dozen machines each needing its own format parts — infeed screws, starwheels, capping chucks, label plates, case grippers — so a changeover is many parallel tasks with the whole line down for all of them, and lines typically run many variants. A single machine changing one tool has neither property.

Answer: 170 minutes and 102,000 bottles a day, 17.7% of the day; economic batches 46% smaller; and lines pay best because the changeover is many simultaneous tasks across many variants. Sanity check: the two prizes are of different kinds and only the first usually reaches the business case — the released capacity is visible on a production report, while the smaller batch shows up as inventory that was never built and is therefore invisible. Note the SMED sequence matters: converting internal work to external is free capacity, whereas streamlining the remaining internal work costs engineering, so the conversion is always done first.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the faster filler that produced no more bottles. A line running below target was upgraded with a new filler rated 15% higher, on the reasoning that the filler sets the line's speed. Output rose by about 2%. The capital had bought almost nothing.

The filler had never been the constraint. The line's machines were rigidly coupled with almost no accumulation, so its availability was the product of theirs — eight machines around 98% giving about 85% — and the losses were short, frequent stops scattered across the capper, the labeller and the case packer, none of which the filler could do anything about. Worse, the downstream machines had been rated to match the old filler rather than to exceed it, so once the new filler ran faster it was blocked by its own capper: the V-curve had been flattened, and a flat curve means every downstream hiccup reaches the filler immediately.

Two practices follow. Diagnose a line from a stoppage Pareto before buying capacity: the machine to improve is the one causing the stops, and it is rarely the one whose nameplate speed is lowest. And treat the V-curve and the accumulation as part of the specification when any machine is replaced — a faster filler obliges every machine downstream of it to be faster still, and buffers to be resized for the new rate, or the upgrade buys a bottleneck rather than removing one.
