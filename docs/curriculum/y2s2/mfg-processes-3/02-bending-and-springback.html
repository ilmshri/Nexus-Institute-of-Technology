Bending and springback

Bend allowance from neutral-axis geometry; bending force; and springback — why it happens, how much to expect, and the compensation strategies press brakes actually use. Kirby's roll-formed and brake-formed building panels as the standing example.

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Compute bend allowance for a 90° bend, R = 6 mm, t = 3 mm.
  • Springback scales with which material ratio — and why does high-strength steel spring back more than mild steel?
  • V-die bending force: what happens to required tonnage when sheet thickness doubles?

§1 · The flat length is not the sum of the legs

Bend a strip and the outer surface stretches while the inner one is compressed. Somewhere between them is a surface whose length does not change — the neutral axis — and the flat blank has to be cut to the length of that, not to the sum of the finished legs. Get it wrong and every part is short or long by a few millimetres, consistently, on a tool that is otherwise perfect.

The neutral axis does not sit at mid-thickness. Material thins on the tensile side more than it thickens on the compressive side, so the neutral surface migrates inward, and the migration depends on how sharp the bend is. Shops carry that in a single factor.

Key relation — bend allowance

\[ \mathrm{BA}=2\pi\frac{\alpha}{360}\bigl(R+K t\bigr),\qquad K\approx0.33\ (R<2t),\qquad K\approx0.50\ (R\ge2t) \]

\( \alpha \) is the swept bend angle, \( R \) the inside radius, \( t \) the thickness. \( K \) is where the neutral axis sits, as a fraction of the thickness measured from the inside surface.

For a 90° bend with \( R=6 \) mm in \( t=3 \) mm sheet, \( R/t=2 \) so \( K=0.50 \), and \( \mathrm{BA}=2\pi(0.25)(6+1.5)=11.78\ \mathrm{mm} \). Draughtsmen more often work with the bend deduction, because parts are dimensioned to their outside faces:

Key relation — bend deduction

\[ \mathrm{BD}=2(R+t)\tan\frac{\alpha}{2}-\mathrm{BA} \]

Flat length = (sum of outside dimensions) − (one BD per bend). The first term is the outside setback: how far the outside faces would run past each other if the corner were sharp.

Here \( \mathrm{BD}=2(9)(1)-11.78=6.22\ \mathrm{mm} \). A U-channel with outside dimensions 50 + 80 + 50 = 180 mm and two 90° bends therefore develops to \( 180-2(6.22)=167.56\ \mathrm{mm} \) of flat blank. Use \( K=0.33 \) by mistake — the value for a tight bend, on a bend that is not tight — and the blank comes out 165.96 mm: 1.6 mm short on a part nobody would suspect, because both bends and both legs are individually correct. The arithmetic is unit-blind, so the same bend at \( R=0.25 \) in and \( t=0.125 \) in gives \( \mathrm{BA}=0.4909 \) in, which is the 12.47 mm the metric calculation would have given.

§2 · Springback: the bend that opens after the punch lifts

Bending strains the outer fibres past yield, but not all of the strain is plastic. When the load comes off, the elastic component recovers and the bend opens: the angle decreases and the radius grows. This is springback, and it is not a defect — it is elasticity doing what elasticity does.

Key relation — springback

\[ \frac{R_i}{R_f}=4\left(\frac{R_i Y}{E t}\right)^{3}-3\left(\frac{R_i Y}{E t}\right)+1 \]

Everything material-dependent enters through \( Y/E \) — yield strength over elastic modulus. A stronger material springs back more; a stiffer one springs back less; and the two effects are of equal weight.

That answers the question shops ask most often. High-strength steel springs back more than mild steel not because it is "harder" but because it has the same \( E \) and nearly three times the \( Y \): at \( R/t=10 \), a 90° bend recovers 3.3° in mild steel and 9.1° in high-strength steel. Aluminium 6061-T6 has a lower yield than that high-strength steel — 275 MPa against 700 — and springs back further still, 10.7°, because its modulus is 69 GPa against steel's 207. Ranking materials by strength predicts springback badly; ranking them by \( Y/E \) predicts it correctly.

Note the other variable in the bracket: \( R_i/t \). Springback grows with it, which is why the same material behaves quite differently in different operations. A tight bend at \( R/t=2 \) barely moves; a wide air bend at \( R/t=20 \) recovers 6.5° in mild steel and 21.3° in aluminium — a quarter of the angle asked for.

Four responses exist and shops use all of them. Overbending is the cheapest: bend past the target by the predicted recovery, so a mild-steel bend aimed at 90° is formed to \( 90/0.9638=93.4^{\circ} \). Bottoming presses the sheet fully into the die so the bend zone yields through its thickness, and coining goes further and sets the radius plastically — both reduce springback and both cost force, coining several times more than air bending. Stretch forming superimposes tension so the whole section is plastic, nearly eliminating springback, and is why aircraft skin panels are formed that way. And an adaptive press brake measures the angle mid-stroke and corrects, which is the only one of the four that copes with sheet whose yield varies from coil to coil.

§3 · Force, and a question with two right answers

V-die bending force comes from treating the sheet as a beam loaded at the punch and supported at the die shoulders.

Key relation — bending force

\[ F=\frac{K_{bf}\,\mathrm{TS}\,w\,t^{2}}{D} \]

\( w \) is the length of the bend, \( D \) the die opening, and \( K_{bf}\approx1.33 \) for a V-die or 0.33 for a wiping die. The thickness appears squared and the die opening divides.

For a 1 m bend in 3 mm steel of 340 MPa with the usual \( D=8t=24 \) mm opening, \( F=1.33(340)(1000)(9)/24=169.6\ \mathrm{kN} \) — 19.1 US tons per metre of bend, or 5.8 tons per foot.

Now the question worth being careful with: what happens when the sheet thickness doubles? If the die opening is left alone, force goes as \( t^2 \) and quadruples to 678 kN. But nobody leaves the die opening alone — it is chosen as a multiple of thickness, so \( D=8t \) grows too, and \( F\propto t^2/t=t \): the force merely doubles, to 339 kN. Both answers are correct and they differ by a factor of two, so the question is incomplete until someone says whether the tooling changed. It is also the reason a press brake's tonnage chart is published against die opening rather than against thickness alone.

One more limit belongs here. Bend a material tighter than it can stretch and the outer fibre cracks. The usable minimum radius follows from the tensile reduction of area \( r \), in per cent: \( R_{\min}/t = 50/r - 1 \). A steel with \( r=40\% \) can be bent to \( R=0.25t \); one with \( r=20\% \) needs \( R=1.5t \). It is a ductility measurement doing duty as a forming specification.

§4 · Direction, sequence, and the tolerance the drawing should ask for

Rolled sheet is not isotropic. Rolling elongates grains and stretches inclusions into stringers along the rolling direction, so the material is weaker across those stringers than along them. A bend line parallel to the rolling direction stretches the outer fibre across the stringers and cracks at a radius the same material tolerates easily when the bend line runs perpendicular. The practical rule is that \( R_{\min} \) is roughly one and a half to two times larger for a bend line along the rolling direction, and the fix is free: rotate the part on the strip. For a part with bends on two axes, one of them will be unfavourable, and the layout has to decide which bend gets the larger radius — which is why Lesson 1's nesting decision cannot be made on utilisation alone.

Two more shop realities shape the drawing. Bend sequence is constrained by tool access: a flange formed early can collide with the ram or the die on a later bend, so the order is planned backwards from the last bend, and a part that cannot be sequenced has to be redesigned rather than argued about. And a bend that stops partway along an edge tears at its ends unless a small relief notch is cut in the flat pattern first — a feature that costs nothing at the blanking stage and cannot be added afterwards.

Finally, what to write on the drawing. A brake-formed angle holds about \( \pm0.5^{\circ} \) routinely and \( \pm0.25^{\circ} \) with in-process angle measurement; a developed flat length holds about \( \pm0.25 \) mm. Those figures are the process talking, and a drawing that asks for \( \pm0.1^{\circ} \) is asking for a different process, not for more care. Metrology & Quality Control makes the same point from the measuring side — a tolerance is only meaningful against a process that can hold it and a gauge that can see it.

What this lesson assumes

  • Achievable tolerance versus a specified one (Metrology & Quality Control L5 and L8) — what a drawing may reasonably ask of a press brake.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Develop the flat blank

Problem. A U-channel is formed from 3 mm sheet with two 90° bends at an inside radius of 6 mm. Its outside dimensions are 50, 80 and 50 mm. Find the bend allowance, the bend deduction and the flat blank length. Then find the error if \( K=0.33 \) had been used instead, and give the bend allowance for the same geometry stated as \( R=0.25 \) in in 0.125 in sheet.

Solution. (1) \( R/t=6/3=2 \), which is not less than 2, so \( K=0.50 \).

(2) \( \mathrm{BA}=2\pi\frac{90}{360}(6+0.5\times3)=\frac{\pi}{2}(7.5) =11.78\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(3) Outside setback \( =(R+t)\tan45^{\circ}=(6+3)(1)=9.0 \) mm, so \( \mathrm{BD}=2(9.0)-11.78=6.22\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(4) Flat length \( =(50+80+50)-2(6.22)=180-12.44=167.56\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(5) With \( K=0.33 \): \( \mathrm{BA}=\frac{\pi}{2}(6+0.99)=10.98 \) mm, \( \mathrm{BD}=18-10.98=7.02 \) mm, flat \( =180-14.04=165.96 \) mm — 1.60 mm short.

(6) In inches: \( R/t=0.25/0.125=2 \), so \( K=0.50 \) again and \( \mathrm{BA}=\frac{\pi}{2}(0.25+0.0625)=0.4909\ \mathrm{in} \), which is 12.47 mm — the same bend, since \( 0.25 \) in is 6.35 mm rather than 6.00.

Answer: BA 11.78 mm, BD 6.22 mm, flat 167.56 mm; using \( K=0.33 \) makes the blank 1.60 mm short; the inch geometry gives BA 0.4909 in. Sanity check: the flat is shorter than the sum of the outside dimensions and longer than the sum of the inside ones, which is the only sensible place for it — the corner material has to go somewhere. Note how the 1.60 mm error hides: both bends are the right angle, both legs are the right length, and only the overall developed length is wrong, so it survives every check except measuring the finished part end to end.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Three materials, one bend

Problem. A 90° bend is formed at \( R_i/t=10 \) in three materials: mild steel (\( Y=250 \) MPa, \( E=207 \) GPa), high-strength steel (\( Y=700 \) MPa, \( E=207 \) GPa) and aluminium 6061-T6 (\( Y=275 \) MPa, \( E=69 \) GPa). Find \( Y/E \) and the springback in degrees for each, rank them, and give the overbend angle needed for the mild steel. Explain why the ranking is not the ranking by yield strength.

Solution. (1) \( Y/E \): \( 250/207000=1.21\times10^{-3} \); \( 700/207000=3.38\times10^{-3} \); \( 275/69000=3.99\times10^{-3} \).

(2) With \( x=(R_i/t)(Y/E) \) and \( R_i/R_f=4x^3-3x+1 \):

(3) Mild steel: \( x=10(1.208\times10^{-3})=0.01208 \), so \( R_i/R_f=4(1.76\times10^{-6})-0.03623+1=0.9638 \) and the bend opens by \( 90(1-0.9638)=3.26^{\circ} \).

(4) High-strength steel: \( x=0.03382 \), \( R_i/R_f=0.8987 \), springback \( 9.12^{\circ} \).

(5) Aluminium: \( x=0.03986 \), \( R_i/R_f=0.8807 \), springback \( 10.74^{\circ} \).

(6) Ranking, least to most: mild steel, high-strength steel, aluminium. By yield strength the ranking would be mild steel, aluminium, high-strength steel — aluminium and the high-strength steel swap places, because aluminium's modulus is a third of steel's and the governing group is \( Y/E \), not \( Y \).

(7) Overbend for mild steel: form to \( 90/0.9638=93.38^{\circ} \), which recovers to \( 93.38\times0.9638=90.0^{\circ} \).

Answer: \( Y/E \) = 1.21, 3.38, 3.99 \( \times10^{-3} \); springback 3.26°, 9.12° and 10.74°; overbend the mild steel to 93.38°. Sanity check: a material that yields late and springs back stiffly recovers little, and both halves of that sentence carry equal weight — which is exactly why the aluminium, with the lowest strength of the two non-mild materials, springs back the most. Note the practical consequence: a press-brake program written for mild steel and run on 6061-T6 will produce bends about 7° open, and no amount of care at the machine will fix it without changing the target angle.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Tonnage, and a question with two right answers

Problem. A 1 m long 90° bend is air-formed in 3 mm steel of \( \mathrm{TS}=340 \) MPa in a V-die opened to \( 8t \), with \( K_{bf}=1.33 \). Find the force in kilonewtons, in US tons per metre and in US tons per foot. Then find the force if the thickness doubles to 6 mm, (a) with the die opening unchanged and (b) with the die opening also scaled to \( 8t \), and say which answer a press-brake tonnage chart is built around.

Solution. (1) \( D=8t=24\ \mathrm{mm} \), so \( F=\dfrac{1.33(340)(1000)(3)^2}{24}=\dfrac{1.33(340)(9000)}{24} =169{,}575\ \mathrm{N}=169.6\ \mathrm{kN} \).

(2) \( 169{,}575/4.4482=38{,}122\ \mathrm{lbf}=19.06 \) US tons per metre, which is \( 19.06\times0.3048=5.81 \) US tons per foot.

(3) (a) Die opening fixed at 24 mm: \( F\propto t^2 \), so \( F=1.33(340)(1000)(36)/24=678{,}300\ \mathrm{N}=678.3\ \mathrm{kN} \) — four times.

(4) (b) Die opening scaled to \( 8t=48 \) mm: \( F=1.33(340)(1000)(36)/48=339{,}150\ \mathrm{N}=339.2\ \mathrm{kN} \) — twice, because \( F\propto t^2/D \) and \( D\propto t \) leaves \( F\propto t \).

(5) A tonnage chart is published against die opening precisely because of this: the answer is not determined by the material and thickness alone.

Answer: 169.6 kN, 19.06 US tons per metre, 5.81 per foot; doubling the thickness gives 678.3 kN at a fixed die opening and 339.2 kN with the opening scaled — a factor of four or of two, depending on the tooling. Sanity check: both answers are right and they differ by a factor of two, so the question as usually asked is under-specified. The physical reason is worth holding: a wider die opening is a longer beam span, and a longer span needs less force for the same bend — at the price of a larger formed radius, which then increases springback by §2's relation. Nothing in sheet forming is adjusted in isolation.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

The flat blank for a bent part is developed from

The neutral surface is the one whose length does not change, so the blank must carry its arc length: \( \mathrm{BA}=2\pi\frac{\alpha}{360}(R+Kt) \). Expressed against outside dimensions this becomes the bend deduction, which is what a draughtsman usually subtracts — 6.22 mm per bend for a 90° bend at R6 in 3 mm sheet.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

The factor \( K \) in the bend allowance is smaller for tight bends because

The outer fibre thins more than the inner fibre thickens, so the surface of unchanged length moves inward — roughly 0.33t from the inside for \( R<2t \) against 0.50t for gentler bends. Using 0.50 where 0.33 belongs, or the reverse, puts every blank out by a millimetre or two while leaving all the angles and legs correct.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

Springback is governed by

The springback relation depends on the material only through \( x=(R_i/t)(Y/E) \). A stronger material springs back more and a stiffer one springs back less, with equal weight — which is why 6061-T6 aluminium, at 275 MPa, springs back further than a 700 MPa steel: its modulus is a third as large.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

At \( R/t = 10 \), a 90° bend in mild steel springs back about 3.3°. The same bend in aluminium 6061-T6 springs back about

\( Y/E \) is \( 3.99\times10^{-3} \) against mild steel's \( 1.21\times10^{-3} \), roughly 3.3 times, and the springback follows: 10.74°. A press-brake program written for mild steel and run on 6061-T6 leaves every bend about 7° open, and care at the machine cannot recover it.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

Springback increases with \( R/t \). The practical consequence is that

\( x=(R_i/t)(Y/E) \), so a wide air bend at \( R/t=20 \) recovers 6.5° in mild steel where a tight bend at \( R/t=2 \) barely moves. It is the main reason bottoming and coining are used where angle accuracy matters, despite costing several times the force.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

V-die bending force is \( F=K_{bf}\mathrm{TS}\,wt^{2}/D \). If the sheet thickness doubles and the die opening is scaled to \( 8t \) as usual, the force

\( F\propto t^2/D \), and scaling \( D\propto t \) leaves \( F\propto t \), so 169.6 kN becomes 339.2 kN. Only if the die opening is left unchanged does the force quadruple to 678.3 kN. Both answers are correct and the question is incomplete without the tooling — which is why tonnage charts are published against die opening.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

A steel with a tensile reduction of area of 20% has a minimum bend radius of about

\( R_{\min}/t=50/r-1=50/20-1=1.5 \). It is a ductility measurement from the tensile test doing duty as a forming specification. A more ductile steel at \( r=40\% \) manages \( 0.25t \), and the same material bent along the rolling direction needs one and a half to two times more.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

A bend line running parallel to the rolling direction

Rolling stretches inclusions into stringers along the rolling direction, and bending parallel to them pulls the outer fibre across them, so cracking starts at a radius the same sheet tolerates easily the other way round. The fix costs nothing — rotate the part on the strip — which is why the layout of Lesson 1 cannot be settled on utilisation alone.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the panel run that changed material and not the program. A fabricator formed roof and wall panels on a press brake to a program developed and proven on mild steel. A contract specified the same profiles in a high-strength grade, at the same thickness and the same nominal dimensions, and the program was reused unchanged on the reasonable-sounding grounds that the geometry had not moved. The first panels came off with every bend several degrees open, the profiles would not interlock, and the batch was scrapped.

Nothing had gone wrong at the machine. Springback depends on the material only through \( Y/E \), and the new grade had the same modulus as mild steel with nearly three times the yield, so at the profile's \( R/t \) the bends recovered roughly three times as far — about 9° instead of 3°. The program's overbend was compensating for the old material's recovery, and against the new one it was simply the wrong number. Worse, the substitution was invisible on the drawing: the geometry, thickness and tooling were all unchanged, and the only thing that moved was a material property nobody had been asked to check.

Two practices follow. Treat a material change as a tooling change: the overbend is part of the process specification, not a property of the part, and it must be re-derived from \( Y/E \) and re-proven on a sample before a run. And where a shop forms several grades on one tool, prefer a strategy that does not depend on knowing the material — bottoming, or an adaptive brake that measures the angle mid-stroke — because coil-to-coil variation in yield within a single grade is enough to move a bend by a fraction of a degree even when nothing has been substituted.
