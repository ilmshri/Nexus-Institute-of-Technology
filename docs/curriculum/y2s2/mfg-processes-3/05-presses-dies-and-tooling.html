Presses, dies, and tooling

Mechanical and hydraulic presses; die sets and progressive dies. Tonnage curves and where presses are overloaded. Die maintenance as a production-uptime discipline.

Lecture

After this lesson you can answer:
  • Mechanical versus hydraulic press: where does each win?
  • Why is die maintenance a production KPI?

§1 · Two ways to push, and why the choice is not about size

A mechanical press stores energy in a flywheel and releases it through a clutch to a crank or eccentric that drives the slide. The slide's position is a fixed function of crank angle, so the stroke length, the velocity profile and the timing are all built into the machine and cannot be changed. A hydraulic press drives the slide with a cylinder, so force, speed, stroke length and dwell are all set by the control valve.

The distinction that matters is not force or size but where in the stroke the force is available. A crank converts torque to force with a mechanical advantage that depends on crank angle.

Key relation — slide force from crank torque

\[ F=\frac{T}{R\sin\theta},\qquad \cos\theta=1-\frac{s}{R} \]

\( R \) is the crank radius, \( \theta \) the angle from bottom dead centre and \( s \) the distance the slide is above BDC. As \( \theta\to0 \) the available force diverges, so a press's rating has to be quoted at a stated distance above BDC.

Take a 100-tonne press with a 100 mm stroke, so \( R=50 \) mm, rated at 6 mm above bottom dead centre. At that point \( \sin\theta=0.475 \). Twelve millimetres up, \( \sin\theta=0.650 \) and the available force is \( 0.475/0.650=73\% \) of the rating. At 25 mm it is 55%. At mid-stroke it is 47%.

So the answer to where each press wins is arithmetic rather than preference. Blanking and piercing finish at the bottom of the stroke and need force only there, so a mechanical press gives its whole rating and its 60–150 strokes a minute. Deep drawing needs force from the moment the punch touches the blank and holds it for the length of the draw, which is high in the stroke — so that 100-tonne press is a 55-tonne press for the job. The drawing operation of Lesson 3 needs 347 kN at that height, which 538 kN covers, but by a factor of 1.55 rather than the 2.8 the nameplate suggests, and the blankholder load arrives higher still where less is available. A hydraulic press delivers its rating anywhere, at any speed, with a dwell if the process wants one; it simply cannot match a mechanical press's cycle rate.

§2 · The die set and the progressive die

A die set is the frame that holds punch and die in register: two shoes joined by guide pillars and bushes, with the punch holder above and the die block below. Its specification follows directly from Lesson 1's clearance. If the guiding lets the punch wander by \( e \), the clearance becomes \( c-e \) on one side and \( c+e \) on the other, and the burr goes with it.

For 3 mm steel, \( c=0.18 \) mm. Holding both sides within \( \pm20\% \) of nominal needs \( e\le0.036 \) mm — even then the worst case is a side-to-side ratio of \( 0.216/0.144=1.5 \). That single number justifies the whole cost of a guided die set, and it is why a die run in a press with worn gibs produces a burr on one side and not the other.

A progressive die strings several stations along one strip: the strip advances by a fixed pitch each stroke, each station performs one operation, and a finished part falls out on every stroke once the die is full. An eight-station die at 100 strokes a minute delivers its first part after eight strokes — 4.8 seconds — and 100 parts a minute thereafter. The strip is the conveyor, which is the source of both its speed and its cost: the carrier web has to survive being fed, located and stripped at every station, so a progressive layout uses noticeably more material than the single-station nesting of Lesson 1.

A transfer die moves parts between stations mechanically instead, with no carrier, which recovers that material and buys the freedom to make parts a strip could not carry — at the cost of a transfer mechanism and its timing.

§3 · The three ways presses are broken

Overloading away from bottom dead centre is the first, and it is the tonnage curve being ignored. The press's gauge and its nameplate both say 100 tonnes; the crankshaft does not care what the nameplate says, and it fails in torsion. Any operation working high in the stroke has to be checked against the curve rather than the rating.

Off-centre loading is the second. If the resultant load does not act through the slide's centre, the slide tips, and the gibs and bearings carry the moment. Presses are rated for a maximum off-centre distance, and two dies of unequal tonnage sharing one bolster is the classic way to exceed it. The fix is layout: balance the tonnage about the centre, or accept a lower total.

Snap-through is the third and the least expected. Under load the press frame stretches elastically and stores energy; at the instant the blank fractures, the load vanishes and that energy is released in milliseconds.

Key relation — stored energy released at fracture

\[ \delta=\frac{F}{k},\qquad E=\tfrac12 k\delta^{2}=\frac{F^{2}}{2k} \]

A frame of stiffness 2,000 kN/mm carrying 1,000 kN stretches 0.5 mm and stores 250 J, all of which is dumped into the machine when the cut breaks through. The resulting reverse tonnage runs at 10–15% of the forward load and cracks frames and tie rods.

Everything that softens the breakthrough softens this: the angular shear of Lesson 1, staggered punch lengths so stations do not all break through together, and slower speeds on thick or hard material. It is the same physics as a bolt cutter kicking when the wire parts, at a scale that breaks castings.

§4 · Die maintenance is a scheduling problem, not a virtue

Punch and die edges wear round. A rounded edge behaves exactly like excessive clearance — heavier rollover, a bigger burr, more force — so Lesson 1's edge zones are the wear gauge, and burr height is the maintenance signal. Sharpening grinds the punch face back by two or three tenths of a millimetre and restores it; a punch with 12 mm of grindable length at 0.3 mm a sharpen has 40 sharpens in it, so at 200,000 hits between them its life is eight million hits.

Choosing the interval is the same problem Metrology & Quality Control solved for calibration. Sharpening costs a planned stoppage. Running too long costs an unplanned one, which is far worse: stripping, repairing and re-setting a crashed die takes hours where a sharpening takes three-quarters of one, and it scraps the parts in the die and sometimes damages the press. Measure both in the currency the line understands — parts of lost output — and the maintenance event costs about 4,500 while a crash costs about 24,000, a ratio of 5.3.

Key relation — the sharpening interval

\[ \text{cost per hit}=\frac{C_m}{N}+\frac{\lambda N C_c}{2}, \qquad N^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{2C_m}{\lambda C_c}} \]

\( N \) is the interval in hits, \( C_m \) the cost of a planned maintenance event, \( C_c \) the cost of a crash and \( \lambda \) the hazard rate, which grows with hits since the last sharpening.

With \( \lambda=10^{-11} \) per hit squared, \( N^{*}=194{,}000 \) hits — which is where shop practice sits, and the arithmetic recovers the rule of thumb rather than contradicting it. At the optimum the two cost terms are equal, exactly as they were for the calibration interval, and that equality is the fastest way to audit an existing schedule: if planned stoppages dominate the cost, the interval is too short, and if crashes do, it is too long.

Which is the answer to why die maintenance is a production indicator rather than a housekeeping one. It is not that maintained dies are tidier. It is that the interval is a decision with a computable cost, the wrong interval is expensive in both directions, and the evidence needed to set it — hits since sharpening, burr height, crash history — is data a shop either records or does not.

Foundations

What this lesson assumes

  • Clearance, burr and cutting force (Lesson 1) — the die set exists to hold that clearance, and burr height is the wear signal.
  • Angular shear (Lesson 1) — the same trick that lowers peak force also softens snap-through.
  • Drawing and blankholder force (Lesson 3) — the operation that needs force high in the stroke.
  • Crank-slider kinematics (Kinematics & Dynamics of Machinery L2–L3) — \( \cos\theta=1-s/R \) and the slide's velocity profile.
  • Torsion and elastic strain energy (Strength of Materials L5 and L9) — the crankshaft's failure mode and the frame energy released at snap-through.
  • Setting an interval from two costs (Metrology & Quality Control L11) — the identical optimisation, here for sharpening rather than calibration.

Problem 1 of 11 · solve, then check

Read the tonnage curve before choosing the press

Problem. A 100-tonne mechanical press has a 100 mm stroke and is rated 6 mm above bottom dead centre. Find the available force at 12 mm and at 25 mm above BDC, in tonnes and kilonewtons. The drawing operation of Lesson 3 needs 347 kN and must have it from 25 mm above BDC. Say whether this press can do the job, and what would.

Solution. (1) The crank radius is half the stroke, \( R=50 \) mm, and \( \cos\theta=1-s/R \), so \( \sin\theta=\sqrt{2s/R-(s/R)^2} \).

(2) At the rating point, \( s=6 \): \( \sin\theta=\sqrt{0.24-0.0144}=0.4750 \).

(3) At \( s=12 \): \( \sin\theta=\sqrt{0.48-0.0576}=0.6499 \), so the available fraction is \( 0.4750/0.6499=0.731 \) — 73.1 tonnes, or 717 kN.

(4) At \( s=25 \): \( \sin\theta=\sqrt{1.0-0.25}=0.8660 \), giving \( 0.4750/0.8660=0.548 \) — 54.8 tonnes, or 538 kN.

(5) The draw needs 347 kN at 25 mm, and 538 kN is available, so the press can supply the force — but with only a 1.55 factor over the requirement, and the machine's gauge and nameplate both suggest 981 kN, which is 2.8 times the truth at that height.

(6) The right machine is hydraulic: full rating anywhere in the stroke, adjustable ram speed, and a dwell at the bottom if the process wants one. A mechanical press would be the right answer for the blanking that produced the disc, which finishes at the bottom of the stroke.

Answer: 73.1 t (717 kN) at 12 mm and 54.8 t (538 kN) at 25 mm; the press can just do the 347 kN draw, at 55% of its nameplate, and a hydraulic press is the correct choice for drawing. Sanity check: the curve is steepest exactly where operations that matter live — between 6 and 25 mm the available force nearly halves — so a press chosen on its nameplate for a drawing job is routinely a press chosen at twice its real capacity. Note that the operation, not the part, decides: the same press is fully rated for blanking the blank and half-rated for drawing it.

Problem 2 of 11 · solve, then check

Guiding, and the energy that arrives after the cut

Problem. (a) A die blanks 3 mm steel at a clearance of 0.18 mm per side. Find the maximum punch-to-die wander the die set may allow if both sides are to stay within \( \pm20\% \) of nominal clearance, and the worst-case ratio between the two sides. (b) The same press has a frame stiffness of 2,000 kN/mm and the operation peaks at 1,000 kN. Find the frame's elastic stretch, the energy stored in it, and say what happens to that energy at breakthrough and what reduces it.

Solution. (1) Wander \( e \) makes the clearance \( c-e \) on one side and \( c+e \) on the other, so \( \pm20\% \) requires \( e\le0.20(0.18)=0.036\ \mathrm{mm} \).

(2) Even at that limit the sides are \( 0.144 \) and \( 0.216 \) mm, a ratio of \( 0.216/0.144=1.5 \) — so one side of every part carries a visibly heavier burr than the other.

(3) \( \delta=F/k=1000/2000=0.5\ \mathrm{mm} \) of elastic stretch in the frame.

(4) \( E=\tfrac12k\delta^{2}=\tfrac12(2000)(0.25)=250\ \mathrm{kN\,mm}=250\ \mathrm{J} \).

(5) At the instant the blank fractures the load vanishes and the frame recoils, releasing all 250 J in milliseconds. The resulting reverse tonnage is typically 10–15% of the forward load and is what cracks frames and breaks tie rods.

(6) Anything that makes the breakthrough gradual reduces it: angular shear on the tool, staggered punch lengths so stations do not break through together, and lower press speeds on thick or hard material.

Answer: \( e\le0.036 \) mm with a worst-case 1.5:1 side-to-side clearance ratio; 0.5 mm of frame stretch storing 250 J, released as reverse tonnage at breakthrough and softened by angular shear, staggered punches and slower speeds. Sanity check: both halves of this problem are the same idea — a press and die are elastic structures, and their deflections are of the same order as the clearances they are supposed to hold. Note that 0.036 mm is tighter than most people's mental image of a press tool, and that \( E=F^2/2k \) means a stiffer frame stores less energy at the same load, which is why heavy frames both deflect less and snap through less violently.

Problem 3 of 11 · solve, then check

Set the sharpening interval

Problem. A planned sharpening costs about 4,500 parts of lost output; an unplanned die crash costs about 24,000. The hazard of a crash grows with hits since the last sharpening, at \( \lambda=10^{-11} \) per hit squared. Find the cost per hit as a function of the interval, the optimum interval, the cost there, and the punch's total life if it has 12 mm of grindable length at 0.3 mm a sharpen. Comment on how to audit an existing interval without redoing the calculation.

Solution. (1) One maintenance event per \( N \) hits costs \( C_m/N \) per hit.

(2) If the hazard rises linearly with hits since sharpening, the expected crashes over an interval are \( \int_0^N\lambda x\,dx=\lambda N^2/2 \), which is \( \lambda N/2 \) per hit, costing \( \lambda NC_c/2 \).

(3) Total per hit: \( K(N)=C_m/N+\lambda NC_c/2 \).

(4) \( dK/dN=-C_m/N^2+\lambda C_c/2=0 \) gives \( N^{*}=\sqrt{2C_m/(\lambda C_c)}=\sqrt{9000/(2.4\times10^{-7})} =\sqrt{3.75\times10^{10}} \), so \( N^{*}=194{,}000 \) hits.

(5) \( K(N^{*})=4500/193{,}649+10^{-11}(193{,}649)(24{,}000)/2=0.0232+0.0232 =0.0465 \) parts of lost output per hit.

(6) Punch life: \( 12/0.3=40 \) sharpens, so \( 40\times194{,}000\approx7.7 \) million hits — about eight million at the round 200,000 interval shops use.

(7) To audit an interval without recomputing: at the optimum the two terms are equal. If planned stoppages dominate the maintenance cost, the interval is too short; if crashes dominate, it is too long.

Answer: \( K(N)=C_m/N+\lambda NC_c/2 \), minimised at \( N^{*}=194{,}000 \) hits costing 0.0465 parts per hit, with the two terms equal; the punch lasts 40 sharpens, roughly eight million hits. Sanity check: the arithmetic lands on the 200,000-hit interval shops already use, which is the right kind of agreement — the model recovers accumulated practice rather than overturning it, and its value is that it says why, and what to change when the costs change. Note that the whole structure is Metrology & Quality Control's calibration-interval problem with different labels: a planned cost that falls with the interval, an unplanned one that rises, and equality at the optimum.

Problem 4 of 11 · multiple choice

A mechanical press's rating must be quoted with

\( F=T/(R\sin\theta) \) diverges as the crank approaches bottom dead centre, so the available force depends entirely on where in the stroke it is measured. A 100-tonne press rated at 6 mm above BDC gives 73 tonnes at 12 mm and 55 at 25. A rating without its measuring point is not a specification.

Problem 5 of 11 · multiple choice

A 100-tonne mechanical press with a 100 mm stroke, rated 6 mm above BDC, can deliver about how much at 25 mm above BDC?

\( \sin\theta \) is 0.475 at the rating point and 0.866 at 25 mm, so the available fraction is \( 0.475/0.866=0.548 \) — 54.8 tonnes. The nameplate and the gauge both still say 100, which is why an operation working high in the stroke is the classic way to break a crankshaft.

Problem 6 of 11 · multiple choice

For deep drawing rather than blanking, the better press is usually hydraulic because

A draw needs force from the moment the punch touches and holds it through the stroke, which is precisely where a crank cannot deliver. Hydraulics have no such curve and add adjustable ram speed and a dwell. The trade is cycle rate: 10–30 strokes a minute against a mechanical press's 60–150, which is why blanking stays mechanical.

Problem 7 of 11 · multiple choice

A die set's guide pillars exist mainly to

Any wander \( e \) makes the clearance \( c-e \) on one side and \( c+e \) on the other, so at \( c=0.18 \) mm even a 0.036 mm error gives a 1.5:1 side-to-side ratio and a burr that differs around the part. The guiding requirement is a fraction of a clearance that is itself a fraction of a millimetre.

Problem 8 of 11 · multiple choice

An eight-station progressive die running at 100 strokes a minute produces

Each stroke advances the strip one pitch and every station works at once, so after the initial eight strokes — 4.8 seconds — a finished part leaves on every stroke. The strip is the conveyor, which is where the speed comes from and also why a progressive layout uses more material than the single-station nesting of Lesson 1.

Problem 9 of 11 · multiple choice

Snap-through, or reverse tonnage, is caused by

The frame stretches under load and stores \( E=F^2/2k \) — 250 J for 1,000 kN on a 2,000 kN/mm frame — which is dumped back in milliseconds when the load vanishes. The reverse load runs at 10–15% of the forward one and cracks frames and tie rods. Angular shear, staggered punch lengths and lower speeds all soften it.

Problem 10 of 11 · multiple choice

The practical signal that a punch needs sharpening is

A worn edge behaves like excessive clearance, so the burr grows — which makes Lesson 1's edge zones the wear gauge, readable on the parts without stopping the press. Stroke count sets the planned interval; burr height is what confirms or corrects it.

Problem 11 of 11 · multiple choice

At the optimum sharpening interval, \( K(N)=C_m/N+\lambda NC_c/2 \) has

Setting \( dK/dN=0 \) gives \( C_m/N=\lambda NC_c/2 \). It is the fastest audit of an existing schedule: if planned stoppages dominate the cost, the interval is too short; if crashes dominate, it is too long. The same structure sets a calibration interval in Metrology & Quality Control.

Applied case — factory floor (Kuwait)

Applied case — the press that was chosen from its nameplate. A shop moved a drawing operation onto a 100-tonne mechanical press because the calculation said the job needed 347 kN — 35 tonnes — and the press had three times that. The first shift produced sound parts. Within a month the press was making an intermittent knock at the bottom of the stroke, and the crankshaft was found cracked at a journal fillet.

The force calculation was right and the press selection was not. The draw needs its force from the moment the punch touches the blank, about 25 mm above bottom dead centre, and at that height the crank's mechanical advantage gives \( \sin\theta=0.866 \) against the 0.475 at the 6 mm rating point: \( 0.475/0.866=55\% \) of the nameplate, or 538 kN. The job at 347 kN was inside that, but only by a factor of 1.55 rather than the 2.8 the nameplate implied — and the blankholder, whose force Lesson 3 put at 146 kN, arrives earlier still, higher in the stroke, where the available force is lower again. The machine was being worked near its real limit every stroke, and the failure was in torsion at the crankshaft, which is where a mechanical press fails when the load is applied where the crank has no advantage.

Two practices follow. Size a mechanical press against its tonnage curve at the height where the operation actually needs the force, never against the nameplate — the two differ by a factor of two over the range where real operations live. And treat operation type as a machine-selection criterion in its own right: blanking finishes at bottom dead centre and belongs on a mechanical press, while drawing needs force throughout the stroke and belongs on a hydraulic one, whatever the force numbers alone suggest.
